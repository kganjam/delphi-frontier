# 16. Engineering a coral: the design space

Generated by `scripts/designspace_doc.py` from the same files the live page reads, so the two cannot drift.
The interactive version, with the figures and the filterable route list, is at <https://studio.morphoswarm.ai/#/studio/coral>.

**The question.** Could you engineer a coral? The question hides four different animals: one that resists bleaching, one of a chosen shape, one that settles and survives where you put it, and one engineered through its symbiont or its microbiome rather than through the animal. They are reached at different scales and they are not interchangeable. This page lays out every route we can source and runs the models that bear on three of them.

## Yes, four different ways — and the weather beats all of them.

Ask for a thermally tolerant coral and you can have one, on paper, by four routes: raise the symbiont's thermal optimum, raise photoprotection, raise tolerance of reactive oxygen, or make the host hold its symbionts longer. Read on the obvious measure — how much heat it takes to lose half the symbionts — all four look comparable. Read on whether the host's carbon balance stays positive and they separate sharply: the thermal optimum moves that threshold about six times further than the other three. Photoprotection, ROS tolerance and retention mostly keep the algae in place without keeping the animal fed. Three of the four buy a coral that still looks brown while it starves, which matters because visual bleaching score is what most selection programmes actually measure.

That model is a line-by-line port of the published Cunning–Muller–Gates–Nisbet model, checked against the authors' own R code to a relative error of 5 × 10⁻¹⁵. It is worth saying what the port revealed: the published model contains no temperature variable at all. Bleaching there is driven by light — excess excitation beyond what photochemistry and non-photochemical quenching can absorb. The thermal coupling on this page is ours, it enters through one parameter, and every thermal number here is a property of that extension rather than of the published model. The first attempt to encode the measured tolerance trade-off came out with the wrong sign, because lowering the quantum yield makes each unit of fixed carbon consume more photons and so reduces the excess; the corrected encoding and the failed one are both in the file.

Ask instead for more corals on the reef and the arithmetic changes the subject. Over twenty thousand samples of a nine-stage chain, the number of recruits surviving a year spans four orders of magnitude — and about four fifths of that spread comes from stages with no published wild value, led by whether a larva encounters suitable substrate at all. So the highest-value next experiment in this entire chain is a measurement, not an intervention trial. Among interventions, two are in a different class from the rest per unit of effort — screening sites for sediment, and mass larval enrichment — and which of those two is first is a coin flip once the cost assumptions are themselves perturbed.

And then the number that frames all of it. Recruit survivorship has been measured at 33% in a normal year and 2% in a heatwave year at the same site. The best intervention in our table is a 22-fold median multiplier on a quantity whose own spread is four orders of magnitude, and the model has no thermal year in it. No intervention tested here moves recruitment as much as the difference between a normal year and a bad one.

The shape question is the one we could not answer. The growth model spans compact mound to open lobed mound and reaches neither true branching nor plating at any setting tried, including four beyond the declared sweep. Light weighting makes colonies smoother rather than flatter. The one metric comparable with real colonies agrees: an area-to-diameter exponent of 1.72 to 1.95 sits inside the measured range for massive and tabular corals and nowhere near the 2.4 to 2.5 of branching ones. That is reported as a target declared and not reached, not as a narrower success.

> Much of this evidence has to travel to reach the animal a route targets. 22 of 42 organism-level routes rest on cross-genus transfer or weaker, and 7 rest on something that is not a reef-building coral at all — five on an anemone or Nematostella, two on a non-reef stony coral. One route carries three different transfer levels within itself, with its mechanism coming from an anemone. Every route states its own, so a reader can see how far the inference has been carried.

## If you asked us to actually do it

**The route.** Put it where it will still be alive in 2050 (`eco-site-prioritisation`)

**Best engineering route.**

sym-shuffle-by-induced-bleaching

**Why two fields.**

Because the honest answer to 'what should we do' and the honest answer to 'what should we BUILD' are different routes, and collapsing them into one field would misrepresent both. The first is siting, which is cheap, reversible and boring. The second is symbiont shuffling. The two sections below say so in that order.

**If you only get to do one thing.**

Pick the reef, not the coral. Mula et al. 2025 found that restoration sites cluster near human settlements, that outcomes are not predicted by environmental covariates, and that most restored localities face high or severe bleaching risk by mid-century with more than half already affected. Nothing else on this page changes the answer by as much, costs as little, or is as reversible. It is also the least interesting thing to publish, which is probably why the siting data look the way they do.

**If you have to engineer something.**

Symbiont shuffling by controlled sub-lethal bleaching, in colonies already carrying a background heat-tolerant type. It is the only route on this page with a causal field-scale coral phenotype attached to a number of degrees (about +1 to 1.5 degC, Berkelmans & van Oppen 2006), it needs no genetic modification and therefore probably no GMO regulator, it requires nothing to be cultured or released, and it is partly reversible. It is chosen over Durusdinium INOCULATION, which gets the same tolerance, because inoculation with a non-native lineage is irreversible in open water and the Caribbean shows what that looks like: a clonal invader associated with calcification reduced by nearly half.

The reasons not to do it are stated plainly. You are deliberately bleaching a living colony; growth falls by more than half for up to 18 months afterwards. You need a pre-existing reservoir of the tolerant type or there is nothing to shuffle to. Durability is verified for THREE MONTHS and no longer. And the direction is conditional: after SEVERE bleaching in a warm recovery environment the tolerant proportion rises sharply, but after MILD bleaching it tends to DECREASE — so an intervention that is too gentle does the opposite of what you wanted.

**The single most useful experiment nobody has run.**

Cross a symbiont intervention with a host trait in one factorial design and measure both the thermal threshold AND the growth rate at ambient. Every thermal route on this page has been tested alone. No study read here crosses them, so whether two interventions add, interact or cancel is unknown in corals — our own simulation says additive, and that is a statement about a four-parameter box in one model, not about a coral.

The second most useful: a multi-year time course of a shuffled symbiont community. The three-month persistence result is the longest verified horizon for shuffling anywhere in the sources read, and a route whose durability is unknown past one season is being discussed as though it were a decade.

**What we would not do.**

Engineer the animal, for now. CRISPR works in Acropora millepora — that is real and recent. Counted from the sources read here, SIX genes have been targeted in a reef-building coral: FGF1a, GFP and RFP (Cleves et al. 2018, chosen to show the method works), HSF1 (Cleves et al. 2020), SLC4-gamma (Tinoco et al. 2023) and SLC26A11 (Maruyama et al. 2026, reported in Aiptasia and in a reef-building coral). Only the last three carry an informative trait phenotype, and every one of the six is in the INJECTED GENERATION. There is no stable germline coral line anywhere in this literature, Acropora takes years to reach reproductive size, spawning is seasonal, and editing adult colonies has not been shown. The two coral gene edits with heat or skeleton phenotypes are both LOSS of function: knocking out HSF1 makes a coral worse, and knocking out SLC4-gamma breaks its skeleton. Neither tells you how to make one better. The animal-engineering route is where the science is most exciting and the design leverage is currently lowest.

We would also not deploy a transgenic symbiont. Not on principle — because no one has made one. Standard transformation methods failed on the one strain where the attempt was published, and the PubMed index returns zero for Symbiodiniaceae CRISPR. And unlike an edited coral, an engineered alga is a self-replicating microorganism in open water: the one route here whose irreversibility is categorical rather than practical.

**The thing the question gets wrong.**

'Could you engineer a coral' treats thermal tolerance as the specification. The literature says the binding constraint is somewhere else. Adult corals are not generally what fails first in restoration — recruitment is. First-year survival of settlers spreads over more than tenfold across sites, devices and thermal years (9.6% on tetrapods in Curacao; 2 to 39% across device types in one Acropora tenuis trial; 33% against 2% between a normal and a heatwave year in Moorea; effectively zero yield at 21 months at one Lizard Island site). In the best-documented larval seeding experiment, 47 colonies survived from roughly 900,000 larvae. A heat-tolerant coral that cannot recruit is a heat-tolerant coral you have to keep planting by hand.

**The arithmetic nobody puts on the slide.**

The median coral restoration project covers 100 square metres (Bostrom-Einarsson et al. 2020, from a review of the field). The Great Barrier Reef Marine Park is about 344,400 square kilometres. Covering even one square kilometre at the median project size takes ten thousand projects. Reef habitat area is far smaller than Marine Park area, so that ratio overstates the gap — but not by the four orders of magnitude it would need to close. Reported restoration costs run about USD 80,000 per hectare at the median in 2010 dollars, with the true median likely two to four times higher and coral reefs among the most expensive habitats to restore. Every route on this page should be read against that denominator.

**What would change our mind.**

A verified multi-year field time course showing a shuffled or inoculated symbiont community holding, with growth measured and not just survival, would move the symbiont route from 'buys a season' to 'buys a decade'. A stable germline coral line would move the gene scale from a calibration tool to a design surface. And a single cross-factorial experiment showing that two interventions combine super-additively would change the recommendation from 'pick one' to 'stack them'.

## The design space: 45 routes

Every row is a published perturbation or a bounded proposal, with its own evidence level. **None of them has been attempted here.** Sources carry read levels: a listing is not a reading.

### Unplaced — 45

These routes name a scale the page does not declare (cell, colony, ecological, gene, larval, microbiome, molecular, population, symbiont). They are listed rather than dropped.

- **Make the coral enhancer map that does not exist yet** (molecular): No coral. The product is the first map of which non-coding coral DNA is open and active, which is the thing every 'tune the regulation of gene X' proposal below silently assumes already exists.
- **Reprogramme gene-body methylation to harden a colony** (molecular): Unpredictable, and the two papers read here point opposite ways. This route is in the table because it is widely proposed, not because anyone has shown it does anything.
- **Knock out HSF1 — the calibration experiment, not a design** (gene): Larvae that live normally at 27 degC and die rapidly at 34 degC, where wild-type and Cas9-only larvae showed no detectable mortality. This marks the floor of coral thermal tolerance. It does NOT give you a heat-tolerant coral and was never meant to.
- **Raise the baseline instead: engineer constitutive frontloading** (gene): A coral that starts hot-ready, like the resilient Acropora hyacinthus colonies in which 60 such genes are already elevated at control temperature. The cost of carrying that expression constitutively is UNMEASURED, and is the obvious place a trade-off would hide.
- **Edit SLC26A11 and lose the symbiosis** (gene): Reduced symbiosis. Reported in Aiptasia and, per the same abstract, in a reef-building coral. This is a route to UNDERSTANDING how the symbiosome is supplied, not a route to a better coral: its phenotype is the thing everything else is trying to protect.
- **Get knockdown and knock-in working, in the coral that is easy first** (gene): No phenotype of interest. The product is the ability to turn a coral gene DOWN and to put a sequence IN, neither of which has been published in a reef-building coral.
- **Break the bicarbonate supply and watch the skeleton fail** (cell): Juvenile polyps with a defective skeleton, with defect severity tracking the mutation frequency within each animal. The cleanest genotype-to-skeleton link in the coral literature.
- **The rest of the calcification machinery, where nothing has been tested genetically** (cell): Expected: reduced calcification. UNTESTED GENETICALLY in any coral. Every existing result on this enzyme is localisation, expression or in-vitro inhibitor chemistry, and inhibitors have off-target effects.
- **Measure the calcifying fluid, so a skeleton phenotype can be diagnosed** (cell): A readout, not a coral. It separates 'the pH pump failed' from 'the organic matrix failed', which no amount of looking at a deformed skeleton can do.
- **Give up the skeleton — the route that works and is still a failure** (cell): Polyps that live, feed and reproduce with no skeleton at all, and rebuild one when returned to normal conditions. And no reef: no structure, no refuge, no habitat, nothing that makes a coral reef worth having. Included because it draws the line between keeping the animal alive and keeping the ecosystem.
- **Give it Durusdinium trenchii: about +1 to 2 degC, and a visibly smaller coral** (symbiont): A colony that bleaches at roughly 1 to 2 degC higher — and grows 29% slower in the laboratory and 38% slower in the field, with Caribbean hosts calcifying at 'nearly half' the rate of those holding native symbionts. Juveniles are worse: clade C grows two to three times as fast as D. You get a coral that survives the heatwave and is small when it is over.
- **Bleach it on purpose so the heat-tolerant symbiont it already has takes over** (symbiont): Colonies that come back more than 90% D1a — a type not detectable before — with less photodamage and less symbiont loss when re-heated three months later. Whether it lasts beyond three months is NOT KNOWN from anything verified here.
- **Inoculate SS8, the one lab-evolved strain that worked in an adult coral** (symbiont): In Galaxea fascicularis: thermotolerance similar to Durusdinium-hosting fragments of the same colony, but FASTER growth than those fragments at ambient, with the strain still present in moderate abundance two years later. And: only two of four coral genotypes took it up at all. Host-genotype compatibility is the gate.
- **Screen evolved strains IN THE HOST, because in-vitro tolerance does not transfer** (symbiont): Most of what you evolved will not work. All ten strains expanded their thermal tolerance in vitro; three of ten improved the holobiont. One strain killed its larvae. The headline '3 of 10' is itself soft: the full text shows those three differ significantly from one wild-type strain and show only a trend against the other.
- **Choose the symbiont per host species, because there is no universal best type** (symbiont): The right partner for that host — which in Acropora tenuis juveniles is type C1, not D, the opposite of the Acropora millepora answer. Avoids paying Durusdinium's growth cost in hosts where Durusdinium is not even the more tolerant partner.
- **Genetically engineer the alga — the route everyone proposes and nobody has** (symbiont): Not demonstrated. The honest prediction is a culture that does not take up the construct. Standard methods — biolistics, electroporation, silicon carbide whiskers, nuclear and chloroplast targets — failed to confer resistance in the one published attempt read here.
- **Feed the coral to offset what the tolerant symbiont stops giving it** (symbiont): Intended: near-normal growth at +1 to 2 degC of tolerance. NOT DEMONSTRATED in any coral. The strongest support is indirect: the published bioenergetic model's own abstract states that bleaching in it is 'exacerbated by nutrients and attenuated by feeding'.
- **Restraint: do not release Durusdinium trenchii where it is not native** (symbiont): Reefs that do not acquire a low-diversity clonal invader whose spread is associated with calcification reduced by nearly half. The counterfactual is the Caribbean.
- **Dose a defined bacterial consortium through a heatwave** (microbiome): In a tank: 100% of fragments survived a 30 degC, 10-day heat treatment against 60% of placebo-treated controls, with stable photosynthetic performance. And the effect stops when dosing stops — the consortium was not detectable at all after the 15-day recovery period.
- **Use dead bacteria — it worked in the water, and releases nothing alive** (microbiome): Maintained photosynthetic efficiency through a 15-day marine heatwave in Acropora cf. valida, where placebo-treated colonies declined significantly. The ecological-release objection to live probiotics does not apply. Fv/Fm, not survival, is what the abstract reports.
- **Transplant the whole microbiome from a heat-tolerant donor** (microbiome): Less bleaching at 34 degC, with 112 (Pocillopora) and 16 (Porites) donor-specific bacterial species detectable in recipients. The cargo is uncontrolled: a tissue homogenate also carries host cells, Symbiodiniaceae and viruses, so the effect cannot be assigned to bacteria.
- **Amoxicillin paste on disease lesions — the one that is actually deployed** (microbiome): Lesions arrested on 70% to 95% of treated lesions in the water, across five species, where the paste alone achieved 4% and 9% — no better than untreated. And the colony keeps developing NEW lesions elsewhere, so this treats lesions, not corals.
- **Phage, given before or within a day of infection — and useless after two** (microbiome): Corals that escape white-plague-like infection and do not transmit it, with phage persisting 37 days at about 10^4 per square centimetre of coral surface. Given two days after infection it failed. The therapeutic window is the result.
- **Change the water, not the genome: grow it in the flow you want the shape for** (colony): Thin, widely spaced, more symmetric branches in slow flow; more compact, thicker-branched, upstream-biased colonies in faster flow. No genetic change, and the skeleton already laid down does not move — only new growth re-steers.
- **Shade it into a plate** (colony): Flatter, laminar or foliose plates instead of digitate or corymbose mounds, with lower surface complexity. The strongest direct evidence is a one-year clonal experiment in ONE genus and it is a PREPRINT, not peer-reviewed.
- **Pick a shape that does not snap: massive and encrusting over tabular** (colony): Lower bending stress at the base and lower dislodgement rates, with a flatter size-mortality curve. Species that become more vulnerable as they grow have bathtub-shaped mortality; mechanically stable ones get safer with size. The bleaching benefit of the same choice is CONTESTED.
- **Pick the opposite shape, because fish live in branches** (colony): More three-dimensional surface per unit of seabed footprint — measured area exponents of about 2.4 to 2.5 for branching forms against about 1.8 to 2.0 for massive and tabular — and the structural complexity that fish density and biomass track. This route DIRECTLY OPPOSES the storm route above. They cannot both be satisfied by one colony.
- **Simulate the shape before you outplant, using a published growth model** (colony): A predicted morphospace position — compactness, symmetry, upstream bias — before anything is in the water. The model class is real and runnable; what is MISSING is the mapping from its dimensionless control parameter to a reef flow speed in centimetres per second.
- **Select the cryptic lineage that already has the shape you want** (colony): A stable compact or open morph with its associated bleaching threshold, which does not drift back because it is a lineage rather than a plastic response.
- **Breed heat-tolerant parents and get about one degree-heating-week per generation** (population): Offspring with measurably higher tolerance — narrow-sense heritability 0.2 to 0.3, and a possible gain of up to 1 degree-heating-week in one generation. Set that against heatwaves that already deliver more than 12 degree-heating-weeks, and against assaying three-to-four-year-old offspring. Worse: selection for short-stress tolerance did NOT improve survival of long stress, with no genetic correlation detected.
- **Move warm-adapted parents poleward** (population): Larvae with up to a tenfold increase in the odds of surviving heat stress when a parent comes from a warmer location. At Ningaloo, larvae with ONE warmer-reef parent had 2.2-fold higher survival and those with TWO had 1.6-fold — the one-parent cross beat the two-parent cross, which is not what a simple dose model predicts.
- **Cross two Acropora species and hope for heterosis** (population): Sometimes better, sometimes not, and the two studies read here disagree. In one species pair some hybrid groups had higher survival and larger recruits; in another, hybrids did not outperform BOTH purebred parents and the purebred did best. One cross direction failed entirely.
- **Precondition with a sub-bleaching pulse, which nature already does** (population): More than 50% less cell mortality and symbiont loss — measured for the NATURAL version of this, in which 75% of past Great Barrier Reef thermal stress events carried such a protective pre-stress trajectory. The warning in the same paper is the point: another 0.5 degC of local warming could abolish the protective trajectory altogether.
- **Freeze the sperm, because you cannot breed from a genotype that is gone** (population): Preserved genotypes and the ability to cross across years and across ranges. It adds NO heat tolerance. In one species frozen sperm fertilised as well as fresh; in another, fertilisation, larval yield, settlement and post-settlement survival were lower by 23%, 23%, 14% and 8%.
- **Put the right crustose coralline alga on the tile** (larval): Far more attached, metamorphosed spat per larva released: Titanoderma cf. tessellatum induced at least 50% settlement in 14 of 15 coral species, mean 81%. But no crustose coralline alga is a universal inducer — three species settled below 10% under every treatment tested — so the alga has to be matched to the coral.
- **Tetrabromopyrrole: 100% metamorphosis, 0-2% attachment — a route that fails** (larval): Larvae that become fully developed polyps within six hours and then do not stick. Attachment of 0 to 2%. This is a wasted cohort, and it is in the table AS A NEGATIVE CONTROL: it shows that metamorphosis and attachment are separable processes and that an assay scoring only metamorphosis will call this a success.
- **Give the spat somewhere to hide: 4 mm grooves** (larval): First-year survival of about 39% against 13% on flat control tiles, in one Acropora tenuis trial. The starkest number in the whole study is not the 39%: it is 100% mortality of recruits on UPPER surfaces, nearly all within six months. Where the spat sits matters more than almost anything else on this list.
- **Wedge unattached tetrapods into the reef instead of gluing anything** (larval): About 9.6% recruit survival at one year with 67% of tetrapods holding at least one colony, at 5 to 18 times lower outplanting cost and 1.5 to 7% of the deployment time. The trade-off is measured: bolting takes about 120 seconds against about 10 for wedging, and it doubled or quadrupled the proportion of units still holding a live coral.
- **Release hundreds of thousands of larvae into a netted plot** (larval): Colonies on the reef at three years, about 16 cm across, spawning — where control plots produced no visible colonies of that species at all. Now the arithmetic: 47 colonies survived from about 900,000 larvae across three plots, which is about 5 x 10^-5 larvae per surviving colony, at a production cost of USD 35.20 each.
- **Play reef sound at the settlement site** (larval): About 1.7 to 1.8 times more settlers near the speaker, detectable at least 30 m away, with a maximum of seven times in one trial. The honest frame: in flow-through conditions the response was minimal and the difference BETWEEN TWO REEFS was tenfold, so site choice outweighs the speaker.
- **Screen the site for sediment before you spend anything else** (larval): Avoids near-total failure. Two millimetres of sediment cuts settlement probability to 25% or less and four millimetres suppresses it; burying recruits under four millimetres cuts ten-day survival to between 0 and 31%. The field failure case is stark: at one site 9% of ceramic devices held a recruit at 21 months and 0% of travertine tiles did, DESPITE travertine attracting more than 2.5 times the settlement.
- **Skip sexual recruitment entirely: microfragment the adults** (larval): Up to ten times more tissue than large fragments over 31 months, once plots with heavy predation are excluded — and predation hit the microfragments and not the large ones. This route does not solve the recruitment bottleneck; it goes around it, which means it adds no genetic diversity at all.
- **Put it where it will still be alive in 2050** (ecological): Restored colonies that are not sitting in the places most likely to cook. Current practice does the opposite: restoration sites cluster near human settlements, outcomes are not predicted by environmental covariates, most restored localities face high or severe bleaching risk by mid-century, and more than half of recently restored sites are ALREADY affected.
- **Get one bred cohort into the water, under permit, and measure it** (ecological): The first field survival data for bred versus wild corals. NO SUCH TRIAL WAS LOCATED. The programme furthest along chose larval seeding and seeding of aquaculture-propagated corals for its 2025 pilots on technology-readiness grounds; assisted evolution was not among them.
- **Treat every route above as a bridge, and say what it is a bridge to** (ecological): Under moderate warming, reefs that persist. Under high emissions, the same reefs lost anyway. The modelling says adaptation succeeds under lower-emissions scenarios and fails under higher ones, and transplanting tolerant individuals at 1-5% per year speeds adaptation without changing which side of that line you are on.

### What a heatwave does to the partnership, and which knob actually moves it

*host-symbiont bioenergetic model under thermal stress · symbiont scale*

**The design question it answers.** Four routes on this page claim to raise thermal tolerance by acting on four different things: the symbiont's thermal optimum, its photoprotective capacity, how much oxidative stress it takes to do damage, and how readily the host expels symbionts under that stress. Are they additive, or does one dominate? And what does each cost when there is no heatwave?

The dynamical core is not invented here: it is a line-by-line port of the published coRal model, checked against the author's own R code to 5e-15 relative error across three environments. That matters because coRal contains NO temperature variable at all -- in the published model bleaching is driven by light -- so the thermal coupling is this page's addition and enters through exactly one parameter. The study's weakness is that single calibration: one parameter was tuned so the unmodified holobiont bleaches inside NOAA's 4-8 degree-heating-week window, which means the model cannot then be used as evidence about that window. A second weakness is numerical and is reported as a failure rather than smoothed over: the step-halving check fails at 9%, because coRal integrates with explicit Euler and a first-crossing readout inherits that slow convergence. The absolute threshold is therefore not converged; the knob ranking, which is what the study claims, is identical at three step sizes.

**THREE OF THE FOUR INTERVENTIONS BUY A CORAL THAT STILL LOOKS BROWN WHILE IT STARVES. Measured on two readouts: on symbiont loss all four design knobs move the failure threshold by a comparable amount, but on the host's CARBON BALANCE the symbiont thermal optimum moves it 5.9 times further than the next best knob (+2.00 degC of anomaly against +0.34, +0.28 and +0.21). Raising photoprotection, ROS tolerance or symbiont retention keeps the ALGAE IN PLACE without keeping the HOST FED. Anyone selecting on visual bleaching score -- which is most of the restoration field -- is scoring the readout that cannot tell those apart. And the thermal knob is not free either. With the trade-off Cunning et al. measured put into the model -- heat-tolerant symbiont proportion raises bleaching resistance and lowers photochemical efficiency, linearly -- the thermal gain from +2 degC falls to zero at a coupling strength of 0.39 -- but whether a real coral sits before or after that point cannot be answered here, because the measured 29% growth penalty is NOT REACHABLE as a steady state at any coupling in the swept range: the worst the model will produce is -14.4%, and past that the holobiont drops into the bleached alternate state instead of settling at a larger penalty. A second arm reaches the same gap by scaling the carbon-fixation ceiling directly, so it is the model's bistability and not either arm's parameterisation. Underneath all of it: the dynamical core is a line-by-line port of the published coRal model, reproducing the author's own R code to 5e-15 relative error, but CORAL HAS NO TEMPERATURE IN IT -- the published model bleaches corals with light. The thermal coupling is this file's addition, it enters through exactly one parameter, and every thermal number here is a property of that extension. The unmodified holobiont bleaches at 5.7 degree heating weeks, which is where the single calibrated parameter put it and is therefore not evidence about NOAA's 4-8 window.**

43 parameters, of which 14 are assumed rather than sourced.

- This is a SIMULATION. No coral was measured. Nothing here is evidence that any intervention raises a real coral's bleaching threshold.
- coRal CONTAINS NO TEMPERATURE. The published model bleaches corals with light, not heat. The thermal coupling is this file's addition, it enters through exactly one parameter, and every number that depends on temperature depends on that choice. A different coupling -- heat acting on symbiont growth, on host respiration, or on the repair arm directly -- would give different thresholds.
- ONE parameter was calibrated: sigma_hot, set so the unmodified holobiont bleaches inside the observed 4-8 degree-heating-week window. The model therefore cannot be used as evidence about that window, and the headline says so.
- The thermal performance curve is an assumed shape with three assumed parameters. No measured thermal performance curve for Symbiodiniaceae photochemistry was retrieved; the research block records that symbiont density per cm2, a bleached-density threshold, mitotic index and absolute Fv/Fm are all unavailable from the sources read.
- The knob RANGES are three-quarters invented. Only T_opt's 2 degC span is sourced (Berkelmans & van Oppen 2006; Pettay et al. 2015, both abstracts). The two doublings and the fivefold cut in b are round numbers. So the ranking of knobs by span is partly a ranking of my choices, which is why the elasticity block exists.
- Host and symbiont biomass are unbounded in coRal, so 'the colony died' is not representable: the model has no mortality. A holobiont whose S/H collapses is scored as bleached, and what happens to it afterwards is outside the model.
- One symbiont type, not a population. Symbiont shuffling -- the route that most of the symbiont literature is about -- is a change in the MIX of two or more types, and cannot be represented by moving one thermal optimum. coRal supports multiple symbionts (nsym > 1) and this port does not use that; a shuffling study would.
- No acclimatisation and no plasticity: the knobs are constants in time, so the preconditioning route (Ainsworth et al. 2016) cannot be represented at all. Nor can anything epigenetic.
- The growth cost IS scored (readout C), but it comes out about an order of magnitude too small. Raising the thermal optimum by 2 degC costs under 2% of host specific growth here, against the 29% (laboratory) and 38% (field) that Jones & Berkelmans 2010 measured for Durusdinium-hosting Acropora millepora, and the 'nearly half' calcification reduction Pettay et al. 2015 report. The Durusdinium cost arm shows the model has no steady state with a penalty that large at all. So the model reproduces the SIGN of the trade-off and not its size, and nothing here should be read as a prediction of what a symbiont swap costs.
- Calcification is not in the model at all. coRal tracks host carbon biomass, not skeleton, so the Pettay 'nearly half' calcification figure has no counterpart here and the comparison above is to GROWTH, which is a different quantity measured a different way.
- Light is constant in the main arms and the anomaly is a single linear ramp. Real thermal histories are not ramps, and in coRal light is the primary driver, so a reef whose summer light and summer temperature peak together is a harsher case than anything here. The seasonal-light robustness arm is one step toward that and not a substitute for real forcing.
- DEGREE HEATING WEEKS CANNOT SEE A SUB-1-degC FAILURE, by construction. The NOAA convention sums HotSpots only where SST exceeds the maximum monthly mean by at least 1 degC, so any failure mode that bites below that accumulates zero DHW. In this model host carbon balance is exactly such a mode: the wild type's growth rate goes negative at an anomaly of about +1 degC, right at the deadband, where the DHW reading is still 0. That is why readout B is reported in degC of anomaly as well, and why the knob ranking and the additivity verdict are taken in degC rather than in degC-weeks. It is a property of the metric, not of the model, and it is worth knowing for anything that uses DHW as a design currency.
- The host is fragile here BECAUSE feeding was switched off. X = 0 in every arm, so the host has no carbon source but its symbiont. Cunning et al.'s own abstract states that bleaching in this model is 'attenuated by feeding', so a fed holobiont would fail later and readout B's sensitivity is partly a consequence of a declared modelling choice.
- Degree heating weeks are computed to the NOAA convention from a SYNTHETIC temperature series. The 4 and 8 degC-week lines on the figures are field thresholds drawn for scale, taken from the NOAA product page at read_level web-page; the primary methods papers were not obtained.
- The bleaching criterion (0.5 of control S/H) is declared but arbitrary. Thresholds move with it, as the criterion_sensitivity block shows. What is tested is whether the knob RANKING moves, which is a weaker claim than it sounds: it is still a ranking inside one model.

### The bottleneck nobody engineers: which intervention actually moves surviving recruits

*Monte Carlo sensitivity analysis over stated unknowns · larval scale*

**The design question it answers.** Restoration does not usually fail because adult corals die. It fails at recruitment. Given larval supply, a settlement cue, and early post-settlement mortality, which intervention moves the most surviving recruits per unit of effort?

This is deliberately a sensitivity analysis and not a point prediction, because the parameters for a prediction do not exist: there is no published value for eggs per colony, wild pelagic larval mortality, no-cue settlement probability, settlers per larva released in the field, or the size at which a recruit escapes incidental grazing. The baseline therefore spans four orders of magnitude, and that is the honest answer rather than a defect. Its own weakness is the cost model: the interventions are ranked per unit of effort and the effort units are mostly invented, with exactly one cost ratio carrying direct published support. The most useful output is not the ranking but the sensitivity decomposition, which shows that about four fifths of the spread comes from stages whose range this page had to invent -- so the highest-value next experiment in this whole chain is a measurement, not a trial.

**NO INTERVENTION IN THIS TABLE MOVES RECRUITMENT AS MUCH AS THE DIFFERENCE BETWEEN A NORMAL YEAR AND A HEATWAVE YEAR, and this model has no thermal year in it at all. Speare et al. measured recruit survivorship of 33% in a normal year against 2% in a heatwave year at the same site in Moorea -- a 16-fold swing -- while the best intervention here has a median multiplier of 22x on a quantity whose own baseline spans four orders of magnitude. The second result is the decomposition, and it is the kind only a sensitivity analysis reaches: about 80% of the spread comes from stages whose range this page had to INVENT, led by 'Encounters suitable substrate' at 40%. So the highest-value next experiment in this entire chain is a MEASUREMENT, not an intervention trial. ## The chain across scales, and where it is not calibrated

From a molecular knob to a coral that is still alive next decade. One junction is a validated port, one is an extension we made, one is refused outright, and one was attempted and missed.

1. **A thermal-tolerance knob → Symbiont retention** — *model*. Inside a port of a published model, reproduced to 5e-15 against the authors' own R. On this readout all four knobs look comparable — which is the problem, because the readout is confounded: one of the knobs IS the coefficient converting oxidative stress into symbiont loss, so asking whether it dominates is close to asking whether b equals b. 1,868 of 2,401 map cells censor on it.
2. **Symbiont retention → A fed host** — *model*. The junction that separates the knobs, read on host carbon balance, where that coefficient does not appear. The thermal optimum moves the threshold about six times further than the other three. This is where 'brown while it starves' comes from.
3. **Our thermal coupling → The published model** — *scenario*. Labelled rather than hidden. The published model has no temperature variable — bleaching there is light-driven — so the coupling is an addition of ours entering through one parameter. A break-even against the measured 29% growth penalty could not be computed at all: the model's worst steady state is -14.4% before it falls into the bleached attractor, and an independent arm hits the same wall at -22.3%. Two routes, one wall, so it is the model's bistability rather than either parameterisation.
4. **An intervention → Surviving recruits** — *scenario*. A sensitivity analysis over stated unknowns, not a prediction. Four fifths of the spread comes from stages with no published wild value, so the ordering is a statement about our ignorance as much as about the interventions. The effort units were themselves perturbed: the top two hold in 87% of draws, and the order within them is 58/41.
5. **Growth parameters → Colony shape** — *refused*. Declared and not reached. The model spans compact mound to open lobed mound and produces neither branching nor plating; light weighting changes smoothness, not flatness. The externally comparable metric agrees it grows mounds. A published counterpart exists — light geometry alone does not explain depth zonation either — so it is a result rather than only a shortfall.

> One junction is a validated port, two are scenario-level, one is refused, and one was aimed at and missed. The end-to-end statement — this intervention gives that reef — is refused. What the chain delivers instead is an ordering of what is worth doing: measure the stages nobody has measured, and do not expect any of this to outrun a heatwave year.

## What it would take to put any of this in the water

**Is a coral a welfare subject?.**

Scleractinian corals are cnidarians: a nerve net, no brain, no centralised nervous system. The usual sentience arguments for vertebrates and cephalopods do not transfer, and no regulator we could identify treats coral research as animal research. We are not going to claim a settled answer from a literature search that did not address the question.

**What the real objection is.**

It is ecological, not individual. Almost every route here acts on a POPULATION or an ECOSYSTEM, and several release a self-replicating organism into open water. A transgenic symbiont, a therapeutic phage and an amoxicillin paste are not recallable in the way a laboratory animal is, and the harm, if there is one, lands on an ecosystem that nobody owns.

**The one documented harm in this table.**

Durusdinium trenchii established in Caribbean hosts, where it is not native, with low clonal diversity and calcification in affected hosts 'reduced by nearly half' relative to hosts carrying native symbionts (Pettay et al. 2015). The authors read the new symbioses as maladapted. That is an inference rather than a fitness measurement, and it is the clearest instance on this page of a heat-tolerance gain arriving with an ecosystem-scale cost.

**Deliberate harm as a method.**

Two routes here work by injuring the coral on purpose. Symbiont shuffling requires deliberately bleaching a living colony, after which growth falls by more than half for up to 18 months. Microfragmentation works BECAUSE of injury-driven edge growth. Both are standard practice and neither is usually described this way.

**Antibiotic release.**

Amoxicillin paste is applied directly onto wild reefs, lesion by lesion, for a disease whose pathogen is unknown. One metagenomic study of 41 colonies found no effect on beta-lactamase diversity after repeated treatment. That is a narrow negative — one species, 41 colonies, one gene family — and not a survey of resistance on the surrounding reef, which nobody read here has done.

**The honest asymmetry.**

The counterfactual is not an undisturbed reef. It is a reef that in many places is already losing most of its coral, and a median restoration project that covers 100 square metres. Precaution about intervention has to be weighed against a baseline that is itself moving fast, and the people arguing both sides of that agree on the numbers and disagree about what follows.

## What this page does not claim

- No coral was engineered, nothing went in the water, and no wet-lab experiment was run for this page.
- The three studies are simulations. None is an observation, and none is calibrated to a measured reef.
- The bleaching model's thermal coupling is ours, not the published model's. The published model contains no temperature variable at all; bleaching in it is driven by light. Every thermal number here is a property of our extension.
- The morphology study did NOT reach branching or plating at any setting tried. No route on this page may cite it as showing that a growth model reproduces branching.
- One pre-declared control in the morphology study fails. The criterion was not moved: a solid hemisphere of the same voxel count measures 2.462 and a solid cube 2.470 against a declared threshold of 2.6, so no object of any shape could have reached it at this size — the criterion was wrong rather than the model, and both the failure and the replacement are shown.
- The bleaching study's step-halving convergence check fails at 9.3% against a declared 5%. The absolute threshold does not survive refinement; the knob ranking and the additivity verdict do, and only those are quoted.
- The effort units behind the intervention ranking are mostly invented. Exactly one cost ratio has direct published support. 'Those two interventions are in a different class' is quotable; 'site screening beats larval enrichment' is not.
- 22 of 42 organism-level routes rest on cross-genus transfer or weaker, and 7 on an animal that is not a reef-building coral.
- NONE OF THESE ROUTES HAS BEEN ATTEMPTED HERE. This is a reading of a literature, with two simulations beside it. No coral was touched.
- Every PMID cited has been title-matched in PubMed, but READ LEVELS VARY and are given per source. Most are abstract-level. The one systematic review here notes publication bias toward success in the restoration-cost literature, and the two deliberate failure cases we did keep (tetrabromopyrrole, and the Lizard Island device trial) are in because they happened to be published, not because we searched for failures.
- Several of the negatives are scoped to a single PubMed query shape and say so inline. 'Not found by this search' is not 'does not exist', and preprints, non-indexed journals, datasets and theses were largely not searched.
- Regulatory statements are the weakest content on this page. No primary legal text was read. The Convention on Biological Diversity and Cartagena Protocol party statuses were verified by fetching the treaty pages; everything about Australian gene-technology law, Marine Park permits, the US Endangered Species Act and FDA jurisdiction is a search summary or an inference, and is labelled as such in the route.
- The outcome classes are regex tags over our own prose, so they describe how we wrote a route as much as what the route does. The ordering rule is declared with them because getting it backwards would silently retag most of the table.

## Why the uncomfortable number is the useful one

A design space is supposed to tell you what to do next. Here it says: stop ranking interventions and go measure how often a larva meets a surface it can settle on, because that single unmeasured stage carries more of the uncertainty than every intervention in the table combined. And it says the thing nobody selling reef restoration wants to put on a slide — that the best intervention we could model is smaller than the gap between a normal year and a hot one. Both conclusions came out of models that were built to answer something else, which is the argument for building them.
