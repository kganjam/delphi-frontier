# 15. Redesigning insect flight: the design space

Generated by `scripts/designspace_doc.py` from the same files the live page reads, so the two cannot drift.
The interactive version, with the figures and the filterable route list, is at <https://studio.morphoswarm.ai/#/studio/insect-flight>.

**The question.** Ultrabithorax represses wing identity on the third thoracic segment, and losing it gives Lewis's four-winged fly — one of the best-evidenced regulatory lesions in biology. The obvious engineering question is whether more wings means more flight. This page maps every route we can source to a redesigned flight apparatus, and asks what the four-winged fly would actually do if you threw it in the air.

## It would fly badly, for two reasons, and neither is "four wings".

Start with the result that needs no biology at all. Lift scales with the second moment of wing area, so splitting one wing into two smaller ones at the same total area and aspect ratio loses more than half of it. In our blade-element model, four wings at the same total area carry 0.46 body weights — it does not leave the ground. Four wings at double the area do fly, at 1.83 body weights, but they cost twice the mass-specific mechanical power. That kills the intuitive "redistribute the area" design before any gene is named, and it falls out of geometry.

The configuration the literature actually produced is worse than either, and the reason is the first thing a designer should know. In flies homozygous for the classic allele combination, the adults have two pairs of wings, but — in the authors' own words — "the homeotically transformed T3 (HT3) has only rudimentary indirect flight muscles". The lesion transforms the epidermis; the mesoderm keeps expressing Antennapedia, which is the likely reason the flight muscle never forms. You get a wing with no engine. Modelled, that configuration carries 0.84 body weights, and it stays below one at every non-negative value of the contested fore–hind interference term, so the conclusion does not rest on choosing a convenient value for a number nobody has pinned down.

The second cost is the sensor. A haltere is a hindwing already spent on something else — a gyroscopic rate sensor — and converting it back to a wing deletes it. The model says why that matters, and the useful form of the answer is not "vision is weaker". Sweeping the feedback delay and giving every delay the best gain available to it, no loop slower than about three wingbeats, 14 milliseconds, can meet the measured recovery from a disturbance at any gain. The haltere loop is inside that bound. The visual loop is well outside it. Vision is not too weak; it is too slow to be turned up.

A caution the page insists on: none of this says four wings are worse than two. Dragonflies have flown with four independently controlled wings for 300 million years, and their fore–hind phasing can improve efficiency rather than cost it. Remove roughly half the wing area from a moth or a butterfly and it still flies, losing acceleration but not turning radius. What makes the dipteran case different is specific to Diptera: the hindwing has already been spent, so converting it back costs a sensor and arrives without an engine.

So the route worth proposing is the one nobody has run. Transform the T3 mesoderm as well as the epidermis, and give the second wing pair an engine. Both halves are published separately — that T2 mesoderm is specified entirely by induction and expresses no selector of its own, that ectopic homeotic expression in it respecifies its muscle, and that Antennapedia persists in the transformed T3 mesoderm. The experiment is to run that in the other direction.

> We found no published measurement of free-flight performance in a four-winged Drosophila. Everything above assembles two separately published phenotypes — rudimentary indirect flight muscle in the transformed segment, and haltere necessity in otherwise normal flies — plus our own model. Nobody has thrown one in the air and measured it.

## If you asked us to actually do it

**The route.** Give the four-winged fly an engine: restore indirect flight muscle in the transformed T3 (`act-restore-t3-flight-muscle`)

**Headline.**

If the question is 'could you redesign an insect's flight apparatus', the honest answer is that it has been done since 1978 and the result flies badly. If the question is 'could you redesign it so that it flies BETTER', the one route nobody has reported trying is the one that fixes what the 1978 result broke: transform the T3 MESODERM as well as the T3 epidermis, so the second pair of wings has an engine. Everything that route needs is published; the combination is not.

**Why.**

- It is the only route on this page that attacks the actual defect. The four-winged fly's second pair is not a bad wing - Fernandes et al. report it as a wing - it is a wing with 'only rudimentary indirect flight muscles'. Adding an aerofoil without an actuator is the single commonest error in the intuition this page is answering.
- Both halves are already published, separately. Roy et al. showed that T2 mesoderm uniquely expresses no bithorax- or Antennapedia-complex selector and is specified entirely by induction, that ectopic homeotic expression IN T2 MESODERM respecifies its muscle, and that Antp persists in transformed-T3 mesoderm. The experiment is to run that in the other direction.
- It is scoreable. A thorax either has a second set of indirect flight muscles or it does not, and that is a dissection, not a judgement.
- It leaves the page's hard question standing rather than dodging it. Even if the muscle route succeeds, the animal still has no halteres, and our own control model puts the usable feedback-loop delay at about 3 wingbeats - roughly 14 ms - against about 30 ms for vision. So the honest prediction is a four-winged fly that can generate force and cannot hold attitude.

**The answer to the judges question.**

No - not as posed. Converting halteres into wings costs an actuator AND a sensor to buy a surface, and the published phenotype confirms both costs independently. But 'four wings are worse than two' is NOT a general truth, and the page says so: dragonflies have kept four independently controlled wings for over 300 million years and their fore-hind phasing can IMPROVE efficiency, and moths stripped of their hindwings still fly while losing their evasive manoeuvring. What makes the dipteran case different is that in flies the hindwing has already been spent on something else.

**What would change our mind.**

A measurement of free-flight performance in a four-winged fly. We found none. Every quantitative flight statement on this page about four-winged Drosophila is an inference from the muscle phenotype and from haltere ablation in otherwise normal flies, not a flight measurement in the mutant. That gap is the most important thing on this page and it is stated here rather than buried in the limitations.

**Cheapest informative experiment.**

Load the haltere knob (route sen-haltere-loading). It is reversible, needs no genetics, has a demonstrated recovery control, and separates 'the haltere's MASS matters' from 'the haltere's SENSING matters' - which is the confound in every ablation experiment on this page.

## The design space: 46 routes

Every row is a published perturbation or a bounded proposal, with its own evidence level. **None of them has been attempted here.** Sources carry read levels: a listing is not a reading.

### Unplaced — 46

These routes name a scale the page does not declare (actuator, cis, comparative, gene, network, neural, organism, pattern, sensor). They are listed rather than dropped.

- **Disable the Ubx-bound enhancers that build a haltere, leaving Ubx protein intact** (cis): A PARTIAL wing-ward transformation of the haltere, target by target, rather than the whole-appendage switch a Ubx null gives. Which traits move depends on which target you hit: Ubx acts on genes at several levels of the wing-patterning hierarchy at once, so no single enhancer carries the whole identity.
- **Titrate Ubx dose rather than removing it, and ask for an intermediate appendage** (cis): AN INTERMEDIATE APPENDAGE, if the decision is a dial. Wing-like traits appear once Ubx falls to about 39% of its endogenous level, and the approach to that point is the question: a graded appendage or a switch. Our own model (study 1) fits the graded reading and, calibrated on the Ubx arm alone, predicts the independently measured Antp threshold to within 4.0%; the switch-like variant misses it by 39.2%. (An earlier, stale copy of that study reported the gap as a factor of 40; re-running its own code showed the switch-like variant passes the same controls, which makes this a fair comparison and a smaller one.) The sharper test needs no model at all: the two published thresholds sit on OPPOSITE branches of the switch, so their ordering tests hysteresis directly, and under both protein-ratio measurements the ordering is the one a hysteretic switch forbids - under the GFP ratio the two doses are within 4% of each other, i.e. effectively a single threshold.
- **Change organ SIZE without changing organ identity, through the Ubx-dally-Dpp axis** (cis): A BIGGER haltere that is still a haltere. The haltere ends larval life with roughly fivefold fewer cells than the wing, and this is the axis that sets that number - so this route separates 'how large' from 'what kind', which is exactly the distinction a wing-number redesign keeps conflating.
- **Drive the vestigial quadrant enhancer in a segment that has no wing** (cis): ECTOPIC WING TISSUE growing out of whatever structure you switched it on in. In Drosophila, vestigial is 'sufficient to induce outgrowths of wing tissue from eyes, legs and antennae' - which is the cleanest demonstration on this page that wing number is not a body-plan constant.
- **The four-winged fly: remove Ubx function from T3** (gene): TWO PAIRS OF WINGS, and a fly that is worse at flying than it was - for two reasons that are NOT equally well evidenced, and the page says which is which. The strong one: the transformed T3 has 'only rudimentary indirect flight muscles'. The second pair has no engine. That is direct, quoted, unambiguous insect phenotype. The weaker one: the halteres are gone with it, and the sensory cost of that is real but GRADED, task-dependent and clade-dependent rather than a flat failure - see the sensor scale. So the honest answer is that the swap costs an actuator outright and a sensor partially, to buy a surface.
- **Run it backwards: force Ubx in T2 and convert the wing into a haltere** (gene): A HALTERE-LIKE WING: Wingless signalling and a subset of Wg- and Dpp-activated genes such as spalt-related are repressed, and the blade loses the wing's margin bristles and its veins. A fly with four halteres and no wings does not fly at all, which is the other end of the same axis and is worth putting on the page beside the four-winged one.
- **Use Antennapedia instead of Ubx, at high dose, to make a wing haltere-like** (gene): A HALTERE-LIKE WING at around 490% of endogenous Antp. That threshold is the quantitative test on this page: a Hox dose model calibrated on the Ubx arm alone predicts it, so the two Hox proteins are acting as one repressive 'dose' on one selector rather than as two qualitatively different instructions.
- **Delete vestigial and lose the wing entirely** (gene): NO WING. vestigial is selectively required for wing-cell proliferation; without it the blade does not grow. This is the negative control for the whole page: it shows the wing is a selector-defined organ, so wing NUMBER is a question about where the selector is switched on.
- **Ask what vestigial does in a beetle, where one of the segments is already wingless** (gene): Loss of wing tissue, AND the discovery that the 'wingless' first thoracic segment of a beetle contains two groups of vg-dependent tissues. That matters for a redesign: a segment with no wing is not necessarily a segment with no wing PROGRAMME, so the cheapest place to add a wing may be a segment that already has the latent tissue.
- **Remove the dorsoventral organiser with apterous and get no wing at all** (gene): NO WING. apterous specifies dorsal identity and creates the dorsoventral compartment boundary; the organiser at that boundary is what induces the blade. The failure mode is instructive for a designer: you cannot add a wing by adding wing tissue, you add it by creating a BOUNDARY.
- **Attack the hinge rather than the blade, with nubbin** (gene): LOSS OF WING STRUCTURES with a localised requirement in the wing HINGE, not the blade. A design that adds wings has to articulate them, and this is the gene that says the hinge is its own growth-control centre rather than the proximal end of the blade's.
- **Change hindwing identity in an insect whose hindwing is still a wing** (gene): A HINDWING THAT LOOKS MORE LIKE A FOREWING, including its colour pattern. This is the route that separates 'Ubx makes a haltere' from 'Ubx makes a hindwing DIFFERENT': in Lepidoptera the same gene differentiates two flying wings rather than destroying one, which is the existence proof that a four-winged redesign is reachable without losing an aerofoil.
- **Specify an extra wing field with localised Wingless** (network): AN ADDITIONAL WING FIELD, if the timing is right. Wing specification is a second-instar event, so this route has a window rather than a dose, which is the kind of knob that makes a design reproducible.
- **Build the organiser from scratch with Notch, Serrate and Delta** (network): AN ECTOPIC WING MARGIN and the outgrowth that follows it. Serrate signals ventrally-to-dorsally and Delta dorsally-to-ventrally, so a designer has two complementary ligands and can place the boundary rather than inherit it.
- **Reshape the wing by reshaping the Dpp gradient** (network): A LARGER OR SMALLER BLADE with its target genes re-positioned: optomotor-blind and spalt are activated at different distances from the source, so moving the gradient moves the landmarks the venation is built on. This is the knob that changes wing AREA, which study 3 shows is the only aerodynamically profitable thing about adding wings in the first place.
- **Replace the wing selector's co-factor with its human ortholog** (network): A NORMAL WING, built with a human protein in place of the fly's own. TONDU 'substitutes for Vg function in wing formation'. For a design space this is the load-bearing fact: the wing selector is a module with an interface, and the interface has survived the whole bilaterian radiation, so a redesign can swap parts rather than rebuild them.
- **Lift the Ubx brake on EGFR/Ras in the haltere** (network): A HALTERE WITH WING-LIKE CUTICLE AND VEINS, approached from the pathway side rather than the selector side. Wing and haltere epithelia differ in 'the size, shape, spacing and number of cuticular hairs' as well as in veins, so this route separates surface differentiation from appendage identity.
- **Change vein number through rhomboid and the EGFR amplification loop** (pattern): MORE OR FEWER LONGITUDINAL VEINS, and ectopic vein material where rhomboid is switched on. rhomboid 'mediates the localized formation of wing veins' and is the amplifier that commits a provein cell.
- **Narrow or widen the veins with Delta-Notch lateral inhibition** (pattern): VEINS OF THE WRONG WIDTH, or vein and intervein fates mis-partitioned within the provein. This is the mechanism our venation model is a caricature of: Delta is higher in central provein cells and Notch higher in lateral ones, which is lateral inhibition refining a stripe.
- **Move a vein rather than adding one, with Hedgehog** (pattern): VEINS IN DIFFERENT PLACES, same number. This is the route that distinguishes POSITION from COUNT, and it is why our reaction-diffusion model of venation can only speak to the second: a Turing mechanism sets a spacing, and Hedgehog sets an address.
- **Attack the intervein instead: blistered / DSRF** (pattern): A BLISTERED WING - the two epithelial sheets fail to appose - or ectopic vein where intervein should be. For a design this is the structural failure mode: a wing is two sheets glued together, and the glue is a cell fate.
- **Give the four-winged fly an engine: restore indirect flight muscle in the transformed T3** (actuator): A SECOND PAIR OF DRIVEN WINGS - the route that would make a four-winged fly actually fly, and NOBODY HAS REPORTED DOING IT. The two ingredients are published separately: the transformed T3 has only rudimentary indirect flight muscles, and Antp persisting in T3 mesoderm is the suggested cause. Specifically ectopic homeotic expression in T2 mesoderm respecifies its muscle pattern, so the mesodermal switch is known to be sufficient in the other direction. Nobody has reported running it in this one.
- **Redesign the gearbox: the wing hinge sclerites** (actuator): SPECIFIC, PREDICTABLE CHANGES IN WING MOTION. The hinge 'transforms tiny, high-frequency oscillations of specialized power muscles into the sweeping back-and-forth motion of the wings', and a trained model now predicts wing motion from steering-muscle activity and attributes it to individual sclerites. If you want a differently-moving wing, this is a far shorter path than a differently-built one.
- **Use the tension muscles: tp1 and the indirect steering system** (actuator): A CHANGE IN FLIGHT STABILISATION without touching the direct steering muscles. Connectome data show the indirect steering muscles receive some of the same synaptic input from the sensory apparatus as the direct ones, which makes them a second, parallel actuator for the same control loop.
- **Re-tune the thorax: the scutellum and sub-epimeral ridge** (actuator): DESYNCHRONISED WINGS AND HALTERES, and a thorax whose resonant frequency no longer responds normally to wing damage. This is the route that shows wingbeat frequency is not a free parameter a designer can set: it is a property of wing-thorax resonance, and clipping wings raises it 'by as much as 90 Hz'.
- **Remove the halteres and measure what flight loses** (sensor): LOSS OF EQUILIBRIUM REFLEXES - but a GRADED and TASK-SPECIFIC loss, not a flat failure, and the page is careful about this because it is easy to overstate. What is measured: angular rotations normally elicit compensatory changes in stroke amplitude and frequency, and uni- and bilateral ablation removes them (Dickinson 1999). Haltereless flies cannot stabilise a WIDE-FIELD stimulus as accurately, yet fixate FIGURES with the same acuity, and can still flap and steer (Mureli & Fox 2015). Takeoff velocity and stability fall in Calyptratae and NOT in other flies (Yarger 2021). The flat claim 'haltere input is necessary for free flight' appears in this literature as a PREMISE sentence rather than as a measured result, and we did not find the free-flight ablation measurement it stands on - see `not_claimed` at the top level of this file.
- **Delete the sensor by DEGREES: graded silencing of haltere campaniform sensilla** (sensor): A LINEAR DECLINE in the magnitude of wing and head motor responses to yaw rotation as more sensilla are silenced. This is the route that turns 'the haltere is necessary' into a dose-response, and it is the sensory analogue of the Hox dose experiment in study 1: the same question - switch or dial - asked of the sensor rather than the selector.
- **Treat the haltere as an output, not just an input** (sensor): A CHANGE IN WING MOTOR NEURON SPIKE TIMING - i.e. steering - produced by moving the SENSOR rather than the wing. This is the finding that most undermines the simple story the page starts from: the haltere is not only a gyroscope being read, it is 'an adjustable clock' being set, and visual input reaches it.
- **Substitute the missing gyro: use the WING's own campaniform sensilla** (sensor): LOSS OF POSTURAL CONTROL in a moth, from a wing sensor rather than a haltere. This is the route that says a four-winged redesign need not be flying blind: the same class of mechanoreceptor sits on the wing, and in Lepidoptera it already does the job. Whether a fly could be made to use its wing sensilla the way a moth does is, as far as we found, untested.
- **Detune the sensor without removing it: load the haltere knob** (sensor): HALTERE FREQUENCY FALLS IN DISCRETE STEPS above a threshold load while WINGBEAT FREQUENCY DOES NOT MOVE - and the effect reverses when the load is removed. A reversible, graded, non-genetic way to break the rate gyro, which makes it the best control experiment on this page for 'is it the sensing, or is it the mass?'.
- **Count what converting a haltere into a wing actually spends** (sensor): A FLY WITH NO RATE GYRO. Every route on this page that produces a second pair of wings also produces this, and the literature nowhere reports a four-winged fly that kept its halteres. Our own model (study 3) puts the boundary at a feedback loop of about 3 wingbeats, roughly 14 ms: slower than that and the measured 60 ms recovery is unreachable AT ANY GAIN. The haltere loop is inside that boundary and the visual loop, at about 30 ms, is well outside it.
- **Skip the genetics: steer the insect with an implanted radio stimulator** (neural): CONTROLLED FLIGHT IN A FREE-FLYING BEETLE. Flight initiation, cessation and elevation through brain stimulation; turns through direct stimulation of either basalar muscle. If the goal is a redesigned flight apparatus that does something new, this is the only route on the page that has already produced a steerable animal, and it required no change to any gene.
- **The same trick on a small insect: optic-lobe stimulation in a honeybee** (neural): STEERING, verified experimentally. Optic-lobe stimulation was already established for initiation and cessation in small insects; the open question this addresses is whether the same electrode can turn the animal.
- **Rewire the fusion rule between the fast and the slow sense** (neural): A LINEAR SUM, for wing motor output - but NOT for gaze. That asymmetry is a design constraint: if you intend to compensate for a deleted haltere by upweighting vision, the literature says the two pathways are already summing linearly at the wing, so there is no untapped nonlinearity to exploit; and the head and the wings are not the same control problem.
- **Switch wings on and off without changing the genome: the pea aphid polyphenism** (organism): WINGED OR WINGLESS OFFSPRING FROM ONE GENOME. This is the only scale on the page where wing number is already a controllable variable in a living animal with no genetic lesion at all, and it is therefore the only route with no lethality and full reversibility.
- **Interrupt the wing network at a different point in each species: the ant castes** (organism): A WINGLESS CASTE, with the interruption at a DIFFERENT NODE in each species. For a design space this is the single most useful comparative fact on the page: the network is conserved in the winged castes, and the points at which it is broken in the wingless ones are 'evolutionarily labile' - so there is no one place a wing programme has to be cut, and a designer has many equivalent entry points.
- **Use a hormone receptor as the wing switch: the planthopper insulin pathway** (organism): LONG-WINGED OR SHORT-WINGED ADULTS, switched by a receptor rather than by a selector gene. Long-winged morphs fly and short-winged ones do not but are usually more fecund, which is the clearest statement anywhere on this page of what flight costs.
- **The same Hox gene, used as a morph switch instead of an identity switch** (organism): A WING-LENGTH CHANGE rather than a wing-identity change. In planthoppers Ubx is expressed in BOTH forewing and hindwing pads - 'different from the canonical model of Ubx expression' - and is regulated by the nutritional status of the host plant. The same protein that deletes a wing pair in a fly tunes wing LENGTH in a bug, which is the strongest warning on this page against reading a Drosophila Hox phenotype as a general rule.
- **Turn the haltere feedback up and down, and ask whether vision can do the same job** (sensor): SACCADE AMPLITUDE TRACKS THE HALTERE AND IGNORES VISION. Increasing haltere-mediated feedback decreases saccade amplitude and decreasing it increases amplitude, while altering visual feedback has no significant effect on saccade dynamics at all. This is the cleanest measured statement on the page that the two senses are not interchangeable, and it is GRADED rather than all-or-nothing - which is what an ablation experiment cannot show.
- **Measure the delay-versus-gain trade-off directly, on a robot** (neural): A HARD TRADE-OFF BETWEEN SENSOR DELAY AND PERMISSIBLE GAIN: the longer the delay, the lower the gain the system tolerates, so a slow sense cannot be compensated by turning it up. This route exists on the page because it is PRIOR ART for our own study 3, which rediscovered the same trade-off in simulation and put the usable-delay boundary at about 3 wingbeats. Our result is a re-derivation, not a discovery, and the page says so.
- **Count what else the haltere is doing before you spend it** (sensor): DEGRADED WALKING AND STANDING, in an animal that is not flying. Intact halteres are necessary to maintain normal walking speeds on vertical surfaces and to respond to sudden changes in gravity; crane flies with ablated halteres show reduced postural stability against perturbations and need longer to recover. This is a cost no flight model on this page counts, including ours, and it is charged to every route that converts a haltere into a wing.
- **Copy the animal that already has four independently controlled wings** (comparative): FOUR WINGS THAT CAN BE BETTER THAN TWO, at the right phase. Dragonflies 'have maintained their distinctive, independently controllable, four-winged form for over 300 Myr', and fore-hind phasing 'can improve aerodynamic efficiency by removing swirl'. This is the route that keeps the page honest: the answer to 'would four wings fly better' is not simply no.
- **Remove the hindwings of an insect that has four, and measure flight** (comparative): THEY STILL FLY, having lost nearly half their wing area, BUT they lose linear and turning acceleration and can no longer execute normal evasive flight. Turning RADIUS is unaffected; speed is not. This is the single most direct measurement on the page of what a second wing pair actually buys, and the answer is agility, not lift.
- **The halteres on the wrong segment: Strepsiptera** (comparative): A FLYING INSECT WITH THE HALTERE/WING ARRANGEMENT INVERTED. If the inversion is a homeotic change, then the design the page is discussing - move which segment carries the aerofoil and which carries the gyroscope - has already happened once in evolution, and the animal flies. If the two organs instead arose convergently, it has not. The literature treats this as OPEN, and the page keeps it open.
- **Ask whether halteres are necessary in every fly, and find that they are not** (comparative): HALTERE REMOVAL HURTS CALYPTRATAE AND NOT OTHER FLIES. In Calyptratae - house flies and relatives - removal cut both takeoff velocity and stability, through a slower leg extension; in non-Calyptratae, including a closely related species with an equally rapid takeoff, it did neither. What this is NOT in tension with is a competing FINDING. The flat sentence often quoted against it - that haltere input is required for unrestrained flight - recurs in this literature as an opening PREMISE, a reason for tethering the animal or for studying walking, and we found no free-flight ablation measurement behind it. So the picture is not two results in conflict; it is a strong background assumption sitting on top of measured results that are narrower than it, graded by task, and graded by clade. See `not_claimed` at the top level of this file.
- **Count how often evolution has already deleted wings, and whether it put them back** (comparative): THOUSANDS OF INDEPENDENT LOSSES, with about 5% of extant winged-order species flightless, and a contested claim that wings were RE-EVOLVED after loss. For a design space the operational reading is that the wing programme is easy to switch off and is not necessarily destroyed when it is, which is the same point the ant castes make from the network side.

## What we ran

Three studies, all simulations. None is an observation, none is calibrated to a measured four-winged fly, and nothing here was run in an animal. In the venation study two of four declared controls FAIL and are reported as failing rather than repaired, so read its controls before its result.

### Wing or haltere: is the Hox switch a dial or a cliff?

*simulation (ODE) · gene scale*

**The design question it answers.** Paul et al. measured the wing-versus-haltere decision from both directions - wing-like traits appear at 39% of endogenous Ubx, a haltere-like wing at 490% of endogenous Antp - and separately measured how much more Ubx than Antp a disc holds. Three numbers over-determine a one-dimensional Hox-dose model. So the model can be calibrated on the Ubx arm alone and then made to PREDICT the Antp arm, which is a test rather than a fit. Does it pass, and does it tell a dial from a cliff?

A one-state ODE for the Vestigial-Scalloped wing selector under Hox repression, run as two variants: a graded one with non-cooperative self-activation, and a bistable one with cooperative self-activation whose parameters were chosen so that it gets a FAIR test rather than a strawman one - wild-type T2 and T3 both sit outside its bistable window, and it passes the same controls the graded variant does. One parameter is fitted, to the Ubx arm only; the Antp threshold is then held out and predicted. On the protein ratio the source paper itself prefers, the graded variant lands 4.0% from the measured 490% and the bistable variant 39.2% away, so the held-out number discriminates between two mechanisms rather than merely fitting one. The study's own weakness is that it is a cartoon of a network with dozens of known nodes - vg, sd, wg, dpp, EGFR/Ras, sal, SRF and the achaete-scute complex are all collapsed into a single variable W - so a correct prediction here is evidence about the DOSE-RESPONSE SHAPE and not about the wiring; and the discrimination it reports is a 10-fold difference in error between two hand-built variants, not a model comparison with any statistical machinery behind it. The strongest result in the file needs no model at all: the two published thresholds sit on opposite branches of the switch, so their ORDERING tests hysteresis directly, and under both protein-ratio measurements the ordering is the one a hysteretic switch forbids. Verified afterwards against the paper's full text: all four numbers the study relies on are confirmed verbatim, the paper prefers the GFP-based protein ratio that the model's best arm uses, and it reports directly that 'intermediate levels led to intermediary phenotypes'.

**Calibrated on the Ubx arm alone, a one-dimensional Hox-dose model predicts the independently measured Antp transformation threshold to within 4% (graded variant, gfp_10.5 protein ratio), which is quantitative support for Paul et al.'s claim that wing-versus-haltere is set by Hox DOSE rather than Hox identity - but the two protein-ratio measurements in that same paper disagree enough to move the prediction by a factor of 1.76.**

- The Hox-dose assumption (Ubx and Antp interchangeable up to a scale factor) is known to be false for distant Hox proteins: Paul et al. show Abd-A rescues the four-winged phenotype and Scr does not. The model would be wrong for Scr.
- kx is fitted, so the model cannot be said to predict the Ubx threshold - only the Antp one. With a single fitted parameter and a single held-out number, the test is weak; it would be much stronger against a dose series, which the paper reports qualitatively but not as tabulated numbers I could extract.
- Time is nondimensional. gamma=1 sets the unit and the model says nothing about how long the decision actually takes, nor about the fact that Ubx acts at several successive stages of haltere morphogenesis (Pavlopoulos & Akam 2011, PMID 21282633).
- The readout W is an abstraction scored against a threshold declared in advance; it is not any measured quantity. Calling W>0.5 'a wing' is a modelling convention, not an observation.
- Spatial structure is absent. The real decision happens across a disc with compartment boundaries and a Dpp gradient whose RANGE Ubx controls (Crickmore & Mann 2006, PMID 16741075); a well-mixed ODE cannot represent that, and organ SIZE - which is most of what Ubx does - is outside the model entirely.

### How many veins can a wing have, and what sets the number?

*simulation (reaction-diffusion) · pattern scale*

**The design question it answers.** If you wanted a wing with different venation - more veins, fewer, somewhere else - what kind of knob would you be turning? A reaction-diffusion model is the cheapest way to ask whether vein NUMBER is set by the chemistry's own length scale or by the size of the sheet it runs on, and whether those two give the same answer.

A Gierer-Meinhardt activator-inhibitor system with saturation, run on a wing-blade-shaped domain, with the vein-counting rule declared in the module docstring before the first run and never changed. The inhibitor's diffusivity is not hand-picked: it is solved from the model's own linear stability analysis so that the fastest-growing wavelength would give five longitudinal veins, which makes the count a test of the nonlinear simulation against its own theory. It passes on SPACING and fails by one on COUNT - the measured wavelength matches the prediction to 0.1%, while the counted number falls short by a near-constant one vein across a 2.4-fold range of blade width, which is what two boundaries each suppressing half a wavelength looks like. The honest reading is that blade width is the clean knob and lateral-inhibition range is not: widen the inhibitor's reach much beyond wild type and the pattern breaks up rather than coarsening, so the counting rule goes out of its domain and the small numbers it returns mean 'no stripes' rather than 'fewer veins'. The study's largest weakness is that stripe ORIENTATION is imposed by anisotropic diffusion rather than earned: without that term the same kinetics make labyrinths, and in a real wing the orientation comes from Hedgehog and Dpp positional information laid down before lateral inhibition refines anything. Its second weakness is that NEITHER convergence control passes - the field is still drifting at the end of the run, and at least one seed's vein count still changes in the second half - so part of the reported seed spread is convergence rather than pattern formation, and the runs were deliberately not lengthened until the control passed.

**Under a counting rule declared before the first run, the model makes 4.0 +/- 0.0 longitudinal veins where its own linear stability analysis predicts 5.0. That deficit is not noise and it is not a one-off: across the blade-width sweep the counted number tracks the predicted number with a near-constant shortfall of 1.0 +/- 0.2 veins, which is the blade's two edges each suppressing a half-wavelength. So the model gets the SPACING right - the measured wavelength matches theory to 0.1% - and the COUNT wrong by one, every time. The lateral-inhibition sweep is messier: widening the inhibitor's reach removes veins one at a time down to about 2.5x wild type, and beyond that the pattern breaks up rather than coarsening, with the seed-to-seed spread exceeding the trend.**

### Would four wings fly better? Lift, power, and the organ you spent to get them

*simulation (blade-element aerodynamics and delayed-feedback control) · organism scale*

**The design question it answers.** The design intuition is that more wing area means more lift means better flight. The question this study exists to answer is what that intuition leaves out - and the literature says it leaves out two things, an actuator and a sensor, because the four-winged fly's second pair has only rudimentary flight muscle and the halteres it was made from were rate gyroscopes.

Two coupled models over the same four wing configurations: a quasi-steady blade-element model for lift, power and control authority, and a one-axis attitude model written as a delay-differential equation with saturating control, run with and without the fast haltere feedback loop. One parameter is calibrated - the lift coefficient, set so the two-wing wild type supports exactly body weight - which makes every other configuration a prediction, and all part-A results are ratios so the absolute scale cancels. The result to start from is pure geometry: redistributing the SAME total area over four wings gives 0.46 body weights and does not fly, because lift scales with the second moment of wing area, so splitting one wing into two smaller ones loses more than half of it and no contested biological parameter enters. Adding a second driven pair on top does buy lift, 1.83 body weights at twice the power, while the configuration the literature actually produced reaches 0.84 and stays below 1 across the whole admissible range of the contested fore-hind interference term rather than at one chosen value of it. The control half gives every loop delay the best gain in a swept family and still finds that no loop slower than about 3 wingbeats can meet the measured 60 ms recovery at any gain - the haltere loop is inside that boundary and the visual loop is well outside it. Three weaknesses, all stated in the file: 18 of 24 parameters are assumed rather than sourced, because the journals holding the measured flight numbers were unreachable; the stroke plane is horizontal, which removes by construction the drag-based weight support Fry et al. measured in real hovering flies; and the intact model recovers in 13 ms where a real fly takes under 60, so it is OPTIMISTIC about control and understates rather than overstates what deleting a haltere costs. The delay-versus-gain trade-off it reports is also not new - Elzinga, Dickson & Dickinson 2011 measured it on a dynamically scaled robotic fly - so this is a re-derivation whose value is that it is calibrated against an independently measured recovery time.

**Redistributing the SAME total wing area over four wings does not fly: 0.46 body weights against 1.00. - Nothing here was measured on an animal. Both halves are simulations and every figure caption says so.
- THIS MODEL IS FASTER THAN THE ANIMAL. The haltere-intact arm returns inside 2 degrees in about 13 ms, where Ristroph et al. measured a real fly at under 60 ms. The miss is in the direction that makes the model OPTIMISTIC about control, so every statement here about what deleting a haltere costs is an UNDERSTATEMENT rather than an exaggeration. Said plainly because a reader who sees a model agree with its author's conclusion should be told which way its errors point.
- THE CENTRAL RESULT IS NOT NEW, and pretending otherwise would be the easiest dishonesty available here. Elzinga, Dickson & Dickinson 2011 (PMID 22188766) measured the same thing on a dynamically scaled robotic fruit fly and reported 'a fundamental trade-off between sensor delay and permissible feedback gain', proposing exactly the architecture this study assumes - 'a haltere-mediated inner-loop proportional controller provides damping for slower visually mediated feedback'. Chang & Wang 2014 (PMID 25049376) derived bounds on sensing rate and delay in simulation and conjectured that flies sense every wingbeat. Our 3-wingbeat boundary is a re-derivation of a published trade-off in a different model, and its value is that it is calibrated against an independently measured recovery time, not that it is a discovery.
- The four-winged configurations barely move the ATTITUDE half: their recovery curves lie within 1% of the two-wing ones, because the added wing inertia is negligible at a 0.5% wing-mass fraction. That is a genuine null and it is reported as one. The four-winged fly's control problem is the deleted SENSOR, not added rotational inertia, and no inertia story should be told from this study.
- The stroke plane is horizontal and the angle of attack is constant through translation. Fry, Sayaman & Dickinson 2005 (PMID 15939772, abstract) report that hovering Drosophila instead use a U-shaped wing trajectory in which 'large drag forces during a downward plunge at the start of each stroke create peak vertical forces'. That mechanism cannot appear in this model. The model is therefore a comparison between configurations, not an estimate of a real fly's lift.
- Quasi-steady blade-element aerodynamics omits wake capture, added mass and rotational circulation. Fry et al. 2005 state that quasi-steady mechanisms 'could account for nearly all of the mean measured force required to hover' while 'temporal discrepancies ... indicate that unsteady mechanisms also play a significant role'. We use the mean, which is the part they say is covered.
- Most parameters are assumed rather than sourced, including body mass, wing length, chord, wingbeat frequency and stroke amplitude. We could not read the full text of the papers that hold measured values: the Journal of Experimental Biology site returns a bot challenge, and Europe PMC has no full text for Fry et al. 2005, Ristroph et al. 2010, Dickerson et al. 2019 or Yarger et al. 2021 (checked; a positive control on Deora et al. 2021, PMC8629423, returned 164 kB of full text from the same endpoint, and Paul et al. 2021, PMC8129201, returned 111 kB, so the endpoint works and the four absences are real rather than a broken query). Every part (A) result is therefore reported as a RATIO, in which the absolute scale cancels.
- The interference term between fore- and hindwings is swept rather than set, because the literature disagrees about its SIGN. Usherwood & Lehmann 2008 (PMID 18477538) report that dragonfly fore-hind phasing 'can improve aerodynamic efficiency by removing swirl'. We did not pick a value: the conclusion that the un-actuated four-wing configuration does not fly holds ACROSS THE WHOLE ADMISSIBLE RANGE of that parameter, from 0 to +0.40, and fails only if the undriven pair actively HELPS the driven pair - which no mechanism we found proposes for an undriven obstacle in a wake.
- Splitting one wing into two smaller ones at constant total area is done here at constant ASPECT RATIO and constant wingbeat frequency. Both are choices. Deora, Sane & Sane 2021 (PMID 34783648, full text) show wingbeat frequency is set by wing-thorax resonance and rises 'by as much as 90 Hz' when wings are clipped, so a real four-winged animal would not hold frequency fixed. Letting it rise would recover part of the lost lift and cost more power; we did not model it.
- The attitude model is one axis and linear apart from the saturation. Real recovery couples roll, pitch and yaw, and Fry, Sayaman & Dickinson 2003 (PMID 12702878) report that 'inertia, not friction, dominates the flight dynamics of insects'.
- The visual loop is a delay and a gain, not a model of vision. Rauscher & Fox 2021 (PMID 33499788) show haltere and visual inputs sum LINEARLY for wing motor output but not for gaze; we model only the wing-motor side.
- The haltere does more than sense rotation. Dickerson et al. 2019 (PMID 31607538) argue that it also 'function[s] as an adjustable clock to set the spike timing of wing motor neurons'. A PD loop does not represent that at all, so deleting it here UNDERSTATES what deleting a haltere costs.
- Recovery time to a fixed 2 degree band depends on how large the disturbance was. Ristroph et al. 2010 describe 'gentle disturbances' without a magnitude in the abstract, so the perturbation is swept and the whole curve is reported rather than one matched number.
- The un-actuated wing arm models a LIMIT. Fernandes et al. 1994 report 'only rudimentary indirect flight muscles' in the transformed segment, not none; a rudimentary muscle is somewhere between our driven and un-actuated arms and nobody has published where.

## The chain across scales, and where it is not calibrated

From a Hox dose to a body in the air. One junction is a model whose held-out prediction lands, one is a published phenotype, one is refused, and one is a null.

1. **Hox dose → Appendage identity** — *model*. The best-supported junction here. Calibrated on the Ubx arm alone, the model predicts the independently measured Antennapedia threshold to within 4.0%, against 39.2% for the switch-like variant — and both variants pass the same controls, so it is a fair comparison rather than a strawman. Stronger still, a test that needs no model: the two published thresholds sit on opposite branches of the switch, and their ordering is the one a hysteretic switch forbids.
2. **Appendage identity → A wing with an engine** — *refused*. Refused, and this is the design space's central finding. Epidermal identity follows the lesion; mesodermal identity does not, so the transformed segment gets a wing and no indirect flight muscle. The two are controlled separately and nobody has transformed both. Until someone does, 'four-winged fly' names an animal with two wings and two ornaments.
3. **Wing number and area → Lift and power** — *model*. Inside the blade-element model, and the part of it that matters is geometric rather than biological: lift goes with the second moment of wing area. Same total area over four wings gives 0.46 body weights; double area gives 1.83 at twice the power. 18 of its 24 parameters are assumed, so the ratios are more trustworthy than the absolute numbers, and the model is calibrated so that wild type supports exactly body weight.
4. **Haltere loss → Loss of control** — *model*. A delay bound rather than a gain comparison: no loop slower than about three wingbeats meets the measured recovery at any gain in the declared family. The model recovers in 13 ms where the real fly takes under 60, so it is OPTIMISTIC about control and therefore understates the cost of deleting the sensor.
5. **Added wing inertia → Loss of control** — *refused*. A genuine null, reported because it is the story one would otherwise tell. Four-wing recovery curves sit within 1% of two-wing ones at a 0.5% wing-mass fraction. The four-winged fly's control problem is the deleted sensor, not the added rotational inertia.

> The end-to-end statement — this allele gives that flight performance — is refused, because the animal it describes has never been flown. What the chain does deliver is an ordering of the obstacles: the missing engine comes first, the missing sensor second, and the added inertia not at all.

## Welfare and the invertebrate question

**Framing.**

Three of the routes on this page are procedures on living animals that a reviewer will want addressed, and one of them - implanting stimulators into free-flying beetles - is the page's most ethically loaded item precisely because it works. This section is here because the absence of it would be a hole, not as an apology.

**Invertebrate status.**

Insects are outside the scope of most national animal-research legislation, including EU Directive 2010/63/EU, which covers live non-human vertebrates and cephalopods. That is a statement about the law and NOT an argument that the procedures are ethically free; we did not find, and did not look hard for, a governing framework for insect welfare, and we are not qualified to supply one.

**The three that need it.**

- Haltere ablation and campaniform ablation are irreversible sensory deletions on an animal that then cannot fly freely. The loading route (sen-haltere-loading) achieves a comparable manipulation REVERSIBLY, with a demonstrated recovery, and should be preferred wherever it answers the question.
- Implanted stimulators (neu-implanted-stimulator-beetle, neu-honeybee-steering) are surgery followed by imposed behaviour. These are the routes with the clearest dual-use profile on the page, and the page names that rather than leaving it implicit.
- Wing clipping and hindwing removal (comp-lepidoptera-hindwing, act-thoracic-linkage) are irreversible and the lepidopteran paper argues explicitly that the loss is lethal in the wild even where it is survivable in a cage.

**Refinements available.**

The polyphenism routes (org-aphid-polyphenism, org-ant-polyphenism) answer wing-number questions with NO procedure at all - the animal makes both morphs itself. Where a question can be asked there, it should be.

**Dual use.**

The neurally actuated insect work is funded and published as an engineering programme, and the page presents it as the one route that has already produced a steerable flying animal. We state that plainly because omitting it would misrepresent where the field's capability actually is, and we do not provide operational detail beyond what the cited abstracts state.

## What this page does not claim

- No fly was made, no animal was used, and no wet-lab experiment was run for this page.
- There is no published measurement of free-flight performance in a four-winged Drosophila. The conclusion assembles two separately published phenotypes plus our own model.
- This page does not claim that four wings are worse than two. Odonata and the hindwing-ablation literature refute that as a general statement; the claim here is specific to Diptera, where the hindwing has already been spent on a sensor.
- The venation study fails two of its four declared controls, including the one added to ask whether its readout had converged. Part of its quoted seed spread is convergence rather than pattern formation, and the runs were not lengthened until it passed.
- 18 of the flight model's 24 parameters are assumed rather than sourced. Its ratios are more trustworthy than its absolute numbers.
- A threshold in a model is not a threshold in an animal.
- We ran no experiments on any animal. Every route on this page is a reading of someone else's published work, or an explicitly labelled proposal. The three studies are simulations and say so in every caption.
- THE CENTRAL GAP, stated in full under `not_claimed`: we found no published measurement of free-flight performance in a four-winged Drosophila, and no free-flight haltere-ablation measurement either. The flat claim 'haltere input is necessary for free flight' appears in this literature as an opening PREMISE - in at least two papers - rather than as a result, and we do not cite it as evidence. So the page's two reasons are not equally well evidenced: the missing flight muscle is direct and quoted; the sensory cost is real but graded, task-dependent and clade-dependent.
- Read levels are counted, not claimed. One source was read at full text (Deora, Sane & Sane 2021, via Europe PMC). The rest are abstracts or bare PubMed listings, and every evidence row says which. A row marked `listing` carries an explicit NOT READ note instead of a finding.
- We could not reach the full text of the journals that hold most of the quantitative flight literature. The Journal of Experimental Biology returns a bot challenge; Europe PMC has no full text for Fry et al. 2005, Ristroph et al. 2010, Dickerson et al. 2019 or Yarger et al. 2021. - Every PMID on this page was resolved against NCBI during this session and every citation string was ASSEMBLED from the record NCBI returned, not typed. The verification run also included a deliberate negative control, PMID 99999999, which resolved to MISSING as it should; a verifier that returns a record for everything is indistinguishable from one that is not running.
- One cited source is a PREPRINT and is flagged in its own row: Teoh et al. 2025 on the tp1 steering muscle is a bioRxiv record and has not been peer reviewed.
- The page preserves at least three live disagreements rather than reconciling them: whether haltere input is necessary for flight in all flies or only in Calyptratae; whether fore-hind wing interaction helps or hurts, which differs in sign between the dragonfly and the general flapping literature; and whether strepsipteran haltere-like organs are homologous to dipteran halteres at all. Where two sources disagree, both are cited and neither is averaged.
- The scale assignment of a route is an editorial choice, not a property of the biology. Several routes act at more than one scale - the Ubx-dally-Dpp route is simultaneously cis-regulatory and a network perturbation - and are filed where they are most useful to a reader, not where they are most correct.
- Nothing on this page has been attempted by us. The routes marked `proposed` have, as far as our search found, not been attempted by anyone; that is a statement about what we searched, which was PubMed, and not a survey of the field.

## What this page is for
