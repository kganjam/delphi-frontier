# S20 — Ecology: would flight pay for a zebrafish?

Project: `zebrafish-flight`. Scale: ecology. Status: complete. The answer is a precisely stated
**unknown**: the cost side is cheap, and the benefit side has no published support.

## 1. The question, stated precisely

Flight (here: any voluntary aerial phase longer than a startle splash, from a ballistic leap
up to a sustained glide) "pays" if the expected fitness benefit per launch is greater than its
expected cost:

    P(death | attacked, no launch) − P(death | attacked, launch)  >  cost_energy + cost_stranding + cost_aerial-exposure

Four facts make this hard for *Danio rerio*:

* **Size.** A 4 cm, ~0.4 g fish. Exocoetids begin to fly at about 50 mm (Davenport 1994,
  abstract via search-summary). An adult zebrafish is under that threshold.
* **Habitat.** Shallow, low-flow floodplain water, often with vegetated margins, rice paddies,
  and pools with about 30 cm visibility (§2). There is little open surface to fly over and
  plenty of emergent clutter to hit.
* **Predator field.** It is dominated by aquatic ambush and benthic predators, plus **wading
  and diving birds, which attack from the air side of the surface** (§2). The flying-fish case
  rests on fast open-water pursuit predators (dolphinfish, squid). A zebrafish's pond is not
  that environment.
* **No predation rates.** The impact of predation on wild zebrafish survival has not been
  measured (§2). The benefit term has no measured inputs.

**Disagreements and tensions I am keeping as they are:**

* **Open vs vegetated habitat.** M2 (Bangladesh) stresses *open, fairly clear* water with
  vegetated edges and full use of the water column. M1 (NE India) stresses *well-vegetated,
  silt-bottomed* pools and paddies. M4 stresses low-flow sandy channels. These are different
  populations and surveys, so I have not merged them into one "typical" habitat. The
  difference matters here, because open water is the only setting where an aerial
  trajectory is unobstructed.
* **Predator lists are inferred, not measured.** M5 is based on co-occurrence and plausibility.
  M6, from the same author, says predation impact is entirely unknown. The two are consistent,
  but the list should not be read as a measured predation spectrum.
* **M10's units** are self-inconsistent as summarised: a mass-specific rate scaling with an
  exponent close to +1 would mean mass-specific SMR rises with size. I carry both readings
  forward (C2) instead of picking one.

## 3. What I computed

Everything in this section is **computation**. It contains no observations.

**C1. Ballistic launch energy.** Assumptions: mass m = 4×10⁻⁴ kg; launch at 45° (maximum range);
no air drag (air drag only increases the cost); g = 9.81 m s⁻². Range d = v²/g, so v = √(g·d).
Kinetic energy at exit: KE = ½·m·v².

| Aerial range d | Exit speed v | KE at exit | Time aloft | Apex height |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.10 m (the stranded-jump scale, M8) | 0.99 m s⁻¹ | 1.96×10⁻⁴ J | 0.14 s | 2.5 cm |
| 0.30 m | 1.72 m s⁻¹ | 5.89×10⁻⁴ J | 0.25 s | 7.5 cm |
| 1.0 m (a short glide-scale escape) | 3.13 m s⁻¹ | 1.96×10⁻³ J | 0.45 s | 25 cm |

To turn KE into metabolic cost I assume an overall efficiency of 0.10 (muscle about 0.2 ×
hydrodynamic/launch about 0.5). Both factors are **assumed, not measured** for *Danio*. On that
assumption, metabolic cost per launch is about **2×10⁻³ J (0.1 m) to 2×10⁻² J (1 m)**. The
anaerobic-recovery overhead is not included; it is unknown for *Danio*.

**C2. Daily energy budget.** I use M10 with M = 0.4 and an oxycalorific coefficient of
13.56 J mg⁻¹ O2. That coefficient is a textbook value I recalled; I did not re-check it in this
session.

* Reading A, where R_S is whole-animal mg O2 h⁻¹ with M in g:
  0.273 × 0.4^0.965 = 0.113 mg O2 h⁻¹, which is 2.71 mg O2 d⁻¹, or **≈ 37 J d⁻¹**.
* Reading B, where R_S is mass-specific: 0.113 mg O2 g⁻¹ h⁻¹ × 0.4 g = 0.045 mg O2 h⁻¹, or
  **≈ 15 J d⁻¹**.

These are standard (resting) rates. The routine field budget is higher, so the fractions below
are upper bounds on the cost share.

**C3. Cost per launch as a fraction of daily SMR.**

* 0.1 m leap: 2×10⁻³ J ÷ (15 to 37 J) = **5×10⁻⁵ to 1.3×10⁻⁴**.
* 1 m launch: 2×10⁻² J ÷ (15 to 37 J) = **5×10⁻⁴ to 1.3×10⁻³**.
* Even 10 one-metre launches a day cost at most about 1.3 % of SMR.

Conclusion of the computation: **energy is not what limits this.** Doubling or halving any
assumption leaves the result unchanged. A 10× error in efficiency still leaves 1 m launches
below about 1.3 % of daily SMR each.

**C4. Why a 4 cm leaper is not yet a glider.** This is a computation and a pointer to the
aerodynamic subproblems; it is not an ecological result. Gliding at the C1 exit speed of
3.1 m s⁻¹ with lift coefficient C_L ≈ 1 needs lifting area S = 2mg/(ρ·C_L·v²) =
2 × 3.92×10⁻³ N / (1.2 × 1 × 9.8) ≈ **6.7 cm²**. That is roughly the planform of a 4 cm body
with fins twice its width. Below that area, any "flight" is the ballistic leap of C1, and the
range is set by exit speed, not by wings. Adult *Danio* aquatic fast-start peak speed is
**unknown**: I searched and found only larval data.

**C5. The benefit term has no inputs.** The benefit is

    B = (attack rate) × [P(capture | attacked, no launch) − P(capture | attacked, launch)].

No published attack rate, capture probability, or predator identity frequency exists for wild
*D. rerio* (M6). B cannot be computed, and I have not invented values for it. Qualitatively,
the signs are mixed:

* **Against benefit:** the bird predators listed in M5, M12 and M13 (kingfishers, herons,
  egrets) sit above the water and strike from the air. An aerial trajectory moves the fish
  toward that predator field and makes it visible against the sky. In shallow, cluttered
  margins a launch risks hitting vegetation or landing out of water. M8 shows *Danio* can
  flip back on land, but the stranding cost in the wild is unmeasured. The best-studied
  wild antipredator response is shoaling and refuge use (M7), not leaving the water.
* **For possible benefit:** *Xenentodon* (a surface-oriented needlefish) and *Barilius* (a
  fast, open-water cyprinid) are listed potential predators (M5). They are the closest
  analogue in this assemblage to the open-water pursuers that Davenport (M11) invokes for
  exocoetids. Against such a pursuer, breaking the surface could break pursuit. This is my
  inference. No source states it for *Danio*.

## 4. The answer

**Evidence level: `unknown`**. The cost side is `derived`; the benefit side is `unknown`.

* **Cost (derived, C1 to C3):** a launch covering 0.1 to 1 m of air costs about 10⁻⁴ to 10⁻³ of
  daily standard metabolism. Energetically, flight would be almost free for a zebrafish.
* **Benefit (unknown, literature):** no published measurement establishes that aerial escape
  would reduce predation on *D. rerio* or any danionin. The only direct statement on wild
  predation (M6) says its impact is entirely unknown. The predators named most concretely
  include birds that hunt from above the surface, for which an aerial phase is plausibly
  worse than staying submerged. This last point is my inference, not a measurement.
* **Refutation test:** I found **no published evidence of aerial predator escape in any
  danionin**. Scope: about 12 web searches (standard and one extended), on 2026-11-04, using
  *Danio*, *Devario*, *Esomus*, *Laubuka*, "danionin", "flying barb" × jump / leap / glide /
  aerial / escape / predator. I had no bibliographic-database access (Web of Science, Scopus,
  Zoological Record) and could not open any full text. The closest leads are hobbyist
  reports of jumping in *Esomus* (M14) and an unverified 2026 *Esomus* "aerial vision" paper
  (M15). Neither shows predator escape. This is a negative search of limited scope, not
  proof of absence.

In one line: **flight would cost a zebrafish almost nothing in energy, but the benefit it
would need to pay for has never been shown and probably is not there.** Its habitat and
predator field contain none of the ingredients behind the exocoetid case (open surface,
size ≥ 50 mm, fast open-water pursuers), and include bird predators that make the air
dangerous.

## 5. What this does NOT establish

* It does **not** show that flight would be *harmful* to a zebrafish. The bird-exposure and
  stranding arguments are qualitative inference with no measured rates.
* It does **not** show that danionins never use aerial escape. The negative search ran over
  web-search summaries only, with no database or full-text access. *Esomus* (the "flying
  barb", with greatly enlarged pectorals) and *Laubuka* (Indian hatchetfish) are the
  obvious danionins to examine in the primary literature, and I could not do that. M15 in
  particular needs to be resolved.
* It does **not** use measured adult *Danio* launch kinematics. The exit speeds are
  ballistic requirements, not measured abilities. Adult fast-start peak speed is unknown to
  me, so I have not shown that a zebrafish *can* reach 3 m s⁻¹ at the surface.
* The efficiency (0.10) is an assumption. Anaerobic recovery cost, the cost of repeated
  launches, and the cost of maintaining or carrying enlarged fins or wings (drag in water,
  growth, reduced manoeuvrability) are **not included**. The carrying cost of wing-like fins
  is plausibly much larger than the per-launch cost, and I did not compute it. It belongs to
  the morphology and hydrodynamics subproblems.
* The SMR figure rests on an allometric equation whose units are ambiguous in the summary I
  saw (M10). I carried both readings; the conclusion holds under either.
* No predator diet data for wild *D. rerio* were found. M12 concerns *D./Devario aequipinnatus*
  in Kerala egrets; I have not shown it holds for *D. rerio*.
* Habitat descriptions differ between surveys (M1, M2, M4), and the Arunachalam et al. 2013
  record carries an Expression of Concern I could not read. "Zebrafish habitat" is not one
  thing. Open floodplain lakes (M2) are more favourable to aerial escape than vegetated paddies
  (M1).
* Nothing here addresses larvae or juveniles, whose predators (e.g. odonate larvae, M5) and
  scale differ.
* It does **not** address selection pressures other than predation, such as dispersal between
  isolated monsoon pools, which might favour leaping. Davenport (M11) treats migration as a
  minor hypothesis for exocoetids. For floodplain *Danio* it is unexamined, and could be a more
  plausible driver than predation. That is an open hypothesis, not a finding.

## 6. What would change the answer

1. **A published field or high-speed video record of a danionin leaving the water in response
   to a predator strike**, especially by *Xenentodon* or *Barilius*. This would refute the
   negative search and turn the benefit from unknown to at least `literature`. The first
   targets are *Esomus* and *Laubuka* in the primary literature, and the M15 paper if it exists.
2. **Measured predation rates and predator identity for wild *D. rerio***: gut-content or
   eDNA diet studies of *Channa*, *Xenentodon*, *Barilius* and wading birds at known zebrafish
   sites. If attack rates are dominated by sub-surface pursuers, the sign of C5 moves toward
   benefit. If birds dominate, it moves further against.
3. **A capture-probability experiment**: wild *D. rerio* against *Xenentodon* in a mesocosm,
   with and without the option to clear the surface (for example a floating net strip versus
   open water). This is the single measurement that would give B a number.
4. **Measured adult *Danio* surface-exit speed and launch efficiency.** This would replace the
   assumed 0.10 and confirm whether 1 m launches are within reach.
5. **Design change:** carrying the design into an open-water lineage or habitat (a
   pelagic/lacustrine danionin with fast pursuit predators and no bird-dominated predator field),
   or scaling body length above about 50 mm, would recreate the conditions under which the
   exocoetid predator-escape argument (M11) applies. Without that, the ecology gives the wings
   no job.
