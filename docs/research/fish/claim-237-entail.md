# Claim 237: what it requires at the organ and behaviour scales, and whether that holds

**Source claim (organism scale, evidence: derived):** Exocoetid flight is a
multi-stroke task, not the single-impulse task S28 and S29 both modelled.
One launch with one impulse does not describe how real flying fish take off.

**Adjacent scales checked:** behaviour (above) and organ (below).

**Search scope for this note (applies to every negative below).** Web search,
2026-11-04, using these queries: flying-fish taxiing tail-beat frequency and
takeoff speed; Davenport 1994; Edgerton & Breder 1941; exocoetid red/white muscle
contraction kinetics and twitch time; Wardle 1975 twitch-time limit. Some hosts
could not be reached from this session (DNS or proxy refusal): cambridge.org,
biodiversitylibrary.org, archive.org, core.ac.uk and meetings-archive.aps.org.
nature.com redirected to a login. Primary sources that only those hosts serve
were therefore not read. I did not search Russian-language literature (Aleyev,
Shoulejkin) or pre-1950 natural-history sources directly.

## C1 (behaviour, existence): a repeated surface-thrust bout must exist

**Statement.** Between leaving the water and the airborne glide, and again
between glides, there must be a behaviour in which part of the body stays in the
water and keeps producing thrust. If no such bout existed, the single-impulse
framing would be correct.

**Status: satisfied** (literature; a secondary citation read in full text).

- F90 (full text) says that *Cypselurus* uses a "taxiing glide" for take-off
  (citing Breder 1929; Hubbs 1933, 1936; Hertel 1966). It says that "the
  elongated hypocaudal lobe of the caudal fin continues to remain in the water
  and generate thrust."
- F90 also says: "Total flight distance is extended by successive glides …
  At the end of a single flight, the flying fish will lower its hypocaudal lobe
  into the water and accelerate the body … Successive glides, totalling as many
  as 12" (citing Hankin 1920; Breder 1929; Aleyev 1977).
- EB41 shows the taxi stage in photographs (listing level only). D19 cites
  Hertel 1966 for "about 50 beats s⁻¹ … in warmer waters".

**Disagreement to keep.** F90 says that *Exocoetus* starts flight by
"emergence from the water at an angle up to 45°", and links it with "fish that
use a short taxi" (citing Shadbolt 1908; Hubbs 1933; Edgerton & Breder 1941;
Hertel 1966). So the claim's phrase "how real fish gliders actually take off"
holds clearly for the *Cypselurus* design. It holds less clearly for the
two-winged *Exocoetus* design. **My inference:** the claim should be scoped by
genus or by wing design, not stated for all of Exocoetidae. Even an
*Exocoetus* launch is preceded by many underwater tail beats. Whether that
counts as "multi-stroke" in the claim's sense depends on the S28/S29 model
boundary. I could not inspect those models from this repository.

**Gap.** F90 gives the *maximum* number of glides (12), not how often flights
have more than one glide. I found no figure for that proportion.

## C2 (behaviour, range): the surface phase must add a non-negligible share of takeoff speed

**Statement.** The speed gained during the surface-thrust phase must be a
large enough share of takeoff speed that a single impulse cannot stand in for
it.

- Quantity: Δv_taxi / v_takeoff. Bounds: lo = 0.2, hi = 1.0 (dimensionless).
- **The 0.2 threshold is my assumption, not a sourced value.** Below about 20%,
  a single-impulse model would be a fair approximation.

**Status: satisfied** (literature; secondary citation).

- F90 (full text) says that the taxi increases "speed for take-off from 10 to
  16–20 m/s" (citing Shoulejkin 1929; Mills 1936a; Edgerton & Breder 1941;
  Franzisket 1965; Aleyev 1977). It also says "The average taxi is 9 m"
  (Hubbs 1933).
- Δv / v_takeoff = (16 − 10)/16 to (20 − 10)/20 = **0.375–0.50**. This is
  arithmetic on cited values, not a measurement of my own.
- Caveat: F90 does not say how each cited author measured speed, or for which
  species. These 1929–1977 figures have not been checked by modern tracking in
  anything I read. D19 cites a "peak speed of about 15–20 m s⁻¹" from Dv94,
  which is consistent with F90 but probably draws on the same old sources.

## C3 (organ, range): a caudal lobe must stay submerged while the body is airborne

**Statement.** The tail must be asymmetric, with a ventral lobe large enough to
stay in the water and produce thrust while the trunk is in the air.

- Quantity: the hypocaudal lobe's share of caudal-fin area. Bounds: lo = 0.5,
  hi = 1.0 (fraction). The 0.5 bound is a generic "ventral lobe dominant"
  threshold, which I chose.

**Status: satisfied** (measured).

- F90 (full text, Table IV) gives genus means of 60.61–63.98% across six genera
  (*Cypselurus* 62.63, *Danichthyes* 60.61, *Exocoetus* 63.89, *Fodiator*
  63.95, *Hirundichthyes* 63.98, *Parexocoetus* 63.98). The pooled value is
  63.1 ± 0.5%, and genera do not differ significantly.
- Note: *Exocoetus*, the short-taxi genus from C1, has the same lobe share as
  the others. **My inference:** the organ does not track the behavioural
  difference between genera. The morphology alone therefore cannot tell taxiing
  genera from non-taxiing ones.

## C4 (organ, timescale): axial muscle must cycle as fast as the taxiing tail beat

**Statement.** The axial muscle driving the tail must finish a contraction
within one half-cycle of the taxiing tail beat. This uses the Wardle-type
relation, a model: maximum frequency ≈ 1/(2·t_c), where t_c is twitch
contraction time. So t_c must be at most 1/(2f).

- Quantity: twitch contraction time t_c of the caudal axial muscle.
  Bounds: lo = null, hi = 7–37 ms. The ceiling depends on which observed
  frequency is correct. I give the strict end, 10 ms, in the JSON for the
  50 Hz case.

**The frequency itself is disputed. I keep the two values separate:**

- 50–70 strokes s⁻¹: Dv94 (as quoted by D19). Hertel 1966 (cited by D19) gives
  "about 50 beats s⁻¹". This implies t_c ≤ 7–10 ms.
- 13.5 Hz: W24, from experiments on tail slapping at the surface
  (search-summary only). This implies t_c ≤ about 37 ms.
- D19's CFD gives its lowest taxiing power at 50 Hz. This is a **simulated**
  result. It does not confirm that 50 Hz is the observed frequency.

**Status: unsatisfied — nobody has looked, within my search scope.**

- I found **no measurement of muscle contraction time in any exocoetid**. I
  searched for exocoetid red/white muscle kinetics and twitch time. The only
  exocoetid muscle item found was Dv92 (search-summary). It says red axial
  muscle is comparable to that of cruising mackerel, and that take-off
  acceleration probably relies on anaerobic white muscle. It gives no kinetics.
- The nearest measured values are for large tropical pelagic fish, from S16
  (full text via fetch), measured at 45% fork length. Dorado
  (*Coryphaena hippurus*, a flying-fish predator): 56.7 ± 10.3 ms, fork length
  0.69 m, 27.5 °C. Barracuda: 47.0 ms, 0.89 m. Little tunny: 48.3 ms, 0.77 m.
  Sailfish: 68.5 ms, 1.47 m.
- These do **not** make C4 unsatisfiable. Contraction time grows with body
  length (S16; Wa75 at listing level), and flying fish are about 0.25–0.3 m,
  2–5 times shorter. **My inference, not an observation:** the 13.5 Hz figure
  needs nothing unusual. The 50–70 Hz figure would need twitch times about 5×
  shorter than the dorado's. That would place exocoetid caudal muscle among the
  fastest fish locomotor muscle, so it is a sharp, cheap test of the higher
  frequency.
- **Implication for claim 237.** The claim survives under either frequency,
  because multi-stroke taxiing is still multi-stroke at 13.5 Hz. What changes is
  how many strokes there are. That number matters for any multi-stroke model
  that replaces S28/S29. At 13.5 Hz, a 9 m taxi (Hubbs 1933, via F90) at
  10–20 m/s lasts about 0.45–0.9 s, which is about 6–12 strokes. At 50–70 Hz the
  same taxi is about 22–63 strokes. This is arithmetic on cited values.

## C5 (organ, energetic): muscle power must cover the cost of taxiing

**Statement.** Mechanical power from the caudal musculature must be at least the
hydrodynamic power needed to taxi at about 10 m/s with only the lower lobe
submerged.

- Quantity: required mass-specific power per kg of body. Bounds: lo ≈ 190,
  hi = null (W per kg body mass).
- Derivation: D19's **CFD** minimum of 36 W for a 0.191 kg model fish at
  10 m/s, 50 Hz and a 15° deflection gives 36/0.191 ≈ 188 W/kg body. With D19's
  own assumption of 50% muscle by mass, that is about 380 W/kg muscle. This
  number is **simulated**, then divided by me. It is not measured.

**Status: open.**

- The demand side exists only as a simulation (D19, a preprint).
- The supply side, measured mechanical power output of exocoetid axial muscle,
  is **unknown**. My searches turned up no measurement. Muscle mass fraction is
  also unknown here; D19 assumes 50% and cites no exocoetid measurement for it.
- D19 compares its figures with "2300 W kg⁻¹" attributed to Gao & Techet 2011.
  I did not read Gao & Techet and cannot confirm what that number measures, so
  I do not use it.
- For context only (simulated): D19 puts **underwater** cruising at 10 m/s at
  350 W, about 3664 W/kg muscle. That is roughly 10× the taxiing cost.
  **D19's interpretation** is that taxiing exists because reaching takeoff speed
  fully submerged is beyond the muscle. If that holds, it supports claim 237
  from below. But it rests on CFD in an unrefereed preprint plus an assumed
  muscle fraction, so I do not mark C5 satisfied.

No constraint is unsatisfiable: no measured number contradicts claim 237 at
the organ or behaviour scale. The claim has two weak points.

1. **Genus scope.** It is well supported for *Cypselurus*-type four-winged fish
   and less so for *Exocoetus* (C1).
2. **Stroke count.** The number of strokes in a "multi-stroke" taxi is
   uncertain by about a factor of 4, because of the 13.5 vs 50–70 Hz
   disagreement (C4).

The cheapest next measurement is twitch contraction time in caudal axial muscle
from any exocoetid of about 0.25 m at about 27 °C. It would settle the frequency
dispute, and so the stroke count, without any simulation.
