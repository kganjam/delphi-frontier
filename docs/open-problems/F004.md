## Record 1: fisher-ftns

**(a) What the source says (Fisher 1930, ch. II).** Quotes are from the OCR. Σ and α are restored where the OCR reads 'Z' and 'a'.
- p. 30: average excess = difference in mean measurement between two "moieties", each one homozygote plus half the heterozygotes.
- p. 32: the "expected value X" is fixed by requiring Σ(x − X) = 0 in each moiety. Hence "the sum for the whole population of the product X(x − X) ... must be zero". This is a least-squares projection.
- p. 33: "the variance of X is shown to be W = Σ(pqaα) ... this quantity we may distinguish as the genetic variance". p. 34: "The genetic variance as here defined is only a portion of the variance determined genotypically".
- p. 34: the definitions are applied "to the special characteristic m which measures the relative rate of increase or decrease" (Malthusian parameter, defined p. 26).
- p. 35: "the rate of increase in fitness due to all changes in gene ratio is exactly equal to the genetic variance of fitness W". Theorem: "The rate of increase in fitness of any organism at any time is equal to its genetic variance in fitness at that time." Same page: gene ratios must be counted "weighted according to the reproductive value of each", and "the theorem is exact only for idealized populations, in which fortuitous fluctuations in genetic composition have been excluded".
- pp. 41-42: "Against the action of Natural Selection in constantly increasing the fitness ... is to be set off ... the deterioration of its inorganic and organic environment."
- Secondary, read: Frank 1998 eq. 2.10, Δ_f w̄ = Cov(w,g)/w̄ = V_g/w̄. The partial change is "obtained by holding constant the contribution of each predictor" (p. 21, citing Price 1972b and Ewens 1989). Ewens 1989 abstract: "correct as a mathematical statement, but of less biological value than was claimed by Fisher".

**(b) What the record says.** The statement says the rate of increase in mean fitness due to natural selection equals the additive genetic variance in fitness at that time. The math is `Δ_NS w̄ = V_A(w)/w̄` (discrete) and `dm̄/dt|_NS = V_A(m)` (Malthusian). The context says: partial change holds average effects and environment fixed (Price 1972, Ewens 1989); "Formal status: proved in this partial-change reading"; false for the total change under dominance, epistasis, frequency dependence or a changing environment.

## Record 2: hamilton-rule

**(a) What the sources say.** Hamilton 1964 was **not read**, so no claim is made about its text. Read:
- Frank 1998 p. 46: "Hamilton's rule states that a behavior increases in frequency when rB − C > 0". "Hamilton (1970, 1972) emphasized that the rule depends on several assumptions, including weak selection, additivity of costs and benefits of fitness components, and a special definition of relatedness that uses statistical correlations among individuals rather than genealogy".
- Frank 1998 pp. 47, 52-53: the covariance derivation is Hamilton 1970. The regression form, w = β_{wg·G} g + β_{wG·g} G + ε with −C = β_{wg·G}, B = β_{wG·g}, r = Cov(G,g)/Cov(g,g), is Queller 1992.
- Frank 1998 p. 54 eq. 3.6: "rB − C > −E(wΔg)/V_g ... an exact, total result". p. 24-25: "If we use the Fisherian definition of partial change ..., then the right side is zero and we recover the standard form of Hamilton's rule".
- GWW 2011 abstract: uses "Fisher's genetical paradigm to demonstrate the generality of Hamilton's rule".
- Nowak et al. 2010 main text: section "Hamilton's rule almost never holds". Inclusive fitness "is only another method of accounting ... there are no predictions that are specific to inclusive fitness theory". A keyword search found 0 hits for "regression" in the main text.

**(b) What the record says.** The statement says a social gene increases in frequency when rb exceeds c. The math is `Δp̄ > 0 ⟺ rb − c > 0` with regression r, b, c (Queller via Price). The context says: additive effects and weak selection in "Hamilton's original derivation"; b, c partial regressions "on the actor's and the partner's genotype"; "proved; exact in the regression form (GWW 2011)"; critics (Nowak 2010) call it "close to an identity". The dct:source is Hamilton 1964 only.
