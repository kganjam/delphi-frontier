Introduction Combining high maneuverability with high cruise efficiency remains a central challenge in aircraft design, as these two objectives often impose conflicting aerodynamic and system-level requirements. High maneuverability typically necessitates low wing loading and a large tail volume to enhance load-factor capability and control authority. However, these features inevitably increase parasitic drag, which substantially reduces cruise efficiency 1 , 2 . This trade-off becomes even more pronounced for avian-inspired flapping-wing robots. On one hand, flight in complex environments requires bird-like agility and maneuvering capability; on the other hand, due to constraints in scale, actuation power, and structural weight, any additional morphing mechanism or degree of actuation may significantly compromise system efficiency and reliability. Consequently, enhancing both maneuvering performance and cruise efficiency without substantially increasing structural complexity remains a key challenge that must be addressed for the practical deployment of avian-inspired flapping-wing robots. Birds offer important biological inspiration for resolving this trade-off. The flight capability of birds, particularly their ability to efficiently transition between different flight states, primarily relies on the dynamic modulation of wing and tail morphologies 3 – 5 . During high-speed cruising, birds typically tuck their tails, maintaining a tail volume coefficient of approximately 0.3 to minimize aerodynamic drag 6 . In contrast, when performing maneuvering tasks such as landing or low-speed flight, they actively extend their wings and tails, with the tail further deflecting up or down to rapidly adjust aerodynamic forces and moments 7 – 9 (Fig. 1b ). For instance, before landing, raptors pitch their extended tails upward (providing negative lift) to generate a nose-up moment. As flight speed decreases, both wings and tails gradually pitch downward (providing positive lift) until touchdown is completed 10 , 11 . Variations in tail pitch angle not only provide the necessary pitching moment and additional lift 12 , 13 , but the extension and tucking of the tail also amplify the effect of angle-of-attack modulation by altering the effective tail surface area. This morphological flexibility constitutes a key mechanism enabling raptors to sustain efficient flight across a broad range of flight speeds 5 . Inspired by these biological principles, avian-inspired aerial vehicles and small drones have begun to integrate wing-tail morphing structures to expand their flight envelope and enhance control performance. Studies on bio-inspired fixed-wing platforms have confirmed that introducing avian-inspired wing and tail morphing capabilities not only effectively extends the flight speed envelope 12 , 14 , but also significantly enhances agility by utilizing planform area changes for pitch control. Furthermore, leveraging avian asymmetric wing tucking and incidence angle adjustment mechanisms can substantially improve roll maneuverability 15 – 18 . These findings suggest that the wing-tail morphing mechanisms observed in avian flight introduce aerodynamic and structural considerations that differ from those of conventional fixed-wing robots, providing insights for the design of micro avian-inspired flapping-wing robots. However, it is important to note that avian flight systems possess high kinematic redundancy—pitch control is often achieved through a coupled combination of wing sweep, twist, and camber variation, alongside tail pitching and morphing 7 , 19 . For artificial flapping-wing robots, attempting to fully replicate such complex multi-degree-of-freedom wing-tail morphing mechanisms would drastically increase mechanical complexity and weight, while imposing significant control challenges. More importantly, lift and thrust generation in flapping wings heavily relies on the passive deformation of flexible wing surfaces, which is highly sensitive to stiffness distribution, inertia distribution, and aeroelastic response. Directly transplanting complex wing morphing technologies into flapping wing systems may disrupt the inherent passive deformation mechanism and substantially degrade system efficiency and reliability. This limitation is a major reason why only a limited number of flapping-wing robots currently utilize active wing morphing technologies 15 . Compared to directly altering the area or surface morphology of the flapping wings, tail extension and tucking provide a more practical approach for modulating the lifting surface area. Systematic ground-based experiments on avian-inspired morphing tail models have demonstrated that both tail morphing and overall tail deflection can generate pitch control moments. Among these methods, tail morphing incurs lower drag and energy consumption, making it a more efficient control mechanism 19 . Furthermore, variations in tail area not only modify tail volume and enhance longitudinal control effectiveness but can also influence the effective lifting area and load-factor capability under specific flight conditions, indicating its potential for wing-loading regulation. These considerations suggest that utilizing tail area modulation as the primary morphing degree of freedom, while avoiding changes in flapping wing area, could facilitate simultaneous improvements in cruise efficiency and maneuverability without significantly increasing the structural complexity of the flapping wings. Complementary to tail-area modulation, flapping-wing incidence-angle control provides a low-complexity means of regulating wing aerodynamic loading without directly altering the area or surface morphology of the flapping wings. Inspired by the pitch-twist modulation of avian wings, independent adjustment of the left and right wing incidence angles can generate differential roll moments, while symmetric adjustment provides auxiliary regulation of lift and pitching moment. Importantly, incidence-angle modulation preserves the intrinsic passive aeroelastic deformation of the flexible flapping wings, thereby avoiding the efficiency and reliability penalties associated with more complex active wing-morphing mechanisms. Taken together, tail-area modulation and flapping-wing incidence-angle control provide two functionally complementary, low-degree-of-freedom variables: the former regulates pitching moment, tail volume, and effective lifting area, whereas the latter provides complementary lift and moment control. Rather than pursuing a full replication of the highly redundant multi-degree-of-freedom wing-tail morphing observed in birds, such a functional decomposition shifts the design philosophy from biological imitation toward the engineering reconstruction of key aerodynamic functions, providing a practical basis for balancing cruise efficiency and maneuvering capability without excessive structural and control complexity. In this work, we propose and validate a model-guided, low-degree-of-freedom wing-tail coordinated design framework and implement it in an avian-inspired micro flapping-wing robot, NPU-Sparrow. The framework adopts a control configuration that combines dynamic tail-area adjustment with flapping-wing incidence-angle control. The linkage-driven morphing tail replaces the conventional elevator and regulates pitching moment, tail volume, effective lifting area, and load-factor capability through tail-area modulation, while independently controlled left and right flapping-wing incidence angles provide complementary pitch and lift regulation as well as differential roll control (Fig. 1c, d ). Rather than directly replicating avian wing-tail kinematics, the framework extracts and reconstructs their principal aerodynamic functions as modelable and parametrically assessable engineering variables. A robot-specific predictive aerodynamic model, constructed from empirical response surfaces fitted to separate wing-body and tail wind-tunnel measurements, is used to guide the selection of the tail installation angle and wing-tail coordination strategy by mapping the trade-off between cruise efficiency and maneuvering capability. The resulting negative-lift tail configuration provides longitudinal static-stability margin during cruise and reverses to an auxiliary positive-lifting surface at high angles of attack (Fig. 1d ). Wind-tunnel experiments confirm the predicted wing-tail aerodynamic trends, while outdoor flight tests show a 22.5% expansion of the level-flight speed envelope relative to the baseline configuration, a peak roll rate of 375° s −1 , a 20.4% reduction in steady-turn radius, and successful loops and barrel rolls (Fig. 1a ). These results demonstrate that the proposed framework can reproduce key functional advantages of avian wing-tail coordination with only two wing-incidence servos and one tail-area servo, providing a low-complexity approach for balancing cruise efficiency, longitudinal stability, and maneuvering capability in flapping-wing robots. Results Cruise-maneuver trade-off For an aircraft with a constant takeoff weight, maneuvering capability is strongly influenced by the available lift, whereas cruise efficiency is primarily constrained by aerodynamic drag and the associated propulsive requirement. Balancing these objectives, therefore, requires reducing cruise drag while retaining sufficient lift capability for maneuvering. Inspired by the tail kinematic degrees of freedom observed in birds, variations in the area and installation angle of an avian-inspired morphing tail can simultaneously affect lift generation, pitching moment, and longitudinal static stability. We therefore investigated different combinations of tail area and installation angle to identify flight-capable design points that provide low aerodynamic drag during level flight while maintaining high available lift under maneuvering conditions. This parameter-space exploration establishes the cruise-maneuver trade-off enabled by the low-degree-of-freedom control configuration of NPU-Sparrow. Overall design and low-degree-of-freedom control configuration The basic dimensions of the NPU-Sparrow (Fig. 2a ) were determined with reference to medium-sized passerine birds. Passerines are characterized by their exceptional agility and long-distance migratory capabilities, making them suitable biological references for investigating the balance between cruise efficiency and maneuvering performance. Based on morphometric data from passerines, the NPU-Sparrow was designed with a takeoff weight of 90 g, a wingspan of 0.45 m, and a cruise speed of 7 m s −1 (Table 1 ). This speed approximates the theoretical minimum-power flight speed of similarly sized passerine birds, enabling the robot to be designed and validated under a cruise condition that is both biologically comparable and engineering feasible. By referring to general parameters of similar-scale artificial aircraft, the wing loading was established at 21 N m - ², and the aspect ratio was set at 5, with the remaining robot parameters derived from these initial conditions (Fig. 2 ). Using the cruise state as the design baseline, the flapping-wing system was refined through iterative wind tunnel tests. The flapping frequency of the robot was determined to be 8 Hz, while under maneuvering conditions, the maximum flapping frequency can increase to 16 Hz. The flapping wings are constructed from a carbon-fiber skeleton bonded to a polyurethane film, and their lift and thrust generation rely on the passive aeroelastic deformation of the flexible membrane wings during flapping. To mimic avian wing cross-sections, the two ribs proximal to the root feature the upper surface of a GOE225 airfoil, corresponding to the arm wing, thereby providing favorable root load-bearing capacity and shape retention. The remaining three ribs employ a flat-plate airfoil, corresponding to the hand wing, to reduce the mass of the outer wing while maintaining the flexible response of the flapping wing. Flapping motion is generated by a motor-driven crank-rocker mechanism through a reduction gearbox, with a stroke amplitude of 80°. To replicate the streamlined and lightweight body morphology of birds, the NPU-Sparrow employs a low-cost, maintenance-friendly design. The fuselage adopts a monocoque structure fabricated through 3D printing, with all electronic components and actuation systems integrated within this body shell. Morphing actuation mechanism Inspired by the biomimetic characteristics of avian feather structures, the tail feathers of the NPU-Sparrow were constructed using a composite design that incorporates foamed polyethylene sheets and carbon-fiber feather shafts, thereby fulfilling the requirements for a lightweight design (Fig. 2c ). Notably, the carbon-fiber shafts serve as kinematic linkages for the tail morphing drive mechanism (see “Methods”). Similar to the structure of a natural bird tail 20 , 21 , the central feather of the robot is fixed. However, to simplify the mechanical structure, the entire tail comprises only five feathers. The remaining four feathers are actuated by a servo motor through a symmetric linkage mechanism, enabling both tail extension and tucking (Fig. 2b, d ). Referring to the design standards of conventional fixed-wing aircraft 1 , the median tail area corresponds to a tail volume coefficient of 0.45. As the tail transitions from the fully tucked to the fully extended state, its area increases by 118.5% (see “Methods”). Birds actively twist their wings through muscle-driven shoulder joints 22 – 24 , where supination (external rotation, increasing incidence angle) and pronation (internal rotation, decreasing incidence angle) dynamically modulate the wing’s angle of attack (AoA) relative to the incoming flow, thereby enabling precise control of lift and roll moment 7 , 22 , 25 . Inspired by this biological mechanism, we designed an incidence angle control system for the flapping wings of NPU-Sparrow (Fig. 2e ). This system decouples incidence angle modulation from flapping motion kinematically by allowing relative rotation between the output rocker of the flapping mechanism and the fore spar of the wing. Additionally, axial freedom is effectively constrained by positioning pins on the fore spar. Finally, servos mounted on the fuselage drive the oblique beams of the left and right wings, enabling a rotation of ±5° around the leading edge (Fig. 2e )—a range determined by the robot’s agility requirements (see “Methods”). The model is a robot-specific empirical response-surface representation constructed from wind-tunnel measurements of NPU-Sparrow. It is intended for trend analysis and design-space screening within the experimentally sampled parameter domain, rather than for direct extrapolation or quantitative aerodynamic prediction across flapping-wing robots of different scales or configurations. In addition, because the reduced-order representation of the combined wing-tail aerodynamics does not fully resolve the nonlinear wing-tail interactions that become increasingly pronounced at high angles of attack, the quantitative accuracy of the model decreases in this regime. Within the sampled parameter range, variations in tail installation angle have only a negligible effect on the aerodynamic-center position, whereas increasing tail area causes the aerodynamic center to migrate rearward (Fig. 3b ). Unlike conventional fixed-wing incidence control, increasing the flapping-wing incidence angle also shifts the aerodynamic center rearward. Consequently, coordinating tail extension with flapping-wing supination amplifies the rearward migration, whereas coordinating tail extension with pronation suppresses it (Fig. 3b ). Unlike conventional elevator deflection, tail-area variation simultaneously modifies pitching moment, tail volume, aerodynamic-center position, and effective lifting area 8 . Because NPU-Sparrow has no independent tail-deflection degree of freedom, the tail installation angle therefore becomes a key design parameter governing the balance between longitudinal static stability and maneuvering load capability. Using the predictive model, these two metrics were evaluated over combinations of tail installation angle and tail area to construct the cruise-maneuver Pareto design map (Fig. 3c ). In the Pareto map, a conventional fixed-tail configuration corresponds to a single design point, whereas each trajectory at a fixed tail installation angle traces the redistribution of cruise and maneuvering performance as the tail area varies. Increasing the tail installation angle generally enhances load-factor capability; however, combinations of large installation angle and small tail area can produce a negative zero-lift pitching moment about the aerodynamic center, requiring the trimmed center of gravity to lie aft of the aerodynamic center and resulting in longitudinal static instability (Fig. 3c ). Consistent with the trend predicted by the model, the tail installation angle does not influence the position of the aerodynamic center. When the tail area is configured to the median state, the locations of the aerodynamic centers for the two tail configurations remain identical. The primary distinction between them lies in the pitching moment generated about the aerodynamic center. In the positive-lift tail configuration, the pitching moment about the aerodynamic center is negative (M AC &lt; 0) (Fig. 4c ), necessitating that the trimmed center of gravity be located aft of the aerodynamic center. Under this condition, the robot demonstrates longitudinal static instability. Conversely, the negative-lift tail configuration exhibits a positive zero-lift moment (M AC &gt; 0), requiring that the trimmed center of gravity be positioned ahead of the aerodynamic center; thus, the robot displays longitudinal static stability. As the tail of NPU-Sparrow extends, the aerodynamic center shifts significantly rearward in both tail configurations (Fig. 4d ). Specifically, the aerodynamic center shifts rearward by 17.3% of the mean aerodynamic chord in the positive-lift tail configuration and by 17.5% in the negative-lift tail configuration. Compared to the control logic based solely on tail extension and tucking, coupling tail extension with flapping wing pronation suppresses the migration of the aerodynamic center in both configurations. For instance, in the negative-lift tail configuration, the rearward shift of the aerodynamic center is reduced to 7.1% of the mean aerodynamic chord as the tail extends. Conversely, coupling tail extension with flapping wing supination amplifies the rearward migration of the aerodynamic center compared to tail extension and tucking alone. Taking the negative-lift tail configuration as an example again, the rearward shift increases to 34.1% of the mean aerodynamic chord as the tail extends. For a fixed moment of inertia, greater available aerodynamic moment corresponds to greater angular control authority. We therefore compared the pitching-moment coefficients generated by the six control strategies about the moment-equilibrium location defined at an angle of attack of 10° and the median tail area (Fig. 4e ). At 10°, the positive-lift configuration produces the largest pitching-moment variation when tail extension is coordinated with flapping-wing pronation, whereas the negative-lift configuration generally exhibits smaller moment variations. However, the positive-lift configuration shows unfavorable trim characteristics at both low and high angles of attack. For the negative-lift configuration, coordinating tail extension with supination increases pitch-control authority relative to tail morphing alone, whereas coordination with pronation results in control reversal near 10°, which is detrimental to the stable flight of the robot. Because the minimum turn radius decreases with increasing load factor at a given flight speed (see “Methods”), the load-factor capability of the two tail configurations was further compared using their measured lift characteristics. With the tail fixed at its median area, the configuration is equivalent to the conventional fixed-tail baseline used in this study. Wind-tunnel measurements show that the positive-lift tail generally provides greater lift at a given angle of attack but also incurs greater aerodynamic drag (Fig. 5b, c ). In both tail configurations, coordinating tail extension with flapping-wing supination further increases lift, although at the cost of increased drag. The negative-lift tail exhibits the state-dependent aerodynamic role predicted by the model. When controlled through tail morphing alone, its lift coefficient exceeds that of the baseline once the complete-robot angle of attack exceeds 13°, because the tail then generates positive lift in the same direction as the flapping wings (Fig. 5d ). Thus, the negative-lift configuration retains its cruise static-stability advantage while recovering substantial load-factor capability under high-angle-of-attack maneuvering conditions. Under lift-trimmed conditions at a fixed flight speed, the available excess thrust increases as the tail is tucked and its area decreases (Fig. 5e ), indicating that tail tucking can support either a higher trim speed or the same flight speed at a lower flapping frequency. This trend is consistent with the Pareto prediction that reducing tail area lowers the aerodynamic requirement for cruise. Taken together, the wind-tunnel results show that although the positive-lift tail provides relatively high load-factor and pitch-control capability, it remains longitudinally statically unstable over the relevant operating range. The negative-lift configuration instead maintains longitudinal static stability during cruise, while tail extension at angles of attack above 13° shifts the tail into the positive-lift regime and recovers high maneuvering load capability. Coordinating tail extension with flapping-wing supination further increases pitch-control authority and available lift at high angles of attack. These results support the central design logic identified by the Pareto analysis: tail-area modulation shifts the robot between low-drag cruise and high-load maneuvering states, while flapping-wing incidence provides complementary lift and pitching-moment regulation. Compared with the experimental results, the predictive aerodynamic quantitatively overpredicts the magnitude of aerodynamic-center migration, while correctly capturing its qualitative trend. This discrepancy reflects the limitations of the reduced-order aerodynamic representation of the wing-tail system, particularly its inability to explicitly resolve complex nonlinear wing-tail interactions, including variations in the local downwash gradient and effective dynamic pressure at the tail. Sensitivity analysis indicates that this quantitative overprediction produces only limited shifts in the Pareto trajectories and does not alter the overall cruise-maneuver trade-off, the selected wing-tail coordination strategy, or the relative performance ranking among different configurations (Supplementary Note 2 ). The corresponding aerodynamic trends and relative differences among the configurations are further supported by the full-vehicle wind-tunnel experiments. A human pilot issued maneuver commands, while a lightweight onboard autopilot implemented pitch and roll control using cascaded PID loops. The tests included steady turns, barrel rolls (Supplementary Movie 1 ), loops (Supplementary Movie 2 ), and horizontal acceleration (Fig. 6c ). Flight-state and control data were recorded onboard (see “Methods”). Using the fixed median-tail configuration defined above as the baseline, we compared the level-flight speed envelope and power consumption with those of NPU-Sparrow using active tail morphing. The morphing-tail configuration expands the level-flight speed envelope by 22.5%, from 4.5 to 13.2 m s −1 (Fig. 6a ), approaching the reported flight-speed range of similarly sized passerine birds 26 , 27 . At speeds above 7 m s −1 , the morphing-tail configuration also requires less power than the baseline. As the trim angle of attack decreases with increasing speed, tail tucking reduces the planform area and associated aerodynamic drag (Fig. 6b ), accounting for this reduction in cruise power requirement. Although NPU-Sparrow employs a negative-lift tail configuration, the measured angle of attack exceeded the 13° transition identified in the wind tunnel during high-load-factor maneuvers such as loops and barrel rolls (Fig. 6c,d , where the black reference line denotes 13° AoA). Under these conditions, the extended tail operates in the positive-lift regime and contributes to the total lifting force. A similar transition occurs during low-speed flight below approximately 6 m s −1 (Fig. 6b ), helping the robot reach lower flight speeds than the fixed-tail baseline. Compared with the baseline configuration, the steady-turn radius of NPU-Sparrow with active tail morphing is reduced by 20.4% (Fig. 6f ). Because the turning speeds are similar, the reduction is primarily associated with the higher maneuvering angle of attack achieved by the morphing-tail configuration. Under these conditions, the extended tail provides greater nose-up control authority, allowing the robot to trim at a higher angle of attack and thereby sustain a higher load factor and a smaller turn radius. During barrel rolls, the peak filtered roll rate of NPU-Sparrow reaches 375°s⁻¹ (Fig. 6e ), demonstrating the roll-control authority provided by differential flapping-wing incidence modulation. The flight-test results of NPU-Sparrow are consistent with the overall performance trends predicted by the model in the Pareto map (Fig. 7a ).The deviations observed in the boundary region primarily stem from the model’s limited capacity to capture nonlinear aerodynamic interference at high angles of attack (AoA &gt;25). Outdoor flight tests validate that the synergistic control of dynamic tail area and wing incidence angle effectively balances cruise stability and high-load maneuverability in real-world environments, together supporting the performance trends and wing-tail coordination mechanisms identified through the model-guided design analysis. By transitioning the negative-lift tail into an auxiliary lifting surface during aggressive maneuvers, NPU-Sparrow expands its flight envelope and reduces turn radius, demonstrating that a mechanistically simplified wing-tail coordination strategy offers a highly efficient pathway for bio-inspired aerial vehicles. Discussion Inspired by the avian ability to synergize tail and wing morphing for balancing efficient cruising with agile maneuvering, we propose and validate a model-guided, low-degree-of-freedom wing-tail coordinated design framework for micro flapping-wing robots, culminating in the development of the NPU-Sparrow platform. In contrast to birds, which utilize a highly complex musculoskeletal system for redundant control, this robot employs an unexplored control configuration: a linkage mechanism modulates the morphing tail area for pitch control, while a flapping wing incidence angle control mechanism provides both pitch and roll authority. This underactuated design strategy does not compromise core performance. Experimental results indicate that the NPU-Sparrow achieves a flight speed range comparable to that of similarly sized birds, a peak roll rate of up to 375° s⁻¹, and a substantially reduced sustained turning radius relative to the baseline configuration, while successfully executing demanding maneuvers such as loops and barrel rolls. These findings demonstrate that, at the micro scale, mechanism-based simplification, rather than morphology-based full replication, can recover some of the functional advantages afforded by avian wing-tail coordination. The model-guided, low-degree-of-freedom wing-tail coordinated design framework represents the central contribution of this work. Tail area variation is the core variable of this framework. Unlike conventional elevator deflection, the morphing tail simultaneously alters the pitching moment, aerodynamic center position, tail volume, and effective wing loading. Tail extension enhances pitch control authority and load-factor capability at high angles of attack; however, it also induces a rearward shift of the aerodynamic center and increases aerodynamic drag. In contrast, a tucked tail reduces cruise drag and decreases the power required to maintain level flight. These results indicate that tail area is not merely a control input but a morphological parameter that determines the operating position of the robot within the trade-off between cruise efficiency and maneuvering capability. A fixed-tail configuration can only correspond to a single compromise point, whereas the morphing tail enables the robot to switch between different operating regions in the Pareto design map. Meanwhile, the flapping wing incidence angle not only modifies the lift and pitching moment of the wing-body assembly but also regulates the aerodynamic center migration and load distribution induced by tail extension and tucking. After the introduction of coordinated flapping wing incidence angle control, the feasible design domain extends beyond the Pareto envelope obtained using tail extension and tucking alone (Fig. 3c ). As a result, the robot achieves improved maneuvering capability and cruise efficiency, along with a wider flight speed envelope than the baseline configuration. Although biological observations indicate that avian tails often generate positive lift to assist in weight support 8 , directly mimicking this feature could expose the robot to severe longitudinal static instability. The negative-lift tail configuration provides a more suitable compromise between static stability and maneuvering load capability, which explains its emergence as the preferred configuration in the Pareto design map.The model reveals the key aerodynamic mechanism underlying the negative-lift tail configuration adopted by NPU-Sparrow: a morphing tail with a negative installation angle can switch its aerodynamic role across different flight states. During low-angle-of-attack cruise, the negative-lift tail ensures longitudinal static stability and provides the necessary trim moment. However, during high-angle-of-attack maneuvering flight above 13°, as the tail extends, its aerodynamic role reverses, generating positive lift in the same direction as the flapping wings. Outdoor flight results further confirm that this mechanism allows the extended tail to no longer act as a load during high-load-factor maneuvers; instead, it transforms into a substantial auxiliary lifting surface, expanding the flight speed range by 22.5% relative to the baseline configuration (Fig. 6a ). Although this design deviates morphologically from the conventional avian state, it represents a practical engineering substitute for the evolutionary advantage of dynamic tail-lift modulation in birds at the level of functional logic. However, it is important to note that the tail morphing motion induces a significant shift in the position of the aerodynamic center relative to the control input, which substantially affects the pitch stability of the robot. Wind tunnel data indicate that as the tail of NPU-Sparrow extends, the overall aerodynamic center shifts considerably aft, with a displacement reaching up to 34.1% of the mean aerodynamic chord (Fig. 4d ). This shift results in a marked increase in the static stability margin, causing the control response to become ‘sluggish.’ Although our control configuration employs a synergistic approach of ‘tail extension combined with an increased flapping wing incidence angle’—leveraging enhanced wing lift to partially offset the pitch-up moment requirement and improve overall high-load-factor capability—this remains a relatively inefficient compensation strategy. In contrast to birds, which can actively manage the aerodynamic center through multiple degrees of freedom, such as wing extension, sweeping, and tail depression, the NPU-Sparrow faces challenges in handling large excursions of the aerodynamic center under its current configuration. This limitation hinders the full utilization of its maneuverability and, as a result, restricts its ability to adjust its attitude under highly complex airflow conditions. We propose that the introduction of a back-and-forth wing sweeping degree of freedom, decoupled from flapping motion, could facilitate active control of the aerodynamic center without increasing the structural complexity of the wings. Concurrently, asymmetric wing sweeping actuation may offer potential for yaw control. Moreover, if the modulation of wing incidence angle and tail area variation can be decoupled, with the tail area dynamically adjusted according to flight speed, may further expand the accessible flight-performance envelope and narrow the performance gap with birds without substantially increasing structural complexity. Although a performance gap remains between NPU-Sparrow and the passerine birds that motivate its design, this study demonstrates that aerodynamic functions derived from biological flight can be translated into modelable, parametrically assessable, and engineering-realizable design variables. By integrating tail-area modulation, tail installation-angle selection, and flapping-wing incidence-angle control within a model-guided, low-degree-of-freedom wing-tail coordinated framework, NPU-Sparrow provides an experimentally validated platform for investigating how simplified control variables can reproduce several functional characteristics of avian wing–tail coordination. These results support the feasibility of mechanism-based engineering simplification in bio-inspired flight and suggest that the design of avian-inspired aerial robots need not rely on direct replication of biological morphology. Instead, identifying the underlying aerodynamic functions and reconstructing them through simplified engineering mechanisms may provide a practical approach for balancing flight performance and system complexity in micro flapping-wing robots. Methods Morphing tail The morphing tail consists of five artificial tail feathers, with the central feather fixed to the fuselage and the remaining four feathers movable, driven by a linkage mechanism. As illustrated in the schematic (Fig. 8a ), a servo actuates a slider that controls the four movable feathers via the linkage system, where the orange and pink links correspond to the shafts of the movable feathers. This configuration enables precise control over the tail planform area, ensuring that the center of pressure remains virtually invariant during the tail extension process. Referencing the tail volume requirements for avian cruise flight 6 , we established a minimum tail volume coefficient of 0.3, which corresponds to a tail spread angle of 30° and a planform area of 0.00813 m². The maximum tail volume coefficient was set at 0.6, corresponding to a spread angle of 107° and an area of 0.01775 m². Throughout the entire tail extension process, the slider travels a distance of 9 mm. By rationally designing the linkage system, we achieved an approximately linear relationship between slider displacement and the variation in tail planform area (Fig. 8b ). Wind-tunnel measurements Wind tunnel experiments were conducted in the low-speed, low-turbulence wind tunnel at Northwestern Polytechnical University. The tunnel exhibits a turbulence intensity of less than 0.5% and dynamic pressure stability better than 0.5%, thereby ensuring a uniform and stable flow field while minimizing measurement errors induced by turbulence. The test section features a circular cross-section with a diameter of 1.6 m. Considering the strong spanwise flow induced by flapping motion, the large cross-sectional area effectively mitigates wall interference, providing a reliable aerodynamic environment for testing. The NPU-Sparrow was mounted on a six-axis high-precision force/torque sensor (ATI Nano 17) using an angle-adjustable mounting rig to facilitate precise control of the AoA. The measured aerodynamic forces were processed using a Butterworth low-pass filter. Given that the flapping frequency does not exceed 16 Hz, the filter cutoff frequency was set to 30 Hz—approximately twice the maximum flapping frequency—to suppress high-frequency noise while preserving signal integrity. The wind tunnel test was conducted at a wind speed of 7 ms⁻¹, which is the design cruising speed of NPU-Sparrow. All signals were processed using consistent filter parameters and sampled at uniform rates to ensure reliable data analysis. The NPU-Sparrow was installed atop the force sensor, which was mounted on the angle adjustment mechanism. Flight-test setup All flight tests of the robot were conducted outdoors under the remote control of a human pilot. Upon receiving control commands, the robot executed pitch and roll maneuvers utilizing a lightweight autopilot featuring simple cascaded PID algorithm. Simultaneously, the onboard black box of the miniature autopilot recorded sensor data throughout the flight. The collected data encompassed GPS position, linear acceleration, angular velocity, angular acceleration, attitude angles, power consumption, and control input signals. Due to space constraints onboard, the data logging capacity of the autopilot was limited. Consequently, GPS data were sampled at 6 Hz, attitude angles and control inputs at 15 Hz, and linear acceleration, angular velocity, and angular acceleration at 200 Hz; and power data at 10 Hz.
