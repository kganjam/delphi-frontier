Introduction The insulin-like growth factor (IGF) receptor signaling pathway is a cornerstone of cellular communication and regulation, serving as a central hub for integrating extracellular signals into intracellular responses. The IGF system is composed of two primary ligands (IGF1 and IGF2), three receptors (IGF1R, IGF2R, and the insulin receptor, InsR), and six high-affinity IGF-binding proteins (IGFBPs, IGFBP1-IGFBP6). 1 , 2 These components collectively orchestrate endocrine, paracrine, and autocrine signaling essential for vertebrate development, growth and physiological homeostasis. Upon ligand binding, both IGF1R and InsR undergo autophosphorylation, activating their intrinsic kinase activity and initiating downstream signaling cascades. The two major effector pathways are the phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K)–AKT pathway, which primarily regulates cell survival and metabolism and growth, and the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK)/ERK (extracellular signal-regulated kinase) pathway, which predominantly controls cell proliferation and differentiation. 3 Conversely, IGF2R lacks tyrosine kinase activity and functions mainly as a clearance receptor, facilitating the internalization and lysosomal degradation of IGF2. 4 The bioavailability and activity of IGFs are tightly regulated by the six IGFBPs, which bind IGFs with high affinity. 5 Nearly all IGF molecules in the extracellular milieu, whether in tissues or circulating in the bloodstream, are complexed with IGFBPs. 6 This binding not only modulates IGF stability and distribution but also fine-tunes their interaction with receptors, ensuring precise control over signaling outcomes. Functionally, the IGF signaling pathway governs a wide array of cellular processes, including cell survival, differentiation, migration, and proliferation. 7 Beyond its role in cellular physiology, IGF signaling is critical for systemic processes such as somatic growth, developmental progression, and aging. 8 Recent studies have underscored that IGF signaling governs organ homeostasis throughout an individual’s lifespan, and its dysregulation is strongly associated with a spectrum of human diseases. 9 – 12 These insights have positioned the modulation of IGF/IGFR signaling as a promising therapeutic strategy for diverse medical conditions. Notably, several IGF/IGFR inhibitors have shown clinical efficacy, 13 – 16 with some candidates advancing through late-stage trials and approaching regulatory approval. IGF signaling exhibits extensive crosstalk with other key pathways, such as insulin, Wnt/beta-catenin, epidermal growth factor (EGF), platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF), vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), Janus kinase–signal transducer and activator of transcription (JAK–STAT), and transforming growth factor-β/bone morphogenetic protein (TGF-β/BMP), to coordinately regulate numerous biological processes. For instance, IGF1 counteracts the neurotoxic effects of tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNFα), a proinflammatory cytokine implicated in neurodegeneration, by activating the JAK–STAT pathway. 17 IGFBP3 signals through the TGF-β type II receptor (TGFBR2)–Smad2/3 axis to mediate growth inhibition in breast cancer. 18 Wnt-driven repression of IGFBP5 sustains IGF-dependent mammary tumor growth. 19 EGF potentiates IGF signaling by upregulating IGF1R expression, thereby enhancing breast cancer cell adhesion to fibronectin. 20 In short, the intricate interplay between IGF and these interconnected networks plays a pivotal role in both cellular homeostasis and disease pathophysiology. A deep understanding of this interaction is critical for the development of more effective therapeutic strategies. This review provides a comprehensive examination of the IGF/IGFR system, tracing its historical development and delineating its core molecular architecture, including key upstream regulators and downstream effectors. We also elucidate the crosstalk between IGF signaling and other pathways in the regulation of cellular homeostasis and disease progression. Emphasis is placed on the pathogenic role and dysregulation of IGF/IGFR signaling in a broad spectrum of human disorders, including cancer, cardiovascular diseases, neurodegenerative conditions, liver disease and metabolic syndromes, skeletal abnormalities, aging-related pathologies, autoimmune disorders, and rare genetic diseases. Finally, we review current therapeutic targets, recent clinical research progress, and U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-approved pharmacological agents targeting this pathway. The IGF/IGFR signaling pathway: a historical perspective The study of growth hormone (GH) signaling in the late 1950s laid the groundwork for the discovery of IGFs, a field that has since expanded through transformative breakthroughs and ongoing clinical investigations. In 1957, IGFs were first identified as the “sulfation factor” (SF) due to their ability to stimulate sulfate incorporation into cartilage. 21 By 1972, SF was renamed “somatomedins” to reflect their role as mediators of GH action. 22 In 1976, their structural and functional homology with insulin led to their reclassification as insulin-like growth factor I and II (IGF1 and IGF2) 23 (Fig. 1 ). The early 2000s saw the translation of IGF1R-targeting therapies into clinical trials, with the monoclonal antibody CP-751871 (figitumumab) entering trials in 2005 and reporting results by 2007. 33 Additional agents, including R1507 and AVE1642, reached phase I testing around the same time. 34 However, challenges such as toxicity, compensatory InsR signaling, and modest efficacy limit their clinical adoption. In parallel, research has increasingly focused on the metabolic implications of IGF/IGFR signaling, particularly its crosstalk with insulin in metabolic disorders. 35 , 36 In 2020, the FDA approved teprotumumab (Tepezza), an antibody targeting IGF1R, for thyroid eye disease (TAO) 37 (Fig. 1 ). Recent years have witnessed the development of next-generation IGF1R antibodies, now in phase I/II trials either as monotherapies or in combination with chemotherapy, immunotherapy, or radiotherapy. 38 , 39 Despite these advances, therapeutic responses remain suboptimal, 40 , 41 and only a handful of IGF1R-targeted agents have progressed to phase III trials, 16 underscoring the complexities of modulating this pathway. This review traces the historical trajectory of IGF/IGFR research, from its foundational discoveries to its current therapeutic challenges, while highlighting its enduring relevance in physiology and disease. The IGF/IGFR signaling axis: structural components, dynamic regulation and signal transduction The IGF signaling pathway represents one of the most evolutionarily conserved regulatory systems in metazoans. 42 Upon ligand binding, IGF1R undergoes rapid autophosphorylation, triggering downstream signaling cascades primarily through the PI3K–AKT and MAPK pathways (Fig. 2 ). Functionally, the IGF axis serves as a master integrator, translating extracellular nutrient availability and hormonal signals into coordinated intracellular responses. This unique positioning enables it to regulate development, tissue homeostasis, and metabolic adaptation, while its dysregulation contributes to cancer, diabetes, and neurodegenerative disorders. Given its broad physiological and pathological implications, a comprehensive understanding of IGF signaling, including its structural composition, dynamic regulation, and downstream transduction mechanisms, is essential for both basic research and therapeutic development. However, in IGF2, incomplete E-domain cleavage occurs in certain conditions, such as impaired glycosylation, leading to incompletely processed forms observed in pathological states, including nonislet-cell tumor hypoglycemia (NICTH) and hepatitis C-associated osteosclerosis (HCAO). 45 Both IGF1 and IGF2 are renowned for their potent mitogenic and antiapoptotic effects, driving cell growth, survival, and tissue development across nearly all tissues. 46 , 47 These multifunctional growth factors exemplify the intricate balance between structural conservation and functional diversity, 48 , 49 highlighting their central role in growth regulation and metabolic homeostasis. IGF-binding proteins (IGFBPs) IGFBPs comprise a family of six high-affinity proteins (IGFBP1-IGFBP6), encoded by distinct genes, that bind IGF1 and IGF2 in humans and other mammals. 50 The number and types of IGFBPs vary across species due to evolutionary adaptations. 6 For instance, chickens lack IGFBP4 and IGFBP6, while certain fish species possess up to four variants of each IGFBP following genome duplication events. 51 – 53 From an evolutionary standpoint, IGFBPs display diverse and partially overlapping functions, 6 with knockout models often revealing functional redundancy or compensatory mechanisms. 6 , 54 Beyond the six canonical IGFBPs, structurally related proteins such as IGFBP7 (mac25/IGFBPrP1), CTGF (IGFBPrP2), and NovH (IGFBP-rP3) belong to the CCN family. 2 These proteins show very low binding affinity for IGFs, and their IGF-dependent functions remain largely unclear. Notably, IGFBP7 has been implicated in acute lymphoblastic leukemia, where it enhances oncogenic signaling by stabilizing IGF1R and prolonging insulin/IGF stimulation, suggesting potential for antibody-based therapies. 55 The extended IGFBP family thus reflects complex regulation of IGF activity and warrants further investigation. IGFBP1 to 6 are secretory glycoproteins localized in circulation, the extracellular matrix (ECM), and intracellular compartments. 49 Circulating IGF levels are tightly regulated, primarily through proteolytic degradation of IGFBPs by specific proteases. 56 Structurally, IGFBPs feature highly conserved N-terminal and C-terminal domains stabilized by disulfide bridges connected by a variable central linker domain (CLD) lacking disulfide bonds, which confers structural flexibility. 6 , 50 This flexibility facilitates proteolytic processing and modulates IGF bioavailability 57 (Fig. 4 ). signaling protects against hyperglycemia, highlighting therapeutic potential. 65 Receptors The IGF signaling pathway involves three distinct receptors, IGF1R, IGF2R, and InsR, each with unique ligand-binding specificities and downstream effects. 2 , 66 IGF1R binds both IGF1 and IGF2 with high affinity (0.1–1 nM), 67 – 70 while IGF2 preferentially binds InsR isoforms, showing ~ 10-fold higher affinity for InsR-A (2–3 nM) than InsR-B (30–40 nM). 66 Hybrid InsR-IGF1R receptors exhibit strong binding to IGF1 (0.2–0.7 nM) and IGF2 (0.2–0.8 nM) but weak affinity for insulin (50–90 nM). 71 , 72 In contrast, IGF2R, a non-tyrosine kinase receptor, selectively binds IGF2 with high specificity (lacking affinity for IGF1), 4 , 73 functioning primarily in ligand clearance and degradation. These differential binding affinities critically regulate the spatiotemporal activation of metabolic, mitogenic, and growth-promoting pathways. IGF1R, the principal transducer of the IGF pathway, is a transmembrane heterotetrametric receptor tyrosine kinase (RTK) composed of two α and two β subunits linked by disulfide bonds. 74 The α subunits mediate ligand binding, while the β subunits contain the tyrosine kinase domain. A single IGF1 or IGF2 molecule binds tightly to one of the two site-1 regions of IGF1R, 75 enabling rapid responses to physiological ligand levels while preventing overactivation at high concentrations. Ligand binding induces conformational changes in the IGF1R dimer, including hinge movements, leading to subsequent receptor activation. 67 , 76 Activated IGF1R triggers downstream cascades and undergoes internalization, which sustains signaling. 77 Internalized IGF1R can be ubiquitinated, trafficked to the Golgi apparatus for recycling, 78 or translocated to the nucleus via interactions with dynactin subunit p150 Glued , importin-β, and RanBP2, 79 where they modulate chromatin-associated transcriptional activity or interact with transcription factors, thereby influencing gene expression. 80 , 81 InsR, a key constituent of the IGF system, regulates metabolic and mitogenic signaling. 82 Like IGF1R, it is a transmembrane RTK with two extracellular α-subunits and two transmembrane β-subunits. 82 Ligand binding induces autophosphorylation and activates the PI3K–AKT and RAS–RAF–MAPK pathways (Fig. 4 ). The InsR gene undergoes alternative splicing to yield InsR-A (lacking exon 11) and InsR-B (containing exon 11). InsR-A predominates in fetal tissues and cancer cells, favoring mitogenic and antiapoptotic signaling, 83 while InsR-B is expressed in adult metabolic tissues (such as the liver, muscles, and adipose tissue) 83 and mediates the metabolic effects of insulin, such as glucose uptake and lipogenesis. 84 This splicing-mediated diversity enables tailored responses to ligands and contributes to physiology and pathology, including insulin resistance and carcinogenesis. 85 Structural mimicry between IGF1R and InsR enables hybrid receptor formation with conformations similar to IGF1R/IGF complexes. 86 Although each receptor binds its native ligands with higher affinity, cross-reactivity supports parallel signaling. For instance, one IGF1 molecule binds hybrids via site-1a on IGF1R and site-1b on InsR, leaving site-2 unoccupied. Insulin and IGF2 can engage site-2 on InsR, implicating ligand-specific confirmational changes in hybrids. In contrast, IGF2R, also known as cation-independent mannose-6-phosphate (M6P) receptor, 87 is a monomeric transmembrane glycoprotein lacking intrinsic kinase activity. 88 It functions primarily as a scavenger receptor for IGF2 (Fig. 2 ), shuttling between the Golgi, endosomes, and cell surface. 89 , 90 IGF2R facilitates the transport of M6P-tagged lysosomal enzymes and mediates endocytosis and lysosomal degradation of IGF2, 91 attenuating proliferative signaling via IGF1R/InsR hybrids. 92 It may also activate G-protein signaling potentially via sphingosine 1-phosphate receptors, 93 broadening its regulatory scope. Adaptor proteins Adaptor proteins serve as critical intermediaries in IGF/IGFR and InsR signaling, linking activated RTKs to downstream effector pathways. The two major classes discussed here, insulin receptor substrates (IRS) and Src homology and collagen (Shc) proteins, orchestrate distinct yet overlapping signaling outputs that regulate metabolism, growth, survival, and differentiation. Insulin receptor substrate (IRS) proteins The IRS proteins are key adaptor molecules for RTKs, including InsR and IGF1R, playing a central role in transducing signals to downstream cascades 94 (Fig. 2 ). Upon ligand binding, these receptors undergo autophosphorylation on specific tyrosine residues, creating docking sites for IRS recruitment. 95 Once bound, IRS proteins are themselves phosphorylated on multiple tyrosine residues, generating binding platforms for SH2 domain-containing effectors, such as the p85 regulatory subunit of PI3K and growth factor receptor-bound protein 2 (Grb2). 96 These interactions initiate the PI3K–AKT pathway and the RAS–ERK pathways. 97 The IRS family comprises several members, with IRS-1 and IRS-2 exhibiting tissue-specific expression and largely nonredundant functions, as demonstrated by knockout and transgenic studies in vivo. 98 Dysregulation of IRS signaling, through mutations, inhibitory serine/threonine phosphorylation, often mediated by feedback loops involving rapamycin complex 1 (mTORC1), JNK or protein kinase C (PKC), or altered expression, has been strongly implicated in insulin resistance and type 2 diabetes (T2D) 99 and cancer progression. 100 Thus, IRS proteins serve as essential integrators of extracellular signals, and their precise regulation is vital for maintaining metabolic homeostasis and preventing pathological states. Shc proteins Shc adapter proteins, particularly the ubiquitously expressed ShcA isoform, 101 play a pivotal role in orchestrating downstream signaling from IGF1R and InsR. ShcA exists in three isoforms, p66 Shc , p52 Shc and p46 Shc , generated by alternative splicing and translational regulation from the SHC1 gene. The p52 Shc and p46 Shc isoforms predominantly activate the RAS–ERK pathway, 102 , 103 promoting mitogenic responses, whereas p66 Shc , distinguished by its unique N-terminal collagen-homology domain, exerts an inhibitory effect on RAS–ERK signaling and is implicated in the regulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) production and aging processes. 104 Following RTK activation, Shc proteins are phosphorylated on tyrosine residues, although with lower efficiency and different kinetics compared to IRS proteins (Fig. 2 ). Shc phosphorylation is particularly sensitive to receptor internalization, which can modulate the duration and amplitude of downstream signals. A critical feature of Shc signaling is its competition with IRS proteins for binding to the phosphotyrosine-binding (PTB) domains on InsR or IGF1R. 105 This competitive interaction can shift the balance, and elevated Shc levels favor mitogenic (RAF–ERK-driven) responses over metabolic (PI3K–AKT-mediated) outputs. Disruption of negative feedback mechanisms that normally limit IRS recruitment may further enhance Shc-dependent mitogenic signaling. This dynamic interplay underscores the delicate balance between metabolic and proliferative signaling, with Shc proteins serving as key modulators of cellular responses to insulin and IGFs. Downstream signal transduction pathways of the IGF/IGFR axis PI3K–AKT pathway The PI3K–AKT pathway is a major downstream signaling cascade activated by the IGF/IGFR axis (Fig. 2 ). Upon IGF ligand binding, IGF1R undergoes conformational changes and autophosphorylation, recruiting adaptor proteins such as IRS and Shc to phosphorylated tyrosine residues. These adaptors, in turn, activate PI3K, which phosphorylates phosphatidylinositol 4,5-bisphosphate (PIP 2 ) at the 3-position to generate phosphatidylinositol (3,4,5)-trisphosphate (PIP 3 ) at the plasma membrane. 106 PIP 3 serves as a lipid second messenger that recruits and activates phosphoinositide-dependent kinase 1 (PDK1), which phosphorylates AKT at Thr 308. 107 Concurrently, mammalian target of rapamycin complex 2 (mTORC2) phosphorylates AKT at Ser 473, 108 leading to full activation of AKT. Activated AKT then phosphorylates a broad array of downstream targets, including forkhead box protein O (FOXO), tuberous sclerosis 2 (TSC2), glycogen synthase kinase 3β (GSK3β) and RabGAP TBC1 domain family member 4 (TBC1D4). 109 , 110 TBC1D4 blocks RAB, facilitating translocation of glucose transporter type 4 (GLUT4) to the plasma membrane for glucose update. TSC2 activates mTORC1 and its downstream effectors to enhance protein synthesis. 111 Through these mechanisms, the PI3K–AKT pathway coordinately regulates glucose, lipid and protein metabolism 112 while also modulating gene expression programs that govern cell proliferation, growth, differentiation and apoptosis 113 (Fig. 2 ). MAPK/ERK pathway Parallel to the PI3K–AKT cascade, ligand-activated IGF1R robustly engages the MAPK/ERK pathway (Fig. 2 ). Beyond tyrosine-phosphorylated IRS and Shc proteins, a diverse array of substrates, including growth factor receptor bound protein-2 (GRB2), GRB2-associated-binding protein 1 (GAB1), and related scaffolding proteins, bridge activated IGF1R to the guanine nucleotide exchange factor son of sevenless (SOS). 114 The nonreceptor protein tyrosine phosphatase SHP2 (PTPN11) plays a critical facilitatory role by stabilizing GAB1/GRB2/SOS and promoting its recruitment to the membrane. 114 This complex formation catalyzes GDP-GTP exchange on RAS GTPases, initiating the canonical RAS–RAF–MEK–ERK cascade. 115 Phosphorylated ERK1/2 translocates to the nucleus, where it phosphorylates and activates key transcription factors, including ELK1, c-FOS and c-MYC, thereby driving gene expression programs that promote cell proliferation, differentiation, and survival. Upstream modulators of the IGF/IGFR signaling pathway The IGF system is governed by a hierarchy of upstream regulators, including GH, nutritional status, stress, environmental factors, inflammatory cytokines and noncoding RNAs. Growth hormone and nutritional regulation GH is the primary endocrine regulator of IGF1 production, particularly in the liver, where it stimulates hepatic IGF1 gene expression and systemic IGF1 release. 116 GH binds its cognate receptor (GHR), activating the JAK2-STAT5 pathway; phosphorylated STAT5 dimers translocate to the nucleus and directly induce IGF1 transcription. 117 Moreover, nutritional status is a major determinant of circulating and local IGF1 levels. 118 Adequate nutrition supports IGF1 synthesis in the liver and peripheral tissues, whereas protein and caloric restriction significantly suppress IGF1 production and bioavailability. 118 , 119 This nutrient-sensitive regulation positions IGF1 as a metabolic sensor integrating energy availability with growth and tissue maintenance. Environmental and physiological stress Environmental and physiological stressors exert bidirectional effects on the IGF system. 120 Chronic psychological stress downregulates IGF1 expression in multiple tissues, contributing to growth retardation, metabolic dysfunction and accelerated aging phenotypes. 121 , 122 In contrast, acute or regular physical exercise enhances IGF1 levels (and locally produced IGF1 in muscle), facilitating muscle repair, hypertrophy, and overall anabolic adaptation. 123 These opposing effects underscore the remarkable plasticity of the IGF axis in adapting to external and internal demands. Inflammatory cytokine-mediated regulation Inflammatory cytokines profoundly modulate the IGF/IGFR axis. Interleukin-6 (IL-6) increases the expression of IGF1 and IGF2 in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) cells, 124 potentially supporting tumor growth. In contrast, TNFα can downregulate IGF1 and IGFBP3 in various cell types. 125 TNF-α, IL-1beta, and IL-6 also induce IGF resistance by interfering with downstream PI3K–AKT and ERK signaling in growth plate chondrocytes. 126 It is plausible that this mechanism is implicated in the complex pathophysiology of growth failure during chronic inflammation. Noncoding RNA-mediated regulation of the IGF axis Noncoding RNAs, including microRNAs (miRNAs), long noncoding RNAs (lncRNAs), and circular RNAs (circRNAs), exert precise and multilayered control over the IGF/IGFR signaling axis. The miRNAs have shown to modulate ligand expression, receptor levels, and pathway activity in a tissue- and context-specific manner. For example miR-206 suppress IGF1 expression, thereby regulating the growth of the teleost tilapia. 127 Tissue-specific miRNAs further control diversity; hepatic miR-122, the most abundant miRNA in the liver, downregulates IGF1R and influences metabolic homeostasis as well as hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) progression. 128 LncRNAs regulate the IGF axis through epigenetic, transcriptional, and posttranscriptional mechanisms. At the imprinted H19/IGF2 locus, H19 and its antisense transcript 91H modulate IGF2 expression via chromatin modifications and imprinting control, influencing fetal growth, IGF-axis activity, and breast cancer pathogenesis. 129 Notably, H19 also serves as a developmental reservoir for miR-675, which limits growth and suppresses IGF1R expression. 130 Many oncogenic lncRNAs function as competing endogenous RNAs (ceRNAs) to modulate IGF1R signaling, including LINC01291, linc00319 , and lnc-MLETA1 , which promote tumorigenesis, invasion, metastasis, or chemoresistance by sponging particular microRNAs and reinforcing IGF1R expression and IGF1R signaling in diverse cancer types. 131 – 133 Conversely, tumor- and fibrosis-suppressive lncRNAs, for example, GAS5, negatively regulate IGF1 expression and the downstream PI3K–AKT pathway. 134 Beyond oncogenesis, lncRNA-mediated IGF modulation extends to endocrine and reproductive disorders; for instance, HCP5 and HOTAIR dysregulate IGF1–PI3K–AKT signaling in polycystic ovarian syndrome, affecting granulosa cell survival and ovarian activity. 135 , 136 Likewise, circRNAs have emerged as key modulators of IGF-induced processes. For example, circCCDC91 and circUSP13 enhance myoblast proliferation, differentiation, and resistance to muscle atrophy through the activation of IGF1–PI3K–AKT cascades. 137 , 138 Pathologically, circ_0001459 and circ_0014130 enhance tumor growth by promoting IGF1R or IGF1 expression via ceRNA networks. 139 , 140 Collectively, these findings reveal that the IGF axis is embedded within a complex regulatory network of noncoding RNAs that adds substantial versatility to IGF signaling control in growth, homeostasis, and disease. Created with BioRender.com Interaction with the insulin signaling pathway IGF and insulin signaling extensively crosstalk through shared downstream pathways, ligand cross-reactivity, and the formation of IGF1R/InsR hybrid receptors. 141 Hybrid receptors preferentially bind IGF1 and IGF2 over insulin but retain the ability to transduce both mitogenic and metabolic signals. 142 IGF1 and insulin signaling synergistically enhance glucose uptake, glycogen synthesis, and protein synthesis, promoting growth and metabolic homeostasis. 143 IGF1 and insulin support the translocation of glucose transporter type 4 (GLUT4) to the cell membrane in skeletal muscles and adipose tissue, enhancing glucose disposal. 143 IGF1 additionally stimulates glycolysis and activates glycogen synthase activity, favoring glucose storage. 144 In InsR -deficient models, IGF1R can partially compensate for metabolic signaling, 145 further illustrating functional overlap. This crosstalk between IGF and the insulin system exhibits a potential role in the pathophysiology and therapeutics of diabetes. IGF1 exerts insulin-mimetic effects by enhancing glucose uptake and utilization in peripheral tissues while suppressing hepatic gluconeogenesis, 146 primarily via PI3K-AKT activation, a pathway shared with insulin. 147 In T2D mellitus, where insulin resistance and progressive β-cell dysfunction predominate, circulating IGF1 levels are often normal or modestly elevated, potentially reflecting a compensatory adaptation to impaired insulin action. Epidemiological and clinical studies link higher bioactive IGF1 levels with improved insulin sensitivity and reduced risk of developing T2DM, 148 supporting a protective metabolic role. Interaction with EGF and PDGF signaling pathways EGF, PDGF, and IGF systems exhibit functional convergence on the core MAPK and PI3K–AKT cascades, which coordinately regulate cellular proliferation, survival, differentiation and tissue remodeling (Fig. 5 ). EGF receptor (EGFR) interacts with IGF1R, modulating its stability. 149 In breast cancer models, IGF1 and EGF cooperatively promote cell adhesion through IGF1R/ERK1/2 signaling, while EGF upregulates IGF1R expression, establishing a positive feedforward loop. 150 Additional intersection points include EGFR transactivation by IGF1 or leptin 151 and stromal EGF/IGF1 coregulation of triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) cellular plasticity. 152 In the esophageal epithelium, EGF suppresses the expression of IGFBP3 , thereby potentiating IGF1R signaling and anabolic responses. 153 Conversely, IGFBP2 suppresses EGFR-STAT3 signaling, protecting against hepatic steatosis. 154 IGF1R has been implicated in acquired resistance to EGFR tyrosine kinase inhibitors (EGFR-TKIs) across different cancer types, including EGFR-mutant lung cancer and squamous cell carcinoma by acting as a bypass signaling pathway. 155 , 156 Preclinical reports show that transient inhibition of IGF1R combined with osimertinib (EGFR-TKI) markedly eradicates AXL-low expressing EGFR mutated lung cancer. 155 However, combination of xentuzumab (antibody neutralizing IGFs) and afatinib (EGFR-TKI) has so far demonstrated limited clinical efficacy. 157 IGF1 induces the expression of PDGF ligands through ERK- and AKT-dependent activation of transcription factor early growth response protein 1 (EGR1), while IGFBP3 negatively regulates this pathway, inhibiting PDGF-driven HCC cell proliferation. 61 Reciprocally, PDGF enhances IGF1R promoter activity. 158 In rat hepatic myofibroblasts, PDGF-BB counteracts IGF1-induced downregulation of IGF1R without altering PDGFR levels, and functional IGF1R is required for PDGF-driven mitogenesis. 159 Dual pathway blockade further reveals interdependence, and IGF1R kinase inhibition attenuates both IGF1- and PDGF-mediated proliferation while specifically blocking PDGF-induced phospholipase C gamma 1 (PLCγ1) phosphorylation 159 (Fig. 5 ). In nonmalignant contexts, PDGF and IGF converge on AKT–MAPK pathways to drive proliferation. Examples include bovine intervertebral disc cells and rat chondrocytes, where cotreatment with PDGF and IGF yields additive DNA synthesis. 160 In osteoarthritis (OA) models, both IGF1 and PDGF-BB inhibit interleukin-1β (IL-1β)-induced nuclear factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells (NF-κB) activation and apoptosis in chondrocytes. 161 These protective effects suggest the potential for combined growth factor-based strategies to mitigate inflammatory cartilage degradation in OA. Interaction with JAK–STAT signaling pathways The JAK–STAT pathway exhibits bidirectional and reciprocal crosstalk with IGF/IGFR signaling, crucially modulating cellular proliferation, differentiation, and survival (Fig. 5 ). IGF1 signaling via IGF1R activates STAT3 through a JAK-dependent mechanism. STAT3 upregulates suppressor of cytokine signaling 3 (SOCS-3), which in turn associates with IGF1R and inhibits further STAT3 activation. 162 SOCS2 inhibits epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) by competitively binding STAT docking sites on IGF1R, 163 inactivating STAT3 and STAT5. In NSCLC, IL-6 facilitates EMT-mediated EGFR-TKI resistance through an autocrine IL-6/IGF1R/STAT3 loop. This circuit involves reciprocal pathway activation and STAT3-dependent transcriptional upregulation of IGF1R and its ligands. 124 In neuronal cells, IGF1 enhances cyclin D1 expression via STAT5 signaling, linking IGF action to cell cycle progression in the nervous system. 164 In rat cardiomyocytes, IGF1 activates JAK1, leading to dual tyrosine/serine phosphorylation of STAT1 and STAT3. 165 Additionally, IGF1 also exerts immunomodulatory effects by polarizing mouse bone marrow-derived neutrophils toward an anti-inflammatory phenotype through a noncanonical JAK2–STAT6 pathway. 166 In mammary epithelium, STAT5 cooperates with PI3K–AKT to orchestrate both physiological development and pathological progression of breast cancer. 167 Additionally, mTORC1 directly phosphorylates STAT3 on Ser727 during hypoxia. 168 Given that IGF signaling robustly activates the PI3K–AKT–mTORC1 axis, it is plausible that IGF1R may induce STAT3 Ser727 phosphorylation in an mTORC1-dependent manner in specific cellular contexts. Interaction with the VEGF signaling pathway The VEGF pathway is a central driver of angiogenesis. 169 – 171 VEGF and IGF pathways exhibit multifaceted crosstalk that coordinates angiogenesis, lymphangiogenesis, and metastasis. IGF signaling potentiates VEGF-driven angiogenesis in a tissue-specific manner. 172 In retinal pigment epithelial (ARPE-19) cells, IGF1 stimulates the secretion of VEGF and IGFBP3 through hypoxia-inducible factor-1 alpha (HIF-1α) activation, while VEGF independently induces IGFBP3. IGFBP3 subsequently provides negative feedback by suppressing IGF1-mediated VEGF release. 172 , 173 Notably, VEGF enhances IGF1R expression, increasing cellular responsiveness to IGF1. 174 In breast cancer, IGF1 upregulates VEGF-C expression via the PI3K–AKT and ERK1/2 pathways, modulating lymphatic metastasis. 175 In osteoblasts, IGF1 stimulates VEGF-A expression by stabilizing HIF-2α protein levels, 176 thereby coupling angiogenesis to osteogenesis during bone repair. Synergistic actions of IGF1 and VEGF also direct mesenchymal stromal cell differentiation toward osteoblastic or endothelial lineages. 177 In ischemic muscle injury models in rodents, combined VEGF and IGF1 treatment synergistically enhances repair by simultaneously promoting angiogenesis and activating satellite cells while inhibiting apoptosis (IGF1), leading to improved vascular perfusion, myofiber regeneration, and functional recovery. 178 Interaction with TGF-β/BMP signaling pathways TGF-β and BMP pathways are pivotal regulators of cell fate, development, and tissue homeostasis. 179 IGF/IGFR signaling engages in bidirectional, context-dependent crosstalk with these Smad-dependent pathways (Fig. 5 ). IGF1 selectively inhibits TGF-β-induced transcriptional responses by attenuating Smad3 activation. 180 In skeletal muscle, IGF1 promotes AKT- and ERK-dependent proteasomal degradation of Smad2/3, thereby preventing muscle atrophy. 181 TGF-β can reciprocally restrain IGF1 signaling. In osteosarcoma cells, TGF-β1 upregulates IGFBP3 expression via the ERK1/2 pathway, forming a negative feedback loop that suppresses IGF1R activation and thereby restrains cell growth. 182 TGF-β also regulates pregnancy-associated plasma protein-A (PAPPA), 183 a protease that cleaves IGFBP4 and increases IGF1 bioavailability, while promoting IGF1 transcription in fibrotic tissues through Smad2 and mTOR signaling 184 (Fig. 5 ). In contrast to antagonism in normal tissues, TGF-β1 and IGF1 often cooperate to drive malignant progression. In glioblastoma, IGF1 potentiates TGF-β signaling by upregulating TGFB1 and TGFBR2 while suppressing miR-4286 , a direct inhibitor of the TGF-β pathway, 185 thereby enhancing EMT and invasion. Pharmacological TGF-β receptor inhibition (e.g., using LY2157299) reverses IGF1-induced invasion and EMT. 185 BMP pathways are similarly modulated by RTK/MAPK cascades activated by FGF, or IGF1. 179 During bone development, BMP-2/9 regulate IGF1-mediated interactions with chondrocytes. 186 Overexpression of IGF1 in rat bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) inhibits IL-1β-induced chondrocyte apoptosis, promotes proliferation, reduces inflammatory factor secretion, alleviates chondrocyte damage, and enhances cartilage repair, effects potentially linked to activation of the BMP2-Smad1/5 pathway. 187 IGF2 also potentiates BMP-9-mediated osteogenic differentiation and bone formation. 188 Conversely, IGFBP3 inhibits BMP-2/4-induced Smad signaling in zebrafish embryos 189 (Fig. 5 ), illustrating species- and context-specific antagonism. Interaction with ROR1/HER2 signaling Receptor tyrosine kinase-like orphan receptor 1 (ROR1) is primarily expressed during embryogenesis and is largely silenced in most normal adult tissues. 190 However, ROR1 is aberrantly re-expressed in numerous malignancies, where it contributes to cancer stem cell (CSC) self-renewal, EMT, invasion and therapy resistance. 191 – 193 In CSCs, ROR1 can heterodimerize with HER2 (ErbB2), leading to the activation of multiple downstream cascades that sustain oncogenic signaling. 194 A novel layer of crosstalk between the IGF network and ROR1/HER2 signaling has emerged through IGFBP5, which has been identified as a functional ligand for ROR1 (Fig. 5 ). In glioblastoma stem-like cells, IGFBP5 binds ROR1, facilitating ROR1/HER2 heterodimerization and subsequent CREB activation. 195 This axis drives transcriptional upregulation of invasion-associated genes, including ETV5 and FBXW9 , thereby promoting invasion and tumorigenesis. 195 Genetic ablation of IGFBP5 significantly impairs these invasive phenotypes and downstream signaling, establishing IGFBP5 as a critical mediator of ROR1/HER2–CREB-driven malignancy in this context. 195 Although evidence for IGFBP5-induced ROR1/HER2–CREB signaling is currently confined to glioblastoma, the oncogenic roles of ROR1, HER2 and CREB, together with the broad expression of IGFBP5 in various cancers, suggest that this axis may represent a novel layer of IGF network integration with RTK signaling. Additional evidence of IGF1R–HER2 crosstalk is observed in trastuzumab-resistant breast cancer, where IGF1R physically interacts with HER2, 196 sustaining HER2 activation and contributing to therapy resistance. Interaction with Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathways The IGF and Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathways exhibit complex, bidirectional, and context-dependent crosstalk with profound implications for embryonic development, tissue homeostasis, and cancer initiation/progression. 197 – 199 In porcine embryos, IGF1 activates the Wnt/β-catenin pathway to promote trophectoderm cell proliferation. 200 Murine models of mammary tumorigenesis reveal dual roles, seemingly paradoxical role for IGF1R signaling in Wnt-driven oncogenesis. On the one hand, IGF1R suppression in normal mammary epithelium enhances canonical Wnt/β-catenin signaling and accelerates Wnt1-driven tumor formation. 201 Consistent with this, mammary tumor regression induced by Wnt/β-catenin inhibitors depends on IGFBP5-mediated IGF1R downregulation, 19 suggesting that intact IGF1R activity can constrain Wnt/β-catenin-driven malignancy in certain settings. Conversely, in models of chronic Wnt/β-catenin activation (e.g., MMTV-Wnt1 transgenics and triple-negative breast cancer xenografts), IGF1R activity constrains Wnt/β-catenin-driven oncogenesis. 202 , 203 Mechanistically, attenuation or loss of IGF1R promotes tumorigenesis by enriching the CD61⁺CD29^ low luminal progenitor population, the primary cellular target for Wnt/β-catenin-mediated transformation. 202 , 203 This progenitor expansion arises from disrupted Notch signaling and reduced Elf-5 expression. 202 , 203 Notably, Wnt/β-catenin-mediated transformation requires intact Notch activity, 204 establishing a functional interdependence among these pathways. Interaction with the Notch signaling pathway The Notch signaling pathway is a well-established regulator of cellular homeostasis. 205 The interplay between IGF and Notch pathways across species has been reported. In the bovine mammary gland, anthelmintic treatment induces the upregulation of IGF1, which enhances epithelial cell proliferation while concurrently activating Notch target genes, suggesting that the IGF and Notch pathways act in concert to regulate glandular development. 206 Complementary studies in murine models have demonstrated that IGF1 modulates neuroinflammatory responses by reinforcing Notch signaling in astrocytes. 207 Specifically, IGF1 suppresses lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced reactive morphology, a hallmark of glial activation in neuroinflammation, through concurrent inhibition of NF-κB signaling and activation of the MAPK pathway, 207 highlighting a protective role for IGF-Notch crosstalk in the central nervous system under inflammatory stress. Endocrine modulation of IGF crosstalk Prolactin and estrogen exemplify distinct endocrine mechanisms that interface with IGF signaling in highly context-specific ways, influencing morphogenesis in normal development and oncogenic progression in pathology. In chick feather development, prolactin establishes a positive feedback loop by coupregulating IGFs and type III deiodinase (Dio3), enhancing local triiodothyronine (T3) activity. 208 This coordinated circuit drives epidermal-mesenchymal proliferation and feather morphogenesis. Conversely, estrogen receptor beta (ERβ) activates IGF1R signaling in lung cancer through dual mechanisms: transcriptional upregulation of IGF1R and direct potentiation of its kinase activity. 209 This forms an oncogenic axis in NSCLC, where estrogen further upregulates IGF1 expression and sustains IGF1R signaling via ERβ, promoting cell proliferation and tumor aggressiveness. 209 These interactions demonstrate how the estrogen-IGF1R axis is amenable to combination therapy (e.g., ER antagonists combined with IGF1R inhibitors). Collectively, the IGF system emerges as a central regulatory hub coordinating organism-wide homeostasis through its pleiotropic control of metabolic and cellular processes. This versatile network precisely regulates insulin secretion and cholesterol metabolism while transcriptionally modulating core cellular programs, proliferation, differentiation, and survival in a highly context-dependent manner. Its multitiered regulatory capacity, encompassing ligand bioavailability (via IGFBPs and proteases), receptor heterodimerization, adaptor-mediated signaling, extensive crosstalk with other pathways, and endocrine modulation, explains the broad pathophysiological consequences of IGF dysregulation. These span neurodegenerative diseases (via AKT/ERK-mediated neuroprotection), cardiovascular disorders (through endothelial nitric oxide synthase regulation and vascular homeostasis), diabetes (via pancreatic β-cell modulation and glucose metabolism control), and cancer (through unrestrained proliferation, survival, and metastasis). The system’s integrative functions encompass neuroprotective effects via AKT/ERK-mediated survival pathways, vascular homeostasis through endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS) regulation, glucose metabolism control by pancreatic β-cell modulation, and tissue growth restraint via cell cycle checkpoint interactions. Such pervasive physiological involvement, coupled with disease-specific pathway perturbations, positions IGF signaling as both a biomarker and therapeutic target for age-related pathologies (Fig. 6 ). Skeletal disorders IGF1 is essential for translating mechanical loading into anabolic bone formation. Global deletion of IGF1 results in reduced trabecular bone volume, cortical thickness, and bone mineral density (BMD), accompanied by impaired mechanotransduction and diminished osteoblast activity. 1 Similarly, genetic ablation of IGF1R in osteoblasts impaired endochondral bone formation. 229 Chondrocyte-specific IGF1R deletion results in shorter and narrower growth plate, impairing endochondral bone formation. 230 Endothelial-specific IGF1R deficiency disrupts microvascular homeostasis, impairs skeletal muscle perfusion, and reduces exercise endurance, suggesting implications for age-related sarcopenia. 231 Beyond skeleton development and homeostasis, the IGF-1R axis is critical for bone regeneration. Postnatal IGF1R deletion impairs fracture callus formation, delays union, and reduces biomechanical strength, highlighting its indispensable role in orchestrating the proliferative, angiogenic, and osteogenic phases of repair. 232 Among the IGFBPs, IGFBP4 modulates osteoblast differentiation and bone mass accrual, 233 while IGFBP2 knockout mice exhibit resistance to trabecular bone loss following gastrectomy. 234 IGFBP7 enhances vascular integrity by upregulating IGF1R expression on endothelial cells, activating PI3K–AKT signaling, and increasing zonula occludens-1 ( ZO-1) expression to stabilize endothelial junctions. 235 Delayed administration of IGFBP7 in cranial bone defect models enhances functional angiogenesis, vascular maturation and bone healing. 235 Metabolic disorders Gradual alterations in glucose homeostasis have been observed in rats with partial IGF1 knockdown. 236 Recent studies in ghrelin-deficient mice have revealed an unexpected role for IGF1 in glucose metabolism during fasting. While adult mice subjected to 60% calorie restriction for 6 days maintain glucose levels and survive acute starvation, ghrelin-deficient mice exhibit a 90% reduction in circulating IGF1 levels and severe hypoglycemia. 237 Administration of IGF1 restores glucose levels by enhancing lipolysis and hepatic gluconeogenesis, suggesting that IGF1 exerts anti-insulin effects during prolonged nutritional stress. 237 Furthermore, IGF1R conditional knockout has been associated with increased adipose tissue mass, primarily due to enhanced lipid accumulation. 238 Conversely, IGF1R knockout mice have a mild reduction in both white adipose tissue (WAT) and brown adipose tissue (BAT). 239 These results highlight the dual role of IGF1R in lipid metabolism. Conditional deletion of IGF1 mitigates aging-induced hepatic injury, implicating chrornic IGF1 signaling in age-related liver decline. 242 Paradoxically, IGF1R deletion confers protection against endoplasmic reticulum stress and cellular injury in cholestasis models, 243 suggesting that excessive or sustained IGF1R activation may exacerbate certain forms of acute hepatic damage. Hepatocyte proliferation and functional recovery during liver regeneration are impaired in mice with liver-specific IGF1R deletion, suggesting the essential role of the IGF1/IGF1R axis in promoting hepatic repair and regeneration. 244 Similarly, IGFBP1 knockout mice exhibit delayed liver regeneration following partial hepatectomy, 215 indicating that IGFBP1 is required for timely postinjury recovery. IGFBP1 plays detrimental role in MI, as its depletion is protective in both acute injury and long-term cardiac remodeling. 254 In mice with MI, knockdown of heart-specific IGFBP5 suppressed myocardial apoptosis and promoted cardiomyocyte proliferation, 255 suggesting that IGFBP5 is a potent therapeutic target for limiting post-infarct remodeling. Heart failure Murine models with global IGF knockout exhibit defective cardiac development, characterized by relative cardiac hypertrophy and impaired contractile function. 256 IGF1 administration diminishes pathological cardiac hypertrophy in obese rats by upregulating Na + /K + -ATPase activity and modulating the mTOR/S6K and IRS-1–AKT pathways. 257 More severe cardiac phenotypes emerge in mice with conditional knockout of both InSR and IGF1R in cardiac and skeletal muscle, leading to early-onset dilated cardiomyopathy, heart failure, and mortality within the first month of life. 258 These findings underscore the nonredundant, synergistic roles of insulin and IGF signaling in maintaining cardiac structure and function. Neurodegenerative disorders Alzheimer’s disease (AD), a progressive neurodegenerative disorder characterized by the pathological accumulation of amyloid-β (Aβ) plaques and neurofibrillary tangles consisting of hyperphosphorylated tau, is profoundly influenced by IGF1/IGF1R signaling. In rabbit model, IGF-1 plays protective role in AD, as it is repressed by Aβ42 and its restoration alleviates pathogenesis. 259 IGF2 has been shown to attenuate oxidative stress, amyloid plaques, and memory decline in AD mice by activating the PI3K/AKT/CREB signaling pathway. 260 Emerging evidence further highlights IGF2’s therapeutic potential in mitigating Aβ-mediated neurotoxicity; intracerebroventricular IGF2 infusion for 7 days significantly reduces hippocampal burden in APP/PSI mice, while a single intrahippocampal IGF2 injection rapidly decreases both plaque density and soluble Aβ concentration. 261 Beyond ligands, IGFRs also influence AD, since conditional ablation of IGF1R attenuates Aβ deposition in chronic sleep restriction (CSR) models by suppressing β-secretase 1 (BACE-1) activity, 262 and IGF1R deletion in mature neurons ameliorates AD pathology by enhancing Aβ clearance. 263 IGF2R knockdown impairs memory consolidation without affecting learning or retrieval. 264 Altered IGFBPs expression further contributes to AD progression, with reduced hippocampal IGFBP3 mRNA levels observed in APP/PS1 mice and elevated IGFBP5 expression localized to Aβ plaques and adjacent hippocampal/cortical regions in TgCRND8 mice. 265 Autoimmune disorders Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) Inhibiting insulin receptor substrate (IRS), a downstream target of IGF1R, significantly mitigates experimental arthritis in mice. 266 In mouse arthritis models, reduced IGF1R expression in synovial tissue (ST) is rescued by IL-27 and IL-35, 267 suggesting a regulatory interplay between IGF1 and cytokine networks that restrain disease progression. IGFBP3 inhibits NF-κB activation and induces apoptosis in RA fibroblast-like synoviocytes (FLS), thereby reducing the inflammatory response and bone destruction. 268 Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) Histopathological analyses of SLE-affected kidneys revealed increased transcriptional and translational expression of IGF1 and IGFBP2, with elevated serum IGFBP2 levels also noted in lupus mouse models. 269 In MRL/LPR mice, a well-established model of lupus, overexpression of IGF1 and IGFBP2 in glomerular cells correlates with renal dysfunction and morphological changes, 270 implicating these molecules in lupus nephritis. Sjögren’s syndrome (SS) Data on IGF axis involvement in SS remain limited but suggest a predominantly protective role in salivary gland homeostasis. Immunohistochemical studies in mice have demonstrated substantial IGF1 expression in saliva, 272 where it supports epithelial integrity and secretory function of the salivary gland. Pretreatment with IGF1 in mice exposed to ionizing radiation minimizes DNA damage in salivary acinar cells and alleviates symptoms associated with radiation therapy. 273 The age-related decline in IGF1 expression may contribute to salivary dysfunction and glandular atrophy observed in the elderly population. 274 Aging The inhibition of insulin/IGF1 signaling has emerged as one of the most conserved mechanisms for lifespan extension and delay of age-related pathologies across multiple species, 275 where loss-of-function mutations in the Daf-2 gene, an ortholog of mammalian IGF1/IGF1R, remarkably extended lifespan by up to twofold. In the fruit fly ( Drosophila melanogaster ), blocking IGF1R-like signaling by a mutation in an IRS also potentially prolongs lifespan. 276 In mammals, the effects exhibit pronounced sexual dimorphism. Decreased IGF1 signaling or IGF1R activity significantly increases both lifespan and health span predominantly in female mice. 276 Late-life administration of an IGF1R-specific monoclonal antibody further extends lifespan and health span selectively in female mice 277 (Table 1 ). Mice with deficiencies in GH secretion have low circulating IGF1 and live longer than wild-type littermates. 278 Comparative hepatic transcriptomic analysis in these long-lived dwarf models confirmed the downregulation of IGF1 expression. 279 Moreover, IGFBP3 serves as a biomarker and mediator of cellular senescence and aging, 280 with elevated levels associated with senescent cell accumulation and inflammatory signaling. PAPP-A serves as an enhancer of local IGF bioavailability. Genetic ablation of PAPP-A significantly extends lifespan in mice maintained on both standard and high-fat diets, while conditional, adult-onset PAPP-A deletion in normally sized mice also prolongs longevity without altering body size. 281 , 282 From bench to bedside: the evolving role of IGF/IGFR in human diseases IGF/IGFR signaling in carcinogenesis In the following we discuss roles of IGF axis components in pathogenesis, prognosis and therapy response in different types of human cancer. Acute myeloid leukemia (AML) IGF1R is overexpressed in AML patients, where posttranslational regulation modulates its activity, reinforcing autocrine signaling and PI3K–AKT activation, thereby promoting AML cell proliferation. 283 Moreover, it is reported that HOXA9 directly activates IGF1R expression, and its genetic knockdown significantly reduces IGF1R levels, suppressing leukemic proliferation and inducing apoptosis. 284 IGF1 drives AML cell proliferation primarily through IGF1R-dependent PI3K–AKT and ERK pathways, mirroring the frequent dysregulation of PI3K signaling observed in ~50% of AML cases. 284 Constitutive PI3K activation, which occurs in approximately 70% of AML samples, is largely sustained by autocrine IGF1/IGF1R signaling loops. 284 This dependency renders the axis pharmacologically vulnerable, as demonstrated by the antileukemic efficacy of PI3K inhibitors and neutralizing anti-IGF1R antibodies in preclinical models. 285 Beyond direct proliferative signaling, IGF1 promotes AML progression through ancillary mechanisms. For instance, ADAM28-mediated proteolytic cleavage of IGFBP3 enhances IGF1 bioavailability and downstream mitogenic effects. 286 IGF2 is highly expressed in CD34⁺ CD38⁻ AML stem-like cells, where its binding to IGF1R activates downstream AKT signaling, upregulating the pluripotency factor Nanog. 287 This axis critically sustains leukemia stem cell (LSC) proliferation and self-renewal, as genetic ablation of Nanog abolishes IGF2-driven leukemogenesis, 287 underscoring the therapeutic relevance of the IGF2/IGF1R/Nanog pathway in LSC maintenance. The IGFBP family exhibits context-dependent roles in AML. IGFBP1 upregulation impairs insulin/IGF1 signaling, exacerbating systemic insulin resistance while paradoxically supporting leukemic cell survival, potentially through inflammatory mediators such as TNFSF13B and IL-8. 288 Low baseline levels of IGFBP1 and IGFBP6 associate with improved progression-free survival (PFS). 289 IGFBP2 upregulation is linked to chemoresistance 290 and serves as an independent predictor of relapse. 291 In contrast, higher IGFBP5 positively correlates with overall survival. 292 Preclinical studies confirm that IGFBP5 overexpression or recombinant IGFBP5 treatment exerts tumor-suppressive effects and promotes chemosensitivity. 292 The prognostic significance of IGFBP7 in AML is also contradictory. One study reported that elevated IGFBP7 levels at diagnosis, a decline upon complete remission, and a rise at relapse, with high expression correlating with shorter overall survival. 293 Another cohort-based study found that reduced IGFBP7 expression is associated with poorer outcomes, aligning with experimental evidence that IGFBP7 promotes apoptosis and enhances chemosensitivity. 294 IGFBP7 has also been shown to induce differentiation and impair the survival of AML stem cells without adversely affecting normal hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells 295 (Fig. 7 ). IGF2-InsR interactions play a critical role in glucose regulation, and IGF2 overexpression causes nonislet cell hypoglycemia (NICH) through excessive insulin receptor activation. 341 While muscle-specific IGF1R knockout surprisingly maintains normal glucose tolerance, 342 systemic IGF1R deficiency triggers compensatory hypergrowth-hormonemia that paradoxically induces insulin resistance. 343 Heterozygous IGF1R mutations predispose patients to hypoglycemia. 344 Consistently, impaired IGF1R signaling (IGF-1 resistance) contributes to the pathogenesis of T2D. 343 The axis demonstrates remarkable metabolic flexibility, with tissue-specific effects and redundant pathways ensuring energy homeostasis. For lipid metabolism, IGF1 exerts dual control by simultaneously activating lipolysis through hormone-sensitive lipase stimulation while suppressing lipogenesis via inhibition of key transcriptional regulators such as sterol regulatory element–binding protein-1c (SREBP-1c). 345 This coordinated action mobilizes adipose triglyceride stores while preventing ectopic lipid accumulation, maintaining metabolic flexibility. 346 The system’s precision is further refined by IGFBPs, particularly IGFBP1/2, which serve as metabolic gatekeepers by modulating IGF1 bioavailability. Elevated IGFBP1 is associated with poor outcomes in metabolic diseases, 347 while deficiency predisposes patients to T2D and metabolic syndrome. 348 In gestational diabetes, IGFBP1 emerges as a sensitive biomarker, with low levels predicting adverse metabolic trajectories. 349 Notably, metabolic surgeries such as Roux-en-Y gastric bypass (RYGB) rapidly restore IGFBP1 levels, correlating with improved glucose homeostasis. 350 IGF/IGFR signaling in cardiovascular diseases The IGF/IGFR axis plays a crucial role in atherosclerosis pathogenesis. Local IGF signaling promotes vascular smooth muscle cell (VSMC) proliferation and migration into the intima, while reduced systemic IGF1 levels correlate with carotid atherosclerosis and an increased risk of acute coronary syndrome. 351 IGFBPs exhibit complex regulatory roles in this process; IGFBP5 accumulates in lesions and interacts with ECM components (PAI-1, SPP1) to promote plaque progression through a positive feedback loop with IGF signaling, 352 whereas IGFBP6 demonstrates atheroprotective properties, and its downregulation in atherosclerotic arteries 353 and endothelial cells exacerbates inflammation, an effect reversible by IGFBP6 overexpression. 252 These findings highlight the therapeutic potential of modulating specific IGF system components to target plaque formation and vascular inflammation. In MI, clinical evidence demonstrates that reduced circulating IGF1 levels correlate with poorer MI outcomes and increased risk of ischemic heart disease and stroke, while higher IGF1 concentrations appear cardioprotective. 354 , 355 IGFBPs show distinct patterns in MI; IGFBP1 serum levels are significantly elevated in acute MI patients, as detected by antibody array technology, 356 while IGFBP5 expression is markedly increased post-MI, 357 suggesting its potential involvement in postinfarction remodeling. In chronic heart failure, impaired IGF1 signaling contributes to maladaptive cardiac remodeling and compromised repair mechanisms. 358 IGFBP4 emerges as a clinically significant biomarker, demonstrating prognostic value in multiple cardiovascular contexts as an independent predictor of mortality and morbidity in acute heart failure, 359 as a risk stratifier for heart failure development post-STEMI, 360 and as an indicator of cardiovascular mortality risk in T1D patients 361 (Fig. 8 ). These findings collectively position IGFBP4 as both a pathophysiological mediator and valuable clinical tool for cardiovascular risk assessment, particularly in high-risk populations. IGF/IGFR signaling in neurodegenerative diseases Alzheimer’s disease AD pathogenesis involves complex dysregulation of the IGF signaling axis, with both protective and detrimental effects on neuronal survival and cognitive function. For instance, IGF1 demonstrates neuroprotective potential by reducing amyloid-β (Aβ) production through BACE1 downregulation 362 and mitigating tau hyperphosphorylation via GSK-3β inhibition. 363 Elevated IGF1R expression in the AD hippocampus and temporal cortex correlates with an impaired PI3K–AKT axis and elevated serine-phosphorylated IRS1, which is associated with Aβ burden and cognitive decline. 265 IGF2 exhibits neuroprotective properties by preserving cholinergic function through ChAT upregulation, 364 although its expression is reduced in AD brains. 260 The IGFBP family shows disease-modifying effects, with IGFBP2 associating with biomarkers of AD, 365 while IGFBP3 demonstrating context-dependent pro-apoptotic and neuroprotective roles. 366 IGFBP5 overexpression impairs memory retention by disrupting IGF1 signaling and brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) induction 367 (Fig. 8 ). Parkinson’s disease (PD) In PD, clinical observations reveal that reduced serum IGF1 levels correlate with fatigue in patients. 368 The mechanisms by which IGF signaling affects PD pathogenesis remain poorly understood. However, recent studies report that aging, a primary risk factor for PD development, 369 is closely associated with dysregulated IGF signaling, with IGF1R deletion shown to extend lifespan through neuroendocrine mechanisms. 370 At the cellular level, conditional IGF1/IGF1R knockout in adult neural stem cells preserves youthful olfactory bulb neurogenesis during aging, 371 implicating IGF signaling in maintaining neuronal plasticity. Genetic studies have identified specific IGF1 variants (p.Ile91Leu and p.Ala118Thr) that impair IGF1R binding or reduce circulating IGF1 levels, potentially contributing to exceptional longevity, while ongoing clinical trials are investigating antiaging therapeutics such as metformin, mTORC1 inhibitors, and GLP1R antagonists that may have relevance for PD treatment. 372 Notably, estrogen’s established neuroprotection in PD, appears to be mediated in part through synergistic activation of PI3K signaling with IGF1 to preserve nigrostriatal dopaminergic neurons 373 (Fig. 8 ). Huntington’s disease (HD) HD pathogenesis involves significant metabolic dysregulation, with emerging evidence highlighting the neuroprotective potential of IGF signaling pathways. While IGF1 does not predict cognitive decline in HD, it demonstrates therapeutic capacity by improving mitochondrial function and energy metabolism in HD human lymphoblasts. 374 More strikingly, IGF2 emerges as a key neuroprotective factor, showing a marked reduction in symptomatic versus presymptomatic patients. 375 Mechanistically IGF2 promotes neuronal structural integrity through actin remodeling and neurite outgrowth while enhancing mutant huntingtin (mHTT) clearance via dual pathways, AKT-mediated phosphorylation to reduce mHTT aggregates, and NF-κB-triggered extracellular vesicle secretion of mHTT 375 (Fig. 8 ), suggesting that IGF2 acts as a pivotal therapeutic target, with its ability to simultaneously address both the cytoskeletal abnormalities and protein aggregation hallmarks of HD pathology. IGF/IGFR signaling in autoimmune disorders Graves’ disease (GD) and thyroid-associated ophthalmopathy In GD, an autoimmune disorder characterized by stimulatory antibodies against the thyrotropin receptor (TSHR), 376 both B and T cells demonstrate IGF1R overexpression, 377 while systemic IGF1 levels are elevated, 378 suggesting fundamental involvement of the IGF pathway in disease development. TAO, a common extrathyroidal manifestation of GD, features orbital fibroblast activation and excessive hyaluronan production, with orbital fibroblasts from affected patients showing marked IGF1R overexpression. 379 Critically, IGF1R forms functional complexes with TSHR, and their crosstalk synergistically enhances hyaluronan secretion, 380 a key driver of TAO pathology. These findings have been translated into clinical therapeutics. The FDA-approved IGF1R inhibitor teprotumumab demonstrates efficacy in TAO treatment 37 (Fig. 8 ). Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) Clinical studies have revealed conflicting patterns of IGF1 and IGFBPs expression in RA patients, with reports of elevated IGFBP2, IGFBP3, and IGF1 in plasma and synovial fluid 381 contrasting with observations of decreased IGFBP3 382 and variable IGF1 levels in synovial fluid. 383 At the cellular level, IGF1R emerges as a key driver of disease progression, amplifying TNF-α-induced inflammation in synovial fibroblasts through ERK-1/2 activation 384 and enhancing T-cell activation via increased IL-10 production. 385 IGF1 demonstrates immunomodulatory potential, as evidenced by human amniotic mesenchymal stem cells engineered to overexpress IGF1 ameliorating collagen-induced arthritis through Treg expansion and Th17 suppression. 386 The system’s involvement extends to RA-associated cachexia, characterized by reduced serum IGF1/IGFBP1 387 and elevated adiponectin, 388 while genetic factors such as the TXNDC5 susceptibility gene exacerbate synovial fibroblast proliferation through IGF1 activation and IGFBP1 downregulation 389 (Fig. 8 ). Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) SLE is a complex autoimmune disorder characterized by systemic inflammation, loss of immune tolerance to self-antigens, and dysregulation of interferon responses, leading to multiorgan damage. 390 Studies have found no relation between components of the IGF1 axis and the pathogenesis or severity of SLE. 391 Unlike other autoimmune conditions, SLE patients show no significant differences in serum levels of IGF1, IGFBP3, or free IGF1 compared with age-matched healthy controls. 391 However, elevated serum levels of IGFBP2 have been consistently observed in SLE patients, 392 suggesting its potential role in disease pathology (Fig. 8 ). Systemic sclerosis (SSc) Clinical studies have revealed elevated serum levels of IGF1 and IGFBP3 in SSc patients, with particularly pronounced IGF1 mRNA overexpression in the skin of diffuse cutaneous SSc (dcSSc) variants. 393 In pulmonary manifestations, the leading cause of SSc mortality, the IGF axis shows distinct activation patterns; bronchoalveolar lavage fluid IGF1 levels correlate with interstitial lung disease severity, 394 while fibrotic lung lesions exhibit marked IGF2 overexpression accompanied by increased IGF2 production in primary lung fibroblasts. 395 IGF1 and IGF2 synergistically promote fibroblast activation through sustained stimulation of collagen synthesis, inhibition of matrix metalloproteinases, and upregulation of fibrogenic mediators, forming a self-amplifying loop of extracellular matrix deposition. 395 Sjögren’s syndrome (SS) SS, a chronic autoimmune disorder, primarily affects exocrine glands, leading to severe dryness of the eyes and oral cavity due to dysfunction of the lacrimal and salivary glands. IGF/IGFR signaling in rare genetic diseases Genetic alterations in IGF signaling pathways produce distinct clinical phenotypes. Disruption of the GH–IGF axis underlies Laron syndrome, highlighting a critical role of IGF1 in a rare genetic growth disorder. 400 Neurodevelopmental manifestations of IGF1 deficiency range from microcephaly and cognitive impairment 401 , 402 to emerging therapeutic potential of IGF1 in Rett syndrome and Fragile X syndrome. 403 The pathway’s pleiotropic effects extend to rare conditions such as Mulibrey-Nanism syndrome via ubiquitin pathway crosstalk. 404 IGFALS mutations cause ALS deficiency, a rare genetic disorder featured by lower circulating IGF1 and IGBFP3 levels and postnatal growth defect. 405 These genotype-phenotype correlations highlight the IGF system’s developmental importance and therapeutic potential, with recombinant IGF1 already established as standard therapy for Laron syndrome and investigational for other disorders. Precise targeting remains crucial given the dual roles of this pathway in growth promotion and tumorigenesis. IGF/IGFR signaling in other diseases The IGF/IGF1R signaling axis demonstrates diverse protective and pathogenic roles across many other disease states. For instance, IGF1 promotes peripheral nerve regeneration through Schwann cell-mediated myelination 406 , 407 and preserves cerebrovascular function in cerebral small vessel disease by enhancing perfusion and blood‒brain barrier integrity via IGF1R/Wnt7b/β-catenin signaling. 408 Reproductive system disorders such as pelvic organ prolapse feature IGF1 deficiency, where it normally regulates collagen metabolism in vaginal fibroblasts through PI3K–AKT signaling. 409 Acromegaly management benefits from IGF1 level monitoring during lanreotide therapy. 410 Psychiatrically, reduced IGF1 correlates with worse posttraumatic symptom severity and mood disorders. 411 These findings collectively highlight the pleiotropic roles of the IGF system in tissue repair, metabolic regulation, and disease modulation across diverse organ systems. IGF/IGF1R signaling as a therapeutic target: current advances and clinical prospects Non-oncologic therapeutic applications IGF1 signaling axis has emerged as a potential therapeutic target for multiple disease, including metabolic disorders, cardiovascular disease, and neurodegeneration. In metabolic disorders, treatment therapy has mainly focused on IGF replacement strategies, specifically in rare insulin-resistance syndromes, whereas inhibition of IGF1R has not translated into effective therapies due to metabolic adverse effects such as hyperglycemia. 412 , 413 In CVD contexts, IGF1 has been reported as cardioprotective both in small and large animal models, with IGF1 administration improving post infarction cardiac remodeling by targeting myeloid cells. 253 , 414 Additionally, IGF1 exhibits pro-angiogenic, and anti-apoptotic properties in preclinical models, although clinical translation remains limited. 415 , 416 Similarly, in neurodegenerative diseases such as AD and PD, IGF1 promotes neuronal survival and reduces neuroinflammation, but therapeutic applications remain largely investigational. 417 , 418 Furthermore, pharmacological inhibition of IGF1R signaling holds therapeutic potential for pulmonary fibrosis, 419 and demonstrates sex-specific geroprotective effects, extending female lifespan by 9% through reduced neoplasms and inflammation. 277 Clinically, mecasermin (rhIGF1) has been approved by the FDA for the treatment of pediatric growth disorders, 420 while teprotumumab (an IGF1R–targeting antibody) represents a major translational success in TAO. 37 Linsitinib is currently being developed to treat TED by Sling Therapeutics and has shown positive topline in phase 2b/3 clinical trials. 16 Therapeutic strategies targeting IGF/IGF1R signaling in oncology Medications targeting IGF/IGF1R signaling axis in oncology can be broadly classified into: monoclonal antibodies (mAbs), small molecules TKIs, and emerging ligand-neutralizing approaches. Monoclonal antibodies Considerable efforts have been devoted to development of mAbs targeting the IGF/IGF1R axis. Various IGF1R-targeting mAbs, including figitumumab, 421 , 422 cixutumumab, 423 ganitumab, 424 , 425 dalotuzumab, 426 istiratumab, 427 and R1507 428 have undergone clinical evaluation for multiple cancers, however the outcomes of trials have been conflicting. Combination therapies have also yielded inconsistent outcomes, with some studies reporting improved responses, 425 , 429 while others failed to show significant clinical benefit, 422 , 430 – 432 albeit with notable adverse effects, including hyperglycemia, hematologic toxicities, and autoimmune reactions. Tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) TKIs targeting the IGF1R/InsR axis present both opportunities and challenges in cancer therapeutics. Many ATP-competitive IGF-1R TKIs exploit 84% kinase domain homology between IGF1R and InsR, 433 yet their varying selectivity profiles form a therapeutic double-edged sword. While designed for IGF1R preference, collateral inhibition of InsR can trigger hyperglycemia and insulin resistance. 434 – 437 In preclinical GBM models, PQ401 suppresses migration, 438 GSK1838705A induces apoptosis in vitro and in xenografts, 439 PPP achieves intracranial regression, 440 and NVP-AEW541 shows synergistic lethality with Bcr-Abl inhibitors. 441 The dual IGF1R/InsR inhibitor BMS-536924 retains activity against temozolomide-resistant GBM, 442 while the IGF-Trap strategy broadly inhibits phosphorylation in multiple carcinomas, 443 , 444 highlighting the delicate balance required in clinical translation. Ligand-neutralizing strategies Emerging ligand neutralization using agents such as MEDI-573 and xentuzumab has demonstrated preclinical antitumor activity and has entered into clinical trials. 157 , 445 Limitations, clinical implications, and future perspectives of IGF1R-targeted therapies in cancer As discussed above, several drugs targeting the IGF1R signaling pathway have entered clinical trials in oncology (Table 2 ). However, despite encouraging preclinical rationale, most IGF1R-targeted therapeutic agents have failed to achieve clinical success in cancer patients. In this section, we discuss the reasons underlying their failures together with clinical implications and future perspectives. However, its phase III trial failed due to adverse effects and poor responses in unselected patient populations, leading to termination of clinical development. 421 , 422 To reconcile this discrepancy, it has been suggested that the canonical paradigm of IGF1R as a classic RTK is insufficient to explain the clinical observations and outcomes induced by IGF1R activation. IGF1R can engage G protein–coupled receptor signaling machinery, including β-arrestins and G protein-coupled receptor kinases (GRKs), thereby activating MAPK and AKT pathways independently of receptor autophosphorylation. 446 – 448 Accordingly, figitumumab not only inhibits IGF1–induced receptor phosphorylation and promotes receptor internalization and degradation but also paradoxically induces β-arrestin1- and GRK-dependent ERK1/2 activation, functioning as a biased agonist. 449 , 450 Subsequent studies in esophageal squamous cell carcinoma further demonstrated that figitumumab suppresses tumor growth and IGF1–driven survival programs and enhances chemosensitivity; however, concomitant β-arrestin- and GRK-mediated ERK1/2 activation limits its antitumor efficacy, as ERK inhibition potentiates figitumumab activity. 451 Moreover, figitumumab induces more robust IGF1R downregulation than IGF1 itself, a process critically dependent on β-arrestin1 and GRKs. 451 The limited efficacy of IGF1R-targeted agents is also attributed to compensatory signaling via InsR (specifically IR-A) and the InsR/IGF1R hybrid receptor. 428 , 452 – 455 IR-A binds IGF2 with high affinity, and its activation can sustain oncogenic signaling even when IGF1R is inactivated. Evidence indicates that tumor cells bypass IGF1R inhibition by directing growth signals through the IGF2–IR-A pathway. 428 IGF1R–specific mAbs leave this IGF2–IR-A axis unchecked. Hybrid receptors further complicate therapeutic targeting and may not be fully inhibited by IGF1R antibodies if the InsR half remains signaling-competent, 456 suggesting that future strategies must consider InsR signaling to overcome resistance against IGF1R-targeted therapies. Crosstalk between IGF1R and other plasma membrane receptors, including EGFR, 149 HER2, 196 and integrins, 457 – 459 orchestrates redundant signaling networks that represent a major challenge to IGF1R-targeted therapies while also offering opportunities for rational cotargeting of compensatory receptor pathways. Furthermore, IGF1R–targeted antibodies upregulate GH, IGF1 and insulin concentrations. 33 , 460 Notably, GH can counteract IGF1R blockade by activating oncogenic PI3K–AKT and MAPK cascades, 461 while elevated IGF1 could compete with mAbs for binding to IGF1R. Constitutive activation of IGF1R downstream signaling pathways can also exert therapeutic resistance. For example, PI3K mutations or PTEN loss sustains pathway activation independent of IGF1R. 462 – 464 Moreover, inhibition of AKT or mTOR induces feedback elevation of IGF1R, adding complexity to therapeutic strategies. 465 It is plausible that mutations in RAS–RAF trigger MAPK activation, regardless of IGF1R blockade. 466 TKIs such as linsitinib and BMS-754807 target IGF1R but also block InsR owing to their structural similarity. 435 , 467 This dual inhibition leads to hyperglycemia, necessitating dose reductions that decrease clinical efficacy. 435 , 468 Additionally, the multi-kinase inhibitor BMS-754807 was seen to inhibit kinases beyond IGF1R. 469 In summary, metabolic toxicity and insufficient kinase selectivity have plagued IGF1R-targeted drug development. Additionally, inadequate trial design probably contributes to failure of IGF1R therapeutics. Early signals of encouraging response were not sustained in later studies, which were often conducted in unselected populations without biomarker-driven patient stratification, a key feature of successful targeted cancer therapies. 470 In addition, as discussed above, noncoding RNAs critically fine-tune IGF1R expression and signaling output. Therapeutic targeting RNA-based networks, through antisense oligonucleotides, CRISPR-based editing, and miRNA mimics, will likely provide more precise strategies to counter resistance against IGF1R-targeted therapies and identify predictive biomarkers across multiple cancer types. Conclusion and perspective The IGF/IGFR signaling axis is an evolutionarily conserved pathway that governs cellular proliferation, survival, and metabolism while coordinating growth, tissue repair, and systemic homeostasis across metazoans. Its biological scope extends far beyond somatic growth regulation, functioning as an integrative hub that processes endocrine, paracrine, and autocrine inputs through a multilayered architecture of ligands, receptors, binding proteins, proteases, and extensive pathway crosstalk. This structural and functional complexity underpins its central role in physiology, while its dysregulation drives a broad spectrum of human diseases. A fundamental conceptual advance is the recognition that IGF signaling operates not as a linear ligand–receptor cascade but as a signaling hub embedded within a broader interactome. This network includes insulin, EGF, PDGF, VEGF, JAK–STAT, TGF-β/BMP, Wnt/β-catenin, Notch, HER2, ROR1, and endocrine modulators such as estrogen and prolactin. These interactions are context-dependent and functionally non-additive, reshaping signaling amplitude, duration, and biological output. Crosstalk with insulin signaling, for instance, influences metabolic homeostasis through receptor overlap and hybrid receptor formation, whereas interactions with EGFR, HER2, and PDGFR sustain mitogenic signaling and therapeutic resistance in cancer. Reciprocal regulation with TGF-β/BMP and JAK–STAT pathways can redirect IGF activity from anabolic or regenerative functions toward fibrogenic, inflammatory, or invasive programs depending on cell type and disease state. Consequently, the outcome of IGF pathway activation cannot be inferred solely from receptor abundance or ligand levels but must be interpreted within its broader signaling and tissue context. This context dependence is starkly illustrated in disease. In cancer, the IGF axis supports proliferation, stemness, invasion, metastasis, angiogenesis, and therapy resistance, yet the contribution of individual components varies across tumor types and stages. IGF1R overexpression correlates with poor prognosis in several malignancies, including acute myeloid leukemia, non-small cell lung cancer, hepatocellular carcinoma, and glioblastoma, but functional outcomes are shaped by coexisting genetic lesions, compensatory receptor usage, downstream pathway wiring, and microenvironmental inputs. Similarly, IGFBPs exhibit dualistic roles, functioning as either tumor suppressors or promoters depending on their abundance, proteolytic processing, subcellular localization, and receptor-independent activities. Beyond oncology, similar duality prevails. In cardiovascular disease, IGF signaling can stabilize plaques and promote cardiac repair or, under different conditions, drive maladaptive remodeling. In the nervous system, IGF ligands are generally neuroprotective, yet chronic or compartment-specific dysregulation may accelerate neurodegeneration, impair proteostasis, or alter stress responses. In autoimmune and fibrotic diseases, the axis may either restrain inflammation or fuel pathological fibroblast activation and matrix deposition. Animal models have been indispensable for resolving these paradoxical roles. Genetic ablation, tissue-specific knockouts, ligand replacement, and pharmacological interventions collectively demonstrate that the physiological consequences of IGF signaling are exquisitely dependent on developmental timing, cell lineage, sex, and organ environment. In skeletal tissues, IGF1 and IGF1R are essential for mechanotransduction, endochondral ossification, and fracture repair. In metabolic systems, tissue-specific knockouts reveal remarkable flexibility, with compensation, redundancy, and noncanonical effects on glucose and lipid handling. In liver, cardiovascular, and neurodegenerative models, both beneficial and detrimental outcomes have been documented, underscoring that signaling magnitude, duration, and anatomical compartment are critical determinants of phenotype. These findings argue against simplistic “inhibit-or-activate” therapeutic paradigms and instead support nuanced strategies that consider local signaling dynamics and systemic context. Clinical translation of these insights has yielded important lessons. Although agents such as ganitumab (IGF1R monoclonal antibody) and FDA-approved mecasermin (recombinant IGF1) have demonstrated efficacy, their utility is limited by compensatory InsR activation and tissue-specific toxicities. Emerging strategies, including CRISPR-engineered IGFBP decoys, stroma-targeted nanotherapeutics, and AI-driven prediction of receptor phosphorylation thresholds, offer promising avenues to overcome these barriers. Future research must prioritize elucidation of IGF/IGFR crosstalk with senescence-associated secretomes and gut microbiota-derived metabolites, addressing critical gaps in cachexia-associated malignancies and autoimmune encephalopathies. A precision medicine framework integrating single-cell spatial transcriptomics with dynamic IGF1 flux modeling will be essential to develop temporally controlled therapies that reconcile the dichotomous roles of this pathway in health and disease.
