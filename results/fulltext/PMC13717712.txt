INTRODUCTION In the same way that laser diffraction applies visible light to striated muscles to measure their inter-sarcomeric spacing at micrometer scales, so X-ray diffraction applies light with angstrom wavelengths to measure intra-sarcomeric spacing at nanometer scales. These measurements result from the scattering of X-rays by electrons within the semi-crystalline order of sarcomeric proteins, namely of actin, myosin and regulatory proteins that ultimately form the biomolecular basis for all muscle phenomena (see Fig. 1A , B ) ( Ma and Irving, 2022 ; Reconditi, 2006 ; Brunello and Fusi, 2024 ). Historically, muscle X-ray diffraction methods utilized lab-based X-ray sources to identify the actin and myosin helical arrangements and hexagonal lattice order using long exposure (~hours) of intact frog sartorius muscles, known for their excellent diffraction quality ( Huxley and Brown, 1967 ). With the advent of synchrotron light sources providing X-rays with at least one million times more photon flux, modern diffraction techniques have pushed the boundaries with shorter exposures (~milliseconds) and higher signal strength ( Huxley, 2004 ; Iwamoto, 2019 ; Squire, 2016 ). The higher flux equates directly to improved measurement sensitivity of weaker diffraction patterns, which allows for broader experimental applications. These include time-resolved dynamics by allocating the improved sensitivity to shorter exposures, small fibers from tissue in limited supply that diffract weakly simply because there is less matter available, permeabilized preparations with increased disorder relative to intact samples, or fibers harboring myopathies that reduce the semi-crystalline order. Synchrotron muscle X-ray diffraction is increasingly used as a core component of the muscle biologist’s experimental toolbox to directly measure sarcomeric protein organization of both skeletal and cardiac muscle fibers under near-physiological conditions, often in conjunction with mechanics experiments ( Ma and Irving, 2022 ; Brunello and Fusi, 2024 ; Squire and Knupp, 2021 ; Powers et al., 2021 ; Iwamoto, 2019 ; Kuehn et al., 2025 ; Hill et al., 2025 ). That structure–function relationships can be simultaneously determined and correlated is a powerful tool and adds a dimension of analysis to study and better model muscle phenomena under active research such as, but not limited to, residual force enhancement/depression ( Hessel et al., 2024b ; Joumaa et al., 2021 ), work loop dynamics ( Pearson et al., 2025 ), activation/deactivation dynamics ( Gong et al., 2022 ; Hill et al., 2022 ), the Frank–Starling law ( Reconditi et al., 2017 ), spontaneous oscillatory contractions ( Kono et al., 2020 ) and drug discovery applications ( Pearson et al., 2025 ; Gollapudi et al., 2021 ). Yet, because only a handful of beamlines worldwide are available for muscle diffraction, synchrotron experiments are generally inaccessible to the majority of muscle biologists. We present methodological advances at the Life Science X-ray Scattering (LiX) beamline of the National Synchrotron Light Source II (NSLS-II) to run high-throughput muscle X-ray diffraction experiments in a ‘plug-and-play’ configuration with rapid sample change and semi-automated data processing. The express intent is to lower barriers of entry to synchrotron experiments for muscle biologists by building on developments made at several beamlines worldwide over the past 20+ years: ID02 at the European Synchrotron Radiation Facility (ESRF) ( Narayanan et al., 2018 ), I22 at the Diamond Light Source ( Smith et al., 2021 ), BioCAT at the Advanced Photon Source (APS) ( Barrea et al., 2014 ), BL30XU at SPring-8 ( Iwamoto, 2019 ), SAXS at Elettra ( Griffiths et al., 2002 ), and BioSAXS at the Cornell High Energy Synchrotron Source (CHESS) ( Koubassova et al., 2025 ). We explicitly focused on efficiently running steady-state imaging of permeabilized and intact muscle fibers at fixed temperature, which are common and technically simpler experiments already validated at other beamlines, yet foundational to muscle science. Preliminaries We proceed with a pedagogical section to aid new beamline users in reading and navigating diffraction patterns by way of graphically introducing the prerequisite building blocks (coordinate frame, hexagonal lattice and discontinuous helices) without diving deeply into the mathematical foundations. For more in-depth reading, we highly recommend the excellent work of Squire (1981) and recent comprehensive reviews from which this section is heavily derived ( Ma and Irving, 2022 ; Squire and Knupp, 2021 ). Coordinate frame Muscles are idealized as a lattice of actin and myosin filaments which lend naturally to a cylindrical coordinate frame ( r , z ,ϕ) in real space (see Fig. 1D ) where the filaments elongate in the axial direction z , the filament thicknesses expand along the radial direction r , and the lattice structure spans the r ϕ cross-sectional plane where ϕ is the angular coordinate. The diffraction pattern I ( q r ,q z ) is a 2D intensity measurement where q r and q z are the radial and axial coordinates in reciprocal space, respectively. The third angular coordinate does not appear in the diffraction pattern because of cylindrical symmetry arising from the random ϕ-orientation of myofibrils that average out at larger scales. Intuitively, this means that we generally would not expect a muscle fiber’s diffraction pattern to change if we were to rotate it around its long axis. The diffraction pattern physically measures the scattering of X-rays by electrons within the sample such that an intensity peak centered at any particular coordinate, say qr =2π/40 nm and qz =2π/14.3 nm, is read as a strong periodic structure repeating every 40 nm in the muscle’s cross-section and 14.3 nm along the filaments (for typical values for the lattice and myosin, see Fig. 1B ). These repeats are referred to as d -spacings by convention and defined in terms of q -values as q =2π/ d . Hexagonal lattice Actin and myosin filaments interlace and pack along a muscle’s cross-sectional area as a 2D hexagonal lattice. Borrowing terminology from the study of crystal structures, the hexagonal lattice itself is completely defined by two basis vectors, and any plane within this lattice is defined in Miller index by two integers ( h , k ). In muscles, there are two principal planes of interest ( Fig. 1B , D ). The (1,0) plane is composed of only myosin filaments and the (1,1) plane is composed of two actin filaments per myosin filament. The planes manifest in the diffraction pattern as sharp reflections or intensity peaks ( Fig. 1E ) along the equator, a line defined as the intensity taken along the radial axis I ( qr , qz =0). The d -spacing corresponding to the (1,0) reflection (denoted d 1,0) thus directly measures the distance between neighboring myosin filaments, which tends to decrease as crossbridges bind and compress the lattice during isometric force generation. The intensity ratio (IR) is defined as the ratio between the integrated area of two reflections I 1,1/ I 1,0, which measures the relative distribution of electron density (or approximate mass) between the (1,1) and (1,0) planes ( Malinchik and Yu, 1995 ) such that higher values equate to a mass transfer from myosin filaments to actin filaments. This mass transfer arises in various ways that include crossbridge binding and ordered-to-disordered transitions of myosin heads on the filament backbone ( Ma and Irving, 2022 ; Ma et al., 2023 ). The equatorial reflections thus provide a quantifiable proxy for crossbridge dynamics based on lattice compression and mass transfer between actin and myosin filaments. Discontinuous helices Actin and myosin filaments are both discontinuous helices in that they are formed by a helical arrangement of discrete monomer subunits. Their individual diffraction patterns take on cross-shaped profiles centered on the meridian, the line taken along the axial coordinate I ( q r =0, q z ). The cross-shaped profiles are organized along horizontal layer lines that increase along the axial coordinate every 1/ R steps, where R is the axial repeat of the helix ( Fig. 1F ). Furthermore, they repeat on the meridian every 1/ c steps, where c is the axial repeat of the monomer subunit ( Fig. 1G ). In vertebrate muscles, ( R , c ) is approximately (42.9 nm, 14.3 nm) for myosin and (35.9 nm, 2.73 nm) for actin. Fig. 1(H , I) draws the idealized diffraction patterns for both actin and myosin at the same scale and with their layer lines indexed. Notably, the actin diffraction pattern appears empty on several layer lines because actin is a non-integer helix (i.e. it requires several helical turns to complete one cycle of monomer subunits). Reading a diffraction pattern Putting all the building blocks together, we can read a muscle X-ray diffraction pattern to a first approximation as a superposition of the idealized actin and myosin helices and their hexagonal lattice packing. The resulting pattern is crowded and contains two independent sets of layer lines that can initially be difficult to find. Here, the meridional reflection on the 3rd myosin layer line (M3) and the off-meridional reflection on the 6th actin layer line (AL6) serve as consistent landmarks and can be used to estimate actin and myosin axial spacings (see Fig. 1I ). Many regions of the diffraction pattern contain multiple overlapping reflections that are not easily separated. During analysis, one typically isolates regions of the pattern with a single reflection and tracks them across perturbative conditions (e.g. pre/post-drug incubation, healthy/diseased samples and stretched/shortening cycles) to infer protein function, drug efficacy and toxicity, disease pathology, or otherwise evaluate muscle biomolecular states based solely on structural measurements. There are many features this first approximation does not capture, such as the so-called ‘forbidden’ meridional reflections, peak splitting due to interference between half-sarcomeres, and troponin reflections ( Ma and Irving, 2022 ; Squire and Knupp, 2021 ), but it forms an approachable baseline for new users to build further upon. MATERIALS AND METHODS LiX configuration The beamline accommodates high-throughput experiments on biological samples and runs a variety of experiments across multiple fields ( DiFabio et al., 2016 ; Yang et al., 2020 , 2021 , 2022 ; Yang, 2024 ). The beam spot size at the sample (5–500 μm) and X-ray energy (7–18 keV, 0.07–0.2 nm wavelength) can be adjusted based on experimental needs. Furthermore, the beamline is equipped with simultaneous data acquisition on wide-angle (Pilatus3 900k) and small-angle (Pilatus3 1M) detectors whose distance from the sample can be set to cover the desired range in q r and q z . For muscle diffraction, the beamline operates under a scanning configuration at ~15 keV, ~0.08 nm wavelength, a photon flux of ~5×10 10 photons s −1 at the sample, and small-angle detector positioned ~4.3 m downstream from the sample to cover the entire range from 0.005 Å −1 to 0.15 Å −1 in q -values or equivalently from 4 nm to 120 nm in d -spacing. The d -spacing relates to beam parameters at small angles by d =2π/ q ≈λ D / a , where D is the sample-to-detector distance, λ is the wavelength and a is the distance of a feature of interest on the detector to the beam center. The beam spot size, typically between 25 and 100 μm, is optimized for the muscle type and experiments by controlling the divergence-defining aperture and compound refractive lens upstream of the sample. Samples are mounted on an open-air sample stage adjusted to be as compact as the mounting hardware allows (~2 cm) to minimize signal degradation due to air scattering. A camera placed in-line with the X-ray beam provides precise spatial positioning of the muscle sample relative to the beam. Muscle X-ray diffraction hardware Although X-ray data acquisition is the core component, muscle samples require only a few minutes on the beamline to produce a diffraction pattern, a minor fraction of time when compared with other steps such as sample loading, calibration and incubation times. Thus, to maximize throughput without compromising on rigor or data quality, we optimized the hardware at LiX to run multiple muscle mechanics experiments in parallel. We built a stand-alone muscle mechanics rig that can be mounted onto the beamline with a preloaded muscle sample only when needed for X-ray exposure, and then quickly dismounted and relocated to a lab bench for all other steps. This approach minimizes deadtime at the beamline. Therefore, throughput is limited not by instrumentation but by experimental design, user experience and skilled researchers available to run the experiments. The upper bound on throughput under optimal conditions (single-condition experiments, sufficient skilled researchers continuously prepping samples and loading them onto rigs, and multiple rigs running in parallel) is around 10 samples per hour. In practice, experienced users running single-condition experiments at LiX have processed 80–120 samples per day. We designed the muscle mechanics rig as a mobile, stand-alone modular unit with a rapid mount/dismount mechanism and equipment/computers attached to a portable power station to ensure continuous power during transport to and from the beamline. These rigs are shown in Fig. 2A – C , and are available to all users running muscle experiments at LiX. Their modularity accommodates the wide range of muscle experiments by allowing piecewise selection of equipment available at LiX, such as force transducers, muscle length motors and temperature controllers, plus user-provided equipment such as electrodes, solution exchangers and oxygen bubblers. The mobility and mounting mechanism are based on optomechanical parts and allow for rapid sample interchanges and sample manipulation off the beamline. The muscle samples themselves are attached to two separate 3-axis linear micromanipulators via specialized clamps made from hypodermic needles. Other methods of attachment, such as T-clips, hooks, sutures and glue, will also work with minor modifications ( Hessel et al., 2026 ). When mounted to the beamline, muscle samples rest inside a temperature-controlled solution bath coupled to a motorized z -stage that moves the bath out of the beam’s path during data acquisition but leaves the muscle unperturbed. By imaging the diffraction pattern while the muscle is held in air, we avoid signal degradation due to absorption by buffer solutions and bath windows. Care is also taken to ensure that the muscle sample is in-air for no longer than 20 s. Multiple temperature controlled solution baths are available and can be quickly exchanged to switch between solution types as needed based on experimental design. For muscle samples that require continuous exposure to solution (e.g. actively contracting intact muscles), the diffraction pattern is acquired with the solution bath still in place. To accommodate these situations, we designed the solution baths with a narrow form factor and thin Kapton windows so that the X-ray beam travels through as little buffer solution as possible. Data acquisition and processing Imaging a muscle sample’s diffraction pattern follows one of several approaches. The first is to identify a single high-quality spot by running an initial pass at very low X-ray exposures across a wide area and then imaging the selected spot multiple times across different experimental conditions at higher X-ray exposures. This strategy provides exceptionally clean diffraction patterns but risks tissue damage as a result of repeated X-ray exposures and is best used for experimental designs with a single or minimal number of conditions. Additionally, the selected spot is often in a similar region across all samples tested, so the data do not reflect spatial heterogeneities present in the tissue. A second approach is to scan a large portion of the muscle under continuous X-ray exposure to generate a single or few diffraction patterns. Exceedingly long X-ray exposures without tissue damage are possible here because no single region is overexposed, and the resulting diffraction patterns have excellent signal-to-noise ratio. The major drawback is an imaging artifact similar to motion-blur, caused by the superposition of spatially heterogeneous sections within a muscle sample. Thus, this approach is best used for homogeneous muscle samples known to have long-range spatial order or for weakly diffracting muscle samples that necessitate long exposures. To optimize the benefits of both spot selection and scanning imaging approaches, we take a third approach at LiX that acquires many pseudo-spot, short-exposure images as the beam moves relative to the muscle in a raster area scan. Using a beam spot size that is small (25–100 μm) relative to muscle samples (typically several millimeters in length and several hundred micrometers in diameter) and exposures ranging from 100 ms to 1 s, each muscle sample generates dozens to hundreds of images. These images are merged together in post-processing to form one final image per muscle sample per condition. The post-processing pipeline is fully automated using custom Python scripts that correct each image for fiber orientation (fibers within a bundle are not always in perfect alignment) and filters out low quality images based on equatorial reflections (see Fig. 2D ). This approach adds granularity to the data acquisition and accommodates spatial heterogeneity of muscle samples due to images containing connective tissues, misaligned or crossed fibers, or regions damaged during preparation that diffract poorly, all of which are removed prior to merging. The merged images are then semi-autonomously analyzed to measure lattice spacing based on equatorial (1,0) and (1,1) reflections, myosin head placement based on equatorial intensity ratio I 1,1 / I 1,0 , and myosin and actin repeats along the filaments based on their nth order meridional reflections. This step emulates many features of MuscleX, an open-sourced suite of data tools commonly applied by users to process muscle diffraction data ( Jiratrakanvong et al., 2024 ), but is optimized for LiX data structures and real-time analysis. It requires an initial calibration process to select background subtraction parameters and reasonable bounds for Gaussian curve fitting. We note here that these parameters vary depending on muscle types, experimental conditions and tissue quality, so the process currently involves some user curation. Initial data processing is performed in real-time at the beamline to enable on-the-spot adjustments as needed, such as scanning a different area on the sample, tuning exposure settings or modifying the experimental protocol to maximize diffraction quality. The entire muscle data processing pipeline (see Fig. 2D ) is open-sourced and available from GitHub ( https://github.com/NSLS-II-LIX/lixtools ). Curated preset parameters that been validated across several muscle types are available. They consist of parameters for the pre-merging quality checks based on equators and post-merge peak measurement based on Gaussian fits of (1,0), (1,1), M3 and AL6. In most scenarios, the preset parameters generate publication-quality diffraction patterns as .tiff files that users can further analyze in their software of choice, including MuscleX, or within LiX’s analysis pipeline to measure other peaks of interest. The analysis pipeline is also designed so that users can tweak the presets to suit their experiment. Muscle study of fMyBP-C To stress test the technical capabilities and throughput of LiX, we repeated an archetypal experimental design in which we compared diffraction patterns taken before and after a treatment (here, selective proteolytic removal of a myofilament protein). In the original study, conducted at the BioCAT beamline of the APS ( Hessel et al., 2024a ), defined the role of fast myosin-binding protein-C (fMyBP-C) in mediating myosin dynamic forces of skeletal muscle by using the genetically modified mouse line SnoopC2, which was specifically engineered to controllably cleave and wash out N-terminal fMyBP-C by incubation with tobacco etch virus protease (TEVp). Permeabilized fiber bundle preparations of SnoopC2 psoas muscles were imaged before and after TEVp treatment. The results demonstrated that fMyBP-C cleavage expanded the actomyosin lattice, stretched the filaments and shifted more myosin heads from the thick filament to thin filaments. Here, we repeated the experiment using permeabilized extensor digitorum longus (EDL) fibers also prepared from SnoopC2 mice. Both EDL and psoas muscles are 90% fast twitch in mouse ( Song et al., 2021 ; Baby et al., 2025 ), and thus we expected EDL to show a similar response to psoas upon fMyBP-C cleavage, at least in terms of direction but not necessarily in absolute value or magnitude. We went further than the original study by introducing mavacamten after fMyBP-C cleavage. Mavacamten is a myosin deactivation agent with a known effect of shifting myosin heads back onto the thick filament backbone in both cardiac and skeletal muscles ( Kuehn et al., 2025 ; Koubassova et al., 2025 ). This further stress tests LiX capabilities in running complex experimental designs and, in this case, with a commonly used agent in muscle research. The Hessel et al. (2024a) study on SnoopC2 psoas tissue consisted of 34 fiber bundles, held at 2.7 μm sarcomere length (SL) and room temperature, with X-ray diffraction data taken at two conditions in series (control, +TEVp) and 1 s total exposure. The study here at LiX on EDL tissue consisted of 29 fiber bundles, also held at 2.7 μm SL and room temperature, but with of X-ray diffraction data taken at three conditions in series (control, +TEVp and +mavacamten) with variable total exposure ranging from 10 s to 40 s depending on the area available for scanning. The control condition corresponds to fiber bundles imaged in relaxing solution without any added compounds. The +TEVp condition corresponds to fiber bundles after incubation (100 units of acTEV protease per 600 μl relaxing solution) and imaged again in relaxing solution. The +mavacamten condition corresponds to fiber bundles after a second incubation (50 μmol l −1 mavacamten in relaxing solution) and imaged a third time in the incubating solution. All images acquired are available as a Dryad dataset ( https://doi.org/10.5061/dryad.ngf1vhj8c ). Animal procedures were performed using protocols approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at the University of Arizona (UA). All mice were bred and housed in a pathogen-free barrier animal facility at UA. Animal and tissue preparation protocols were identical to those of the Hessel et al. (2024a) study with the exception of switching to EDL muscles. Briefly, 10 adult mice (8F/2M, 2–6 months, strain C57BL/6NJ) were humanely euthanized by isoflurane inhalation followed by cervical dislocation; their EDL muscles were extracted, stored at −20°C in glycerol and shipped to LiX for X-ray data acquisition. Two to three fiber bundles per muscle were prepared at LiX via a thorough wash in relaxing solution with protease inhibitors and then tying with silk suture knots. Fiber bundles were then imaged at the beamline at the three conditions in series although some were truncated if equatorial quality indicated that fibers had degraded. The statistical analysis consisted of a mixed model ANOVA with main effect of condition (control/+TEVp/+mavacamten), and individual random effect to account for repeated measures across conditions. Response data were best Box–Cox transformed to meet normality assumptions. A significant main effect was followed up with Tukey’s honestly significant different (HSD) post hoc procedure for multiple comparisons with alpha set at 0.05. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION Raster area scanning We first examined the convergence of our data acquisition and processing scheme as the number of individual frames or, equivalently, the effective total exposure, was increased. The first step was to pre-filter out low-quality frames based on equatorial traces by fitting (1,0) and (1,1) peaks to each and rejecting non-physiological measurements of d 1,0 spacing and IRs. For mouse skeletal muscles in relaxing solution, preset bounds were [0, 1] for IR and [32 nm, 44 nm] for d 1,0 spacing, although users can freely change them as needed. This pre-filtering also rejected frames dominated by collagen as the fits for equatorial peaks typically fail in its presence. The remaining frames were then averaged together to generate the final merged diffraction pattern. Meridional and layer line features generally do not have sufficiently high signal-to-noise ratios in individual frames to consistently fit peaks and are only captured in the merged diffraction pattern. The merging process is thus based entirely on quality checks of equatorial traces. Fig. 3A shows the evolution of equatorial traces for three representative permeabilized mouse EDL fibers in relaxing solution as more frames are cumulatively averaged together in a randomized order. Fig. 3B shows scatter plots of d 1,0 spacing versus IR for both individual frames and the cumulatively averaged trajectories. Fig. 3C , D further plots the trajectories as the effective total exposure is increased and shows convergence of both d 1,0 and IR within 10 s, or within 100 frames randomly sampled throughout the muscle fiber. We note, based on the scatter plots, that individual frames map out a distribution of IR and d 1,0 spacings with broad peak-to-peak variation due to spatial inhomogeneties present in muscle fibers. This scatter emphasizes that we ultimately characterize only the average diffraction features within a muscle fiber. General quality of LiX datasets LiX has imaged several muscle types from animals ranging from zebrafish ( Mead et al., 2024 ) to humans ( Ochala et al., 2025 ). Diffraction quality strongly depends on muscle type and disease state. Fig. 4 shows a representative selection of permeabilized preparations from mouse and rat skeletal muscles and pig cardiac muscles under passive conditions. The selection was sourced from multiple datasets acquired at LiX (rat tibialis anterior, M.A.C.; mouse tibialis anterior, S.L.; and pig papillary, C.T.N.) and permeabilized in a nearly identical manner to the fMyBP-C study. Certain reflections are readily resolvable in most muscle types and disease states. These include the equatorial reflections (1,0) and (1,1), which measure lattice spacings and relative mass between thick and thin filaments, and meridional M3 and AL6, which measure the nth order reflections of the principal myosin and actin repeats. The diffraction quality of other reflections, such as myosin layer lines, low-order M clusters, M6, M11, AL5 and AL7, and higher-order equatorials, each of which map to different features of thick and thin filaments ( Koubassova et al., 2025 ; Ma and Irving, 2022 ; Brunello and Fusi, 2024 ; Reconditi, 2006 ), is case specific. At LiX, we have resolved these more difficult reflections in some muscle types by optimizing sample preparation, beam spot size and exposure settings, but success is not guaranteed for all experiments. Repeated study with the SnoopC2 mouse model With any new experimental setup, it is prudent to compare it against already existing setups to ensure confidence in its results. We thus ran a repeated study to compare experimental endpoints, stress test the new setup and demonstrated that LiX is equipped to help users run full muscle studies (see Materials and Methods ). The original study was previously conducted at the BioCAT beamline and investigated the downstream effects of fMyBP-C removal in the SnoopC2 mouse line, which is engineered to cleave fMyBP-C upon addition of TEVp ( Hessel et al., 2024a ). Fig. 5 summarizes the repeated study by visualizing shifts in muscle diffraction values. For all measures, we showed that the data collected at LiX recapitulate the directionality and conclusions of the original study. Namely, fMyBP-C cleavage under relaxed conditions expanded the actomyosin lattice (increased lattice spacing d 1,0 ), allowed more myosin heads to move to thin filaments (increased I 1,1 / I 1,0 ratio) and stretched the thick filaments (increased M3 spacing). Furthermore, a second treatment using the myosin deactivator mavacamten showed partial reversal of fMyBP-C cleavage effects where I 1,1 / I 1,0 returned to pre-cleavage values, but d 1,0 and M3 spacings only partially approached baseline measurements (values were not different from either control or +TEVp conditions based on ANOVA testing). Taken together, the data suggest that mavacamten shifts myosin heads back to the thick filaments but not necessarily in the same molecular configuration as in the presence of full-length fMyBP-C. Although the repeated and original datasets showed the same directionality between control and +TEVp states, differences in magnitude between the two datasets were also significant. We attribute this change to different muscle types available to us at the time, psoas in the original study and EDL in this repeated study. The physics of X-ray diffraction measurements are not specific to the originating beam line so we do not expect beam-specific settings such as photon flux, exposure times and beam spot size to be significant contributors to the observed magnitude difference given that the signal-to-noise ratio is adequate. Ongoing developments at LiX We continue to optimize the muscle diffraction capabilities at LiX on several fronts. The first is time-resolved experiments to study dynamic muscle phenomena such as tracking sarcomere structures during length changes. The biggest limiting factor here is the X-ray exposure time required for sufficient signal-to-noise ratio. Our fastest acquisition windows thus far consist of 33 ms frames in intact preparations of rat left ventricle cardiac slices (M.A.C. and B.P., unpublished data). Thus, with the current muscle diffraction capabilities at LiX, time-resolved experiments with the highest likelihood of success are those involving muscle samples known to diffract strongly (e.g. intact preparations) and where dynamics that can be captured with 33 ms or longer acquisition windows are of interest (e.g. residual force enhancement/depression, slow oscillatory rheology, work loop analysis at ~3 Hz or slower). The second development is the inclusion of wide angle scattering data analysis to extend the meridional axis beyond the small angle scattering data used here. Wide angle data permit investigation into higher-order myosin repeats (15th order and beyond) and the principal actin repeat at ~2.7 nm. Muscle diffraction studies have historically focused on low-order reflections, but recent work has renewed interest in higher-order reflections to measure filament strains with higher sensitivity than possible in the small angle q -range ( Ma et al., 2018 ; Squire and Knupp, 2021 ) or to reconstruct filament structures at finer scales ( Koubassova et al., 2025 ; Dutta et al., 2023 ). LiX is already configured for simultaneous small and wide angle operations using multiple synchronized detectors but the wide angle data analysis for muscle diffraction has not yet been developed and validated. Conclusions The methodological advances at LiX are geared towards lowering the barriers of entry to synchrotron-based muscle diffraction experiments and increasing the total supply of beamtime available for muscle biologists. The standardized hardware is designed to accommodate most forms of mechanics experiments (e.g. force–length, force–velocity, ramps, sinusoidals and step perturbations) during simultaneous X-ray exposure and now provides a ‘plug-and-play’ option for new users running foundational experiments under steady-state conditions and fixed temperature. The data acquisition and processing pipeline yields results in real-time for on-the-spot troubleshooting and generates publication-quality diffraction patterns for users to analyze using their tools of choice. The standardized hardware design and data tools are freely available for muscle biologists to use at LiX, and have been validated across several muscle types and stress tested at LiX in a full-sized repeated fMyBP-C study. Taken together, the methodological advances developed here progress the muscle X-ray diffraction pipeline into a more readily accessible technology for new users whose research would benefit from the structural data it provides. We conclude by noting that the limiting factors in generating high-quality muscle diffraction patterns remain the users’ ability to secure synchrotron access through the competitive, months-long proposal process and to prepare muscle fibers. Our efforts at LiX mitigate factors to relating hardware, data reduction and analysis challenges so the user can focus primarily on sample preparation. However, the beamline can ultimately process only as many samples as the users themselves can prepare. Efficient preparation of muscle samples for X-ray diffraction takes extensive practice and experience that users build with time over several beam runs. We thus encourage new users to run pilot studies prior to committing to a full study to (1) practice and iterate their fiber preparation protocol for X-ray diffraction using real-time tools at LiX and (2) determine the diffraction features that can be reliably measured at LiX and adjust their experimental design accordingly. A repeated muscle diffraction study performed at LiX and BioCAT on the downstream effects of fMyBP-C removal in mouse skeletal muscles. (A,B) Side-by-side comparison of representative image quality between the original study on psoas muscle at BioCAT and the repeated study on EDL muscle at LiX. Both images are control muscle samples at 2.7 μm sarcomere length (SL) and room temperature. (C–E) Measurements of key diffraction features for control, fMyBP-C removal via TEVp cleavage and partial reversal of fMyBP-C removal via the addition of the myosin deactivator mavacamten in the LiX-EDL study. Lattice spacing d 1,0 reflects the spacing between actomyosin filaments, intensity ratio I 1,1 / I 1,0 reflects the relative distribution of myosin heads between thick and thin filaments (where higher values imply more crossbridge attachment), and M3 spacing reflects the thick filament strains. Color bars indicate means±s.e.m. and asterisks indicate statistically significant differences between groups ( P &lt;0.05, mixed model ANOVA + Tukey HSD testing). Sample sizes for the repeated study were 29/24/20 fiber bundles for the control/+TEVp/+mavacamten conditions sourced from 10 adult mice with 2–3 bundles per mice.
