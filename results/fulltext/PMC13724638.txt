Introduction Uveal melanoma (UM) is the most common primary malignant intraocular tumor in adults 1 . While primary UM can often be effectively controlled, a significant proportion of cases (40% or more) eventually develop distant metastases, most commonly in the liver 2 . Once metastatic, UM remains a lethal disease with limited treatment options. Metastatic UM is generally unresponsive to chemotherapy or immune checkpoint inhibitors 3 , 4 . Although currently approved treatments such as tebentafusp (IMCgp100) and melphalan (Hepzato) have demonstrated survival benefits, there are still unmet needs due to the limitations of these drugs. Tebentafusp is only approved for patients who are HLA-A*02:01 positive, while Hepzato is limited to those with unresectable hepatic metastases involving less than 50% of the liver and no extrahepatic disease, or extrahepatic disease amenable to resection or radiation 5 – 7 . UM is molecularly distinct from cutaneous melanoma (CM) and exhibits a different pattern of driver mutations. Unlike CM, where BRAF or NRAS mutations are common, UM predominantly harbors mutually exclusive somatic mutations in the G protein-coupled receptor (GPCR) signal transducers GNAQ and GNA11 (encoding Gαq G proteins) in 92.5% of cases. Additional mutations occur in the GPCR CYSLTR2 (4%) and the downstream effector PLCβ4 (2.5%) 8 . CYSLTR2 is known to couple with Gαq to activate PLCβ4, suggesting that these genes operate within the same signaling pathway. These findings highlight the central role of constitutive activation of Gαq signaling in driving tumorigenesis in virtually all cases of UM 1 , 8 . Mutations in GNAQ / 11 impair GTPase activity, leading to constitutive activation of G proteins in their GTP-bound state. Because Gαq proteins play essential roles in numerous physiological processes, direct targeting of Gαq remains unfeasible. Compounds such as FR900359 and YM-254890, which inhibit Gαq directly, do not distinguish between wild-type and mutant Gαq, resulting in toxicity and rendering them unsuitable for clinical use 9 , 10 . Consequently, targeting the downstream signaling pathways of Gαq has emerged as a more viable therapeutic strategy. Oncogenic Gαq can engage multiple downstream signaling branches, including the canonical PLCβ-PKC-MAPK cascade and YAP signaling 11 . Although oncogenic Gαq activates multiple downstream pathways, available evidence points to YAP signaling as a particularly important effector in Gαq-driven UM 12 – 15 . In this context, MEK inhibition has shown limited clinical benefit in metastatic UM 12 , whereas YAP has been demonstrated to mediate key tumorigenic outputs of mutant Gαq/11 in experimental UM models 13 – 15 . These findings support a model in which, although oncogenic Gαq activates multiple downstream pathways, YAP functions as a major downstream transcriptional effector in UM. However, YAP lacks enzymatic activity and is generally considered difficult to target directly because it does not contain a suitable small-molecule-binding pocket. Currently, no specific YAP inhibitors are in clinical use 16 . Although YAP-targeting approaches such as antisense RNA are under clinical investigation ( NCT04659096 ), these strategies still face delivery challenges compared with conventional small-molecule therapeutics. Therefore, identifying druggable regulators of this critical downstream signaling axis may provide a more tractable therapeutic strategy for Gαq-mutant UM. Guided by this rationale, we sought to identify therapeutically actionable vulnerabilities in Gαq-mutant UM. Prior patient-level and experimental studies suggest that Gαq-driven UM is associated with transcriptional dysregulation and increased sensitivity to perturbation of transcription-associated regulators 14 , 17 . As key modulators of transcriptional output and chromatin state, epigenetic regulators were therefore prioritized for focused vulnerability screening. In this work, through parallel CRISPR/Cas9 screens in an isogenic cell pair differing only in GNAQ status, we identify p300 as a synthetic lethal vulnerability in GNAQ -mutant UM. We show that p300 supports YAP-dependent transcription downstream of oncogenic Gαq and that both genetic depletion and pharmacological inhibition of p300 suppress tumor growth in vitro and in vivo, as well as metastasis in vivo. Together, these findings establish p300 as a therapeutically actionable dependency in GNAQ -mutant UM and support p300 inhibition as a potential strategy for targeting oncogenic Gαq-YAP signaling. Results CRISPR/Cas9 knockout screens identify EP300 as a candidate synthetic lethal target in GNAQ -mutant UM Because oncogenic Gαq signaling is associated with distinct transcriptional features in UM 14 , we asked whether Gαq(Q209P) knock-in could establish a coordinated transcriptional and chromatin state linked to exploitable vulnerabilities. To address this question, we performed RNA-seq and ATAC-seq analyzes in an isogenic pair of wild-type and Gαq(Q209P) knock-in HEK293A cells established in our previous study 17 . Differential expression analysis identified 997 significantly upregulated genes and 1,038 downregulated genes in Gαq(Q209P) knock-in cells relative to wild-type cells, indicating extensive transcriptomic rewiring (Supplementary Fig. 1a ). Because transcriptional output is closely linked to chromatin accessibility, we next profiled genome-wide accessibility using ATAC-seq. Principal component analysis showed clear separation between Gαq(Q209P) knock-in and wild-type cells, indicating widespread alterations in chromatin accessibility associated with the knock-in mutation (Supplementary Fig. 1b ). Integration of the RNA-seq and ATAC-seq datasets further revealed that genes upregulated in Gαq(Q209P) knock-in cells displayed increased accessibility around their transcription start sites (TSSs), whereas downregulated genes showed reduced accessibility at the corresponding regions (Supplementary Fig. 1c ). Consistent with these changes, Gαq(Q209P) knock-in cells exhibited enhanced anchorage-independent growth, as evidenced by increased colony number and size in soft-agar assays (Supplementary Fig. 1d ). Moreover, inducible knockdown of Gαq selectively suppressed colony formation in Gαq(Q209P) knock-in cells but had minimal effect in wild-type cells, indicating that the knock-in mutation confers a selective dependency on oncogenic Gαq signaling for sustained growth (Supplementary Fig. 1e ). To further assess the disease relevance of this Gαq(Q209P)-associated transcriptional and chromatin state, we compared the gene expression changes induced by Gαq(Q209P) knock-in in HEK293A cells with the TCGA UM cohort using a framework adapted from a prior study of Gαq-driven UM, in which Gαq-negative skin cutaneous melanoma (SKCM) samples were used as the reference control because of the limited number of Gαq-negative UM cases 14 . Using this approach, we found a highly significant overlap between genes upregulated in Gαq(Q209P) knock-in HEK293A cells and genes overexpressed in Gαq-positive human UM, defined as tumors harboring mutation or amplification in GNAQ , GNA11 , or CYSLTR2 (hypergeometric test, p &lt; 3.598 × 10 −39 ; see Methods). These findings support the use of the isogenic HEK293A Gαq(Q209P) system as a tractable model for identifying vulnerabilities associated with oncogenic Gαq signaling. UM typically has a low mutation burden and remains relatively stable at the nucleotide level during metastasis 18 , suggesting that non-genetic regulatory mechanisms may contribute to disease progression. Patient-level transcriptomic analyses have also revealed distinct gene expression programs associated with Gαq-driven UM 14 . In addition, in our previous study, oncogenic Gαq mutations induced broad transcriptional changes and conferred enhanced sensitivity to transcription-associated inhibitors 17 . Together with the transcriptional and chromatin rewiring observed here in Gαq(Q209P) knock-in HEK293A cells, these findings suggested that epigenetic regulators, as key modulators of transcriptional output and chromatin state, might represent a tractable source of vulnerabilities in Gαq-driven UM. We therefore performed a CRISPR/Cas9 knockout screen using a previously characterized focused sgRNA library targeting 450 epigenetic regulators, with seven sgRNAs per gene 19 , together with 100 non-targeting control sgRNAs 20 . We performed the CRISPR/Cas9 library screen in duplicate under conditions favoring the transduction of individual sgRNAs into each cell. Following puromycin selection, cells were cultured for 14 days, after which sgRNA abundance was quantified by next-generation sequencing (Fig. 1a ). Genes that were selectively depleted in Gαq(Q209P)-mutant HEK293A cells relative to wild-type controls were identified using the MAGeCK algorithm, with lower robust rank aggregation (RRA) scores indicating stronger negative selection 21 . As expected, the non-targeting control sgRNAs were not differentially represented between Gαq(Q209P)-mutant and wild-type cells (Supplementary Fig. 2a ). Among the negatively selected genes, EP300 emerged as the top-ranked candidate, with sgRNAs targeting EP300 showing marked depletion. Notably, CREBBP , which encodes the closely related paralogue CBP, was also identified as a negatively selected hit and ranked eighth in the screen (Fig. 1 b, c ). Because EP300 showed the strongest negative selection, we selected p300 for further investigation. The EP300 gene encodes the transcriptional coactivator p300, a close paralogue of CBP encoded by CREBBP ; both proteins possess intrinsic histone acetyltransferase activity 22 . To validate the requirement of p300 in Gαq-mutant UM cells, we generated stable 92.1 and OMM2.3 cell lines expressing doxycycline-inducible non-targeting shRNA or two independent p300-targeting shRNAs. Doxycycline treatment efficiently reduced p300 expression without affecting CBP levels (Fig. 1d ), and p300 knockdown markedly suppressed colony formation in both cell lines (Fig. 1e ). Because CREBBP also scored among the top negatively selected genes in the screen, we next assessed whether CBP similarly contributes to the growth of Gαq-mutant UM cells. In parallel inducible knockdown experiments, depletion of either p300 or CBP suppressed colony formation in both OMM2.3 and 92.1 cells (Supplementary Fig. 3a , b ). We next examined the sensitivity of Gαq-mutant UM cell lines (92.1, Mel202, Mel270, and OMM2.3) to pharmacological inhibition of p300/CBP, in comparison with Gαq wild-type UM cell lines (Mel285 and Mel290) and BRAF-mutant CM cell lines (A375, M14, and SK-MEL-28). CCS1477 and CPI-637 target the bromodomain, while A485 targets the HAT domain. Because of the high sequence similarity between p300 and CBP, specific small-molecule inhibitors for either protein alone are not currently available. Gαq-mutant UM cell lines displayed marked dose-dependent sensitivity to all three inhibitors, with half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) values approximately at or below 1 μM. In contrast, Gαq-wild-type UM cell lines and BRAF-mutant CM cell lines showed minimal sensitivity, with IC50 values around or above 10 μM (Fig. 1f ). Similarly, Gαq(Q209P)-mutant HEK293A cells were more sensitive to these inhibitors than their wild-type counterparts (Supplementary Fig. 2b ). To further assess the functional relationship between p300 and CBP in this setting, we compared the effects of individual and combined depletion of the two proteins. In both OMM2.3 and 92.1 cells, co-depletion of p300 and CBP did not further reduce colony formation beyond that observed with depletion of either protein alone (Supplementary Fig. 3c , d ). Collectively, these data identify EP300 as the top synthetic lethal candidate from the screen and support a functional requirement for p300/CBP in the growth of Gαq-driven UM cells. p300 supports YAP-dependent transcription downstream of oncogenic Gαq in UM To gain mechanistic insight into the synthetic lethality between EP300 and oncogenic GNAQ , we first examined the transcriptional changes induced by p300 depletion in 92.1 cells, a GNAQ -mutant UM cell line, by RNA sequencing (RNA-seq). KEGG enrichment analysis of the downregulated genes identified the Hippo pathway as the most significantly enriched pathway (Supplementary Fig. 4a , b ). YAP is a major transcriptional coactivator downstream of Hippo signaling, and previous studies in UM have established YAP as a key transcriptional effector downstream of oncogenic Gαq 13 – 15 . We therefore investigated whether p300 contributes to YAP-dependent transcription in GNAQ -mutant UM cells. To establish a framework for assessing this possibility, we first defined the YAP-activated transcriptional program in 92.1 cells by RNA-seq following YAP knockdown. Efficient YAP depletion by two independent shRNAs was confirmed by immunoblotting (Supplementary Fig. 4c ). Genes significantly downregulated by both YAP shRNAs were defined as YAP-activated genes ( n = 1186) (Fig. 2a ). This gene set was then used to assess the effect of p300 depletion on YAP-dependent transcriptional output. We compared the transcriptomes of control and p300-depleted 92.1 cells. A substantial proportion of YAP-activated genes was downregulated upon p300 knockdown, with 70% (833 out of 1186) and 66% (787 out of 1186) reduced in the p300 shRNA#1 and p300 shRNA#2 groups, respectively. By contrast, the corresponding fractions among all expressed genes were much smaller, at 12% (1294 out of 10,525) and 13% (1399 out of 10,525) (Fig. 2b ). Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) further confirmed that most YAP-activated genes were suppressed upon p300 knockdown (Fig. 2c ). Consistently, p300 depletion reduced the transcript abundance of YAP-activated genes more strongly than that of the remaining expressed genes (Fig. 2d ). Together, these analyses indicate that p300 preferentially supports the YAP-activated transcriptional program. To validate the RNA-seq findings at the level of individual genes, we examined the expression of representative YAP target genes in OMM2.3 cells. siRNA-mediated p300 knockdown reduced the mRNA levels of representative YAP target genes, including CTGF , CYR61 , FGF-1 and CDC6 (Fig. 2e ). Similar results were observed following treatment with CCS1477 (Fig. 2f ). We next focused on CTGF , a representative YAP target gene, to further examine the effect of p300 depletion on YAP target gene regulation. In OMM2.3 cells, p300 knockdown significantly reduced CTGF promoter-luciferase reporter activity (Fig. 2g ). Because a transiently transfected promoter-luciferase reporter does not fully recapitulate the chromatin organization of the endogenous CTGF locus, we further examined H3K27ac enrichment at the endogenous CTGF promoter in the same cellular context. H3K27ac ChIP–qPCR at the endogenous CTGF promoter region spanning the TEAD-binding site showed that p300 depletion reduced local H3K27ac enrichment (Supplementary Fig. 4d ). Together, these results indicate that p300 supports CTGF promoter-driven reporter activity and is also required for H3K27ac enrichment at the endogenous CTGF promoter in OMM2.3 cells. Given the close paralogy between p300 and CBP, we also examined whether CBP depletion affects the same YAP-associated transcriptional program. In 92.1 cells, GSEA showed that most YAP-activated genes were suppressed upon CBP knockdown (Supplementary Fig. 5a ). Consistently, YAP-activated genes were more strongly reduced than the remaining expressed genes upon CBP knockdown (Supplementary Fig. 5b ). These data indicate that CBP depletion largely phenocopies p300 depletion with respect to suppression of YAP-activated genes. Together, these results indicate that p300 is required to sustain YAP-dependent transcriptional output in GNAQ -mutant UM cells. p300 interacts with YAP and TAZ Various nuclear partners of YAP, including transcription factors and epigenetic regulators, have been shown to modulate YAP transcriptional activity 16 , 23 . Although an association between YAP and p300 has been reported 24 , whether this interaction is direct and how it contributes to YAP-dependent transcription remain incompletely defined. To characterize the interaction between p300 and YAP, we first performed reciprocal co-immunoprecipitation (co-IP) assays in HEK293T cells. HA-tagged p300 co-immunoprecipitated with Flag-tagged YAP, confirming the association between ectopically expressed p300 and YAP (Fig. 3a ). In addition, proximity ligation assay (PLA) demonstrated an endogenous interaction between p300 and YAP in the nucleus of OMM2.3 cells (Fig. 3b ). Together, these results establish that p300 interacts with YAP under both overexpression and endogenous conditions. To define the YAP region responsible for p300 binding, we generated a series of YAP deletion mutants (Fig. 3c ). Progressive carboxy-terminal truncation analyses identified amino acids 153-272 of YAP as the minimal region required for interaction with p300 (Fig. 3d ). This region contains the WW1 and WW2 domains. Deletion of either WW1 or WW2 alone had a modest effect on p300 binding, whereas simultaneous deletion of both WW domains markedly impaired the interaction (Fig. 3e ), indicating that the WW domains cooperatively mediate the association between YAP and p300. We next sought to define the YAP-binding region within p300. A series of p300 truncation mutants was generated (Fig. 3f ) and co-expressed with YAP in HEK293T cells. Co-IP analyses showed that the N-terminal region of p300 (1-680 aa) interacted with YAP (Fig. 3g ). Further truncation mapping localized the minimal YAP-binding region to amino acids 567-680, a segment encompassing the KIX domain (Fig. 3h ). Consistent with this result, deletion of the KIX domain abolished the interaction between p300 and YAP (Fig. 3i ), indicating that the KIX domain is required for the association with YAP. Given the high sequence similarity between the KIX domains of p300 and CBP, we next asked whether CBP also interacts with YAP through its KIX domain. Co-immunoprecipitation assays showed that CBP interacted with YAP, whereas deletion of the CBP KIX domain abolished this interaction (Supplementary Fig. 6a–c ). These results indicate that YAP binding through the KIX domain is not unique to p300. Given that TEAD family proteins have been implicated in enhancer-associated transcriptional regulation involving p300 25 , we next asked whether the YAP-p300 association depends on TEAD proteins. Simultaneous knockdown of TEAD1/2/3/4 did not impair the interaction between YAP and p300 in co-IP assays (Supplementary Fig. 7a ), arguing against a TEAD-mediated bridging mechanism. To determine whether YAP and p300 bind directly, we purified recombinant p300 (567-680 aa) and GST-YAP (155-290 aa) proteins and examined their interaction using surface plasmon resonance (SPR). p300 bound GST-YAP in a concentration-dependent manner, whereas binding to GST alone was not detected (Supplementary Fig. 7b , c ). Together, these results demonstrate that YAP directly binds to p300 in a TEAD-independent manner, and that this interaction is mediated by the WW domains of YAP and the KIX domain of p300. We next asked whether disruption of the p300-YAP interaction affects YAP-dependent transcription. Full-length p300 or a KIX-deleted mutant of p300 (p300-ΔKIX) was co-expressed with YAP in HEK293T cells, and transcriptional activity was assessed using the 8×GTIIC-Luc reporter, which contains multimerized TEAD-binding motifs and is widely used to monitor YAP/TAZ-dependent transcription 26 . Full-length p300 significantly enhanced YAP-dependent reporter activity, whereas p300-ΔKIX failed to do so (Fig. 3j ). These results indicate that the KIX domain is required for p300-mediated enhancement of YAP transcriptional activity. To further define the p300 domains required for YAP-dependent gene expression, we generated p300 mutants lacking the KIX domain, bromodomain (BRD), or HAT domain and co-expressed them with YAP in HEK293T cells (Supplementary Fig. 8a , b ). RT-qPCR analysis showed that deletion of any of these domains markedly impaired the ability of p300 to induce the expression of the YAP target genes CTGF and CYR61 (Supplementary Fig. 8c ). These results indicate that the KIX-mediated p300-YAP interaction, together with the functional contributions of the BRD and HAT domains, contributes to p300-dependent regulation of YAP target gene expression. Because YAP and TAZ are closely related transcriptional coactivators that share TEAD-dependent transcriptional outputs, we then examined whether a similar mechanism also applies to TAZ. Co-IP assays showed that TAZ also interacted with p300, whereas deletion of the TAZ WW domain or the p300 KIX domain abolished this interaction (Supplementary Fig. 9a–e ). Functionally, p300 knockdown markedly suppressed TAZ-dependent reporter activity, and wild-type p300 enhanced TAZ-dependent reporter activity, whereas p300-ΔKIX failed to do so (Supplementary Fig. 9f–h ). These results indicate that p300 regulates both YAP and TAZ through a domain-dependent interaction mechanism. To gain structural insight into the p300-YAP interaction, we used AlphaFold3 to predict the structures of p300 (567-680 aa) and YAP (171-266 aa), followed by protein-protein docking analysis using HDOCK to generate a structural model of the complex (Supplementary Fig. 10a ). Structural analysis of the modeled complex suggested that the interaction may be stabilized by multiple hydrogen bonds, one salt bridge, and hydrophobic contacts (Supplementary Fig. 10b ). These modeling results are consistent with the biochemical evidence supporting direct binding between p300 and YAP. In summary, our data demonstrate that p300 directly binds YAP in a TEAD-independent manner through the KIX domain of p300 and the WW domains of YAP, and that this interaction contributes to p300-mediated enhancement of YAP-dependent transcription. A similar domain-dependent mechanism also applies to TAZ. p300 regulates YAP-dependent transcription independently of YAP acetylation Previous studies have shown that YAP can be acetylated by p300 under certain conditions 27 . However, whether p300 regulates YAP-dependent transcription through direct acetylation of YAP remains unclear. Consistent with previous reports, ectopic expression of p300 increased YAP acetylation in HEK293T cells (Fig. 4a ), whereas stable knockdown of p300 using two independent shRNAs reduced endogenous YAP acetylation (Fig. 4b ). To test whether YAP acetylation is required for YAP-dependent transcription, we generated an acetylation-deficient YAP mutant, YAP (13KR), in which all 13 lysine residues were substituted with arginine (Fig. 4c ). As expected, co-expression of p300 markedly increased acetylation of wild-type YAP but not YAP (13KR) (Fig. 4d ), confirming that the 13KR mutant abolishes p300-mediated acetylation of YAP. We next examined whether loss of YAP acetylation alters p300-enhanced YAP-dependent transcriptional output. RNA-seq analysis in HEK293T cells co-expressing p300 with either wild-type YAP or YAP (13KR) showed that the two groups remained highly similar within the predefined YAP-activated gene set ( n = 1186), as illustrated by sample-to-sample distance analysis (Fig. 4e ). Consistently, co-expression of p300 with either wild-type YAP or YAP (13KR) increased expression of the same YAP-activated gene set to a comparable extent (Fig. 4f ). In agreement with these transcriptome-level analyses, ectopic expression of either wild-type YAP or YAP (13KR) similarly increased mRNA levels of the canonical YAP target genes CTGF and CYR61 , and co-expression of p300 further enhanced their expression to a comparable extent (Fig. 4g ). Together, these results indicate that loss of YAP acetylation does not substantially impair p300-enhanced YAP-dependent transcriptional output. We then asked whether pharmacological inhibition of p300 suppresses YAP-dependent transcription by blocking YAP acetylation. The p300 HAT domain inhibitor A485 abolished YAP acetylation, whereas the p300 bromodomain inhibitor CCS1477 had no detectable effect on YAP acetylation (Fig. 4h ). Nevertheless, both inhibitors reduced global H3K27ac levels (Fig. 4h ) and similarly suppressed expression of YAP target genes (Fig. 4i ). In addition, treatment with either inhibitor did not alter the intracellular localization of YAP in OMM2.3 cells (Supplementary Fig. 11 ). These results indicate that the transcriptional effects of p300 inhibition are better associated with reduced chromatin acetylation than with loss of YAP acetylation. Together, these results indicate that p300 regulates YAP-dependent transcription primarily through modulation of chromatin acetylation rather than through direct acetylation of YAP. Genome-wide association between YAP and p300 Given the role of p300 in epigenetic regulation 22 , we investigated whether p300 influences YAP-dependent transcription through its association with YAP on chromatin. ChIP-seq analysis of endogenous YAP and p300 in 92.1 cells identified 8996 YAP-binding sites and 6890 p300-binding sites, with p300 associated with 35% of YAP-binding sites (3134/8996; Fig. 5a ). The summits of p300 peaks coincided with those of YAP peaks when centered on YAP peaks (Fig. 5b ). Moreover, p300 signal intensity was positively correlated with YAP signal intensity at the associated binding sites (Fig. 5c ). Together, these analyses indicate a genome-wide association between YAP and p300 on chromatin. Discussion UM is a cancer characterized by stereotyped oncogenic events, notably activating Gαq mutations. This offers a promising opportunity to identify synthetic lethal targets that could benefit the majority of UM patients. Given that results from non-isogenic cell lines may be confounded by heterogeneous genetic backgrounds, we employed an isogenic CRISPR screening strategy. This approach led to the identification of p300 as a vulnerability specific to Gαq-mutant cells. Importantly, inhibition of p300, using both genetic and pharmacological methods, demonstrated potent antitumor effects in in vivo models representing clinically relevant stages of UM harboring Gαq mutations. Mechanistically, p300 sustains YAP-dependent transcription downstream of oncogenic Gαq by maintaining H3K27 acetylation and the occupancy of CDK9 and RNA polymerase II at YAP target-gene promoters, providing a mechanistic basis for the selective dependence of GNAQ -mutant UM on p300 (Fig. 8 ). Our study highlights the potential of targeting p300 in Gαq-mutant UM, particularly because direct targeting of mutant Gαq itself or its primary downstream effector YAP remains challenging. (2026) https://BioRender.com/30acp6h . p300 and CBP are closely related paralogous transcriptional coactivators that regulate gene expression and chromatin state, and both have been implicated in sustaining malignant transcriptional programs 29 , 30 . Prior studies, largely based on co-immunoprecipitation, have suggested that YAP forms complexes with p300/CBP in other biological contexts 24 , 31 , 32 . However, how p300/CBP coordinates the transcriptional activity of constitutively activated YAP downstream of oncogenic Gαq in UM remains unclear. Our data show that p300 and YAP interact in the nucleus and further demonstrate direct binding between the KIX domain of p300 and the WW1/2 domains of YAP. A similar WW-KIX-dependent interaction was also observed for TAZ, suggesting that both YAP and TAZ may be regulated by p300/CBP through a shared domain-dependent interaction. Previous studies have shown that p300/CBP can promote transcriptional activity by acetylating transcription factors, such as MYB, which increases DNA binding and transactivation 33 , 34 . However, our data suggest that, although p300 promotes the acetylation of YAP, this modification does not affect YAP’s transcriptional activity. Specifically, acetylation-deficient YAP and wild-type YAP exhibited similar transcriptional activity, indicating that YAP acetylation is dispensable for YAP-dependent transcription in this setting. Moreover, HAT and bromodomain inhibitors of p300/CBP suppressed the transcriptional activity of YAP despite their differing effects on YAP acetylation. Remarkably, our data suggest that the interaction between p300 and YAP at chromatin plays an important role in YAP’s transcriptional activity. Specifically, the YAP-p300 interaction enhances H3K27 acetylation at the promoters and putative enhancers of YAP target genes, thereby facilitating recruitment of transcriptional machinery. Consistent with this, CBP depletion produced effects similar to those of p300 depletion, reducing global H3K27ac, decreasing H3K27ac at YAP target gene promoters, and suppressing YAP-associated transcriptional output. These findings support the idea that both paralogues contribute to the YAP-dependent transcriptional program in Gαq-mutant UM. The potential selectivity of targeting p300/CBP is unlikely to arise from direct discrimination between mutant and wild-type Gαq. Rather, it may reflect a context-dependent transcriptional dependency created by oncogenic Gαq signaling. In our study, this interpretation is supported by the selective depletion of EP300 and CREBBP in the isogenic Gαq(Q209P) screen, the preferential sensitivity of Gαq-mutant UM cells to p300/CBP inhibition compared with Gαq-wild-type or BRAF -mutant melanoma cells, and the antitumor activity of p300 inhibition in vivo at tolerated doses. Conceptually, this is consistent with previous studies showing that BET inhibition can preferentially suppress YAP/TAZ-dependent transcriptional addiction through BRD4 23 . Although p300/CBP have been proposed as general regulators of RNA polymerase II-dependent transcription, genome-wide studies have instead shown that p300/CBP inhibition does not globally or indiscriminately suppress cellular transcription but rather more selectively impedes transcription of distinct oncogenic networks across different cancer types 29 . In line with these findings, we demonstrated that p300/CBP inhibition selectively disrupts the oncogenic YAP transcriptional network, which may contribute to the marked sensitivity of Gαq-mutant UM to p300/CBP inhibition. This hypersensitivity likely arises because Gαq-mutant UM is highly dependent on YAP for survival and growth 13 , 14 , 35 . In addition, this sensitivity may also reflect activation of p300/CBP HAT activity by YAP-associated complexes assembled on chromatin. It has been reported that not all p300/CBP recruitment events result in chromatin acetylation and gene expression, indicating that HAT activity at certain sites may be restrained 36 , 37 . Indeed, p300/CBP exists in two states: basal and active. In the basal state, the auto-inhibitory loop (AIL) occupies the substrate-binding groove and interacts with the RING domain, thereby blocking the active site of the enzyme. In the active state, the AIL is autoacetylated and disengages from the active site, allowing the RING domain to shift position and the enzyme to become active 22 . Interestingly, transcription factor dimerization has been shown to activate p300 HAT 38 . Specifically, when transcription factors dimerize, they adopt a DNA-binding-competent conformation, enabling them to engage two p300 molecules in the nucleus. This interaction increases the likelihood of AIL disengagement from its inhibitory position in cis and its capture in trans by a second p300 molecule 38 . Notably, YAP can form stable homodimers, which exhibit enhanced oncogenic behavior compared to their monomeric forms 39 . In light of these studies, together with our data showing that both CBP and p300 are required for YAP-associated transcriptional output in UM cells, we propose that YAP dimerization may promote the formation of YAP-associated transcriptional complexes that engage both p300 and CBP and facilitate HAT activation within these complexes to support YAP target gene expression in Gαq-mutant UM. The marked reduction of global H3K27ac after depletion of either paralogue, together with reduced H3K27ac at YAP target promoters, raises the possibility that YAP-associated p300/CBP complexes regulate H3K27ac as part of a dynamic acetylation-deacetylation process rather than through a simple static deposition mechanism. In this context, endogenous HDAC activity may also influence the turnover and homeostasis of H3K27ac at YAP target loci, adding another layer of complexity to YAP-dependent transcriptional regulation in Gαq-mutant UM. The precise composition, organization and functional regulation of these YAP-associated p300/CBP complexes, however, remain to be determined. This mechanism may help sustain and reinforce YAP transcriptional activity in Gαq-mutant UM, and the functional interdependency between YAP and p300/CBP may help explain the marked sensitivity of Gαq-mutant UM to p300/CBP inhibition. Dependency on p300 and CBP appears to be context-dependent across cancers. In some tumor types, p300 dependency arises in the setting of CBP deficiency, consistent with a paralog addiction model 40 , 41 . In other contexts, however, tumor cells may remain dependent on both p300 and CBP 42 . Our findings in Gαq-mutant UM instead support the latter possibility. In UM cells, depletion of either p300 or CBP impaired clonogenic growth, and co-depletion was not more inhibitory than depletion of either paralogue alone. In vivo, our study focused on p300, and p300 depletion was sufficient to induce robust anti-growth and anti-metastatic effects. Analysis of the UM TCGA cohort further showed that CREBBP expression was not lower than EP300 , and CREBBP mutations were not observed (Supplementary Fig. 17 ), arguing against a CBP-deficient background in this disease. Consistent with functional involvement of both paralogues, EP300 and CREBBP mRNA levels positively correlated with the YAP target genes CYR61 and CTGF in the UM TCGA cohort (Supplementary Fig. 18 ). Together, these observations suggest that dependency on p300 or CBP in Gαq-mutant UM is unlikely to be explained by loss of the other paralogue. Evidence from knockout studies in the mouse has shown that CBP and p300 are critical for the development and function of normal cells and tissues 43 , 44 . Therefore, inhibition of either p300 or CBP alone may be sufficient to exert anti-tumor effects and could still offer an expanded therapeutic index in Gαq-mutant UM. Because EP300 emerged as the top-ranked hit in our screen and p300 was the paralogue validated most extensively in vivo in this study, p300-directed strategies provide a practical example for therapeutic development. While current HAT and BRD small molecule inhibitors cannot discriminate between CBP and p300, recently developed small-molecule and peptide proteolysis-targeting chimera (PROTAC) drugs demonstrate potent and selective p300 degradation 45 , 46 . This highlights the potential of p300-targeting PROTACs as a promising therapeutic approach. In addition, we propose that new therapeutics may be designed around the YAP-p300 interaction, particularly by targeting the p300 KIX domain. Interestingly, there has been growing interest in investigating KIX domain inhibitors, and several small molecule KIX inhibitors have been reported 47 – 49 . We demonstrate that p300 inhibition suppresses tumor growth and metastasis of Gαq-mutant UM cells in xenograft mouse models and blunts the dissemination of UM cells in orthotopically xenografted zebrafish embryos. Future studies could expand on these findings by utilizing more sophisticated models of UM, such as patient-derived xenografts or organoids, to further validate the sensitivity of Gαq-mutant UM cells to p300 inhibition. We also note that the HEK293A Gαq(Q209P) knock-in system used for the discovery screen is not a lineage-faithful model of UM. Because suitable lineage-faithful isogenic models are currently not available for pooled genetic screening, we used this system as a tractable discovery platform; future studies using uveal melanocyte-derived isogenic Gαq-wild-type and Gαq-mutant models may provide a more physiologically relevant framework. This study also underscores the need for additional immunocompetent in vivo models to assess the anticancer efficacy of p300 inhibition in Gαq-mutant UM, as p300/CBP have been shown to regulate anti-tumor immune responses 50 , 51 . Furthermore, our findings support a functional interdependency between YAP and p300, while also supporting a role for CBP in YAP-associated transcriptional output, suggesting that YAP-dependent transcription in this context is supported by both p300 and CBP. In addition to oncogenic Gαq mutations, various mechanisms, including genetic alterations in Hippo pathway components, epigenetic dysregulation, cross-talk with other oncogenic signaling pathways, and tumor microenvironmental cues, may also contribute to YAP activation 16 , 52 . Therefore, future studies are warranted to explore the therapeutic potential of p300 inhibition in cancers in which YAP is aberrantly activated and functionally important. Protein-protein docking and interaction analysis Protein-protein docking and interaction analyses were performed as previously described 53 . Briefly, the structures of p300 (567-680 aa) and YAP (171-266 aa) were independently predicted using AlphaFold3. The predicted structures were subsequently subjected to protein-protein docking analysis using the HDOCK server to predict and rank the most probable binding mode. The resulting complex model was then analyzed using the PLIP (Protein-Ligand Interaction Profiler) platform to identify non-covalent interactions, including hydrophobic interactions, hydrogen bonds, and salt bridges. Additional interaction details were visualized and further explored using PyMOL. These analyses generated the predicted p300-YAP interaction interface and the corresponding residue-level intermolecular interactions presented in Supplementary Fig. 10 . Protein purification and binding affinity measurement Recombinant GST-YAP (155-290 aa) was expressed in Escherichia coli BL21(DE3) cells, induced with 0.2 mM IPTG at 16 °C overnight, and purified using glutathione affinity chromatography. Similar procedures have been described previously 54 . Recombinant p300 (567-680 aa)-His protein was expressed in Expi293F cells and purified at 4 °C using Ni-NTA agarose affinity chromatography. Briefly, clarified culture supernatant was loaded onto a Ni-NTA column at a flow rate of 0.5 ml/min, and the flow-through was reloaded once to improve binding efficiency. The column was washed with equilibration buffer, followed by impurity-washing buffer, and the bound protein was eluted with buffer containing 250 mM imidazole. Eluted fractions were dialyzed against PBS at 4 °C to remove imidazole. Protein concentration was determined by BCA assay, and purified protein was aliquoted and stored at − 80 °C until use. The binding affinity between GST-YAP (155-290 aa) and p300 (567-680 aa) was determined by surface plasmon resonance (SPR) on a Biacore S100 system essentially as described previously 54 . GST-YAP (155-290 aa) or GST was immobilized on a CM5 sensor chip, and serial dilutions of purified p300 (567-680 aa) protein were injected in running buffer. Sensorgrams were analyzed using Biacore S100 Evaluation Software to calculate the equilibrium dissociation constant (K D ). RNA sequencing and analysis Total RNA was extracted from cells using the RNAsimple Total RNA Kit (FOREGENE, RE-03113). Three biological replicates were prepared for each condition, and RNA samples were sent to Genewiz for sequencing. Raw reads were adapter-trimmed using Trim Galore and aligned to the human reference genome (GRCh38) using HISAT2. Gene-level read counts were generated with FeatureCounts, and FPKM values were calculated for expression visualization. Genes were considered expressed if FPKM was &gt; 1 in at least two samples. Differential expression analysis was performed using edgeR. Upregulated differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were defined as those with an adjusted p -value &lt; 0.05 and fold change &gt; 1.33, whereas downregulated DEGs were defined as those with an adjusted p- value &lt; 0.05 and fold change &lt; 0.75. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was performed to evaluate YAP-activated gene signatures. Sample-to-sample distance analysis for YAP-activated gene sets was performed in DESeq2 using normalized count data. To examine whether the transcriptional program induced by Gαq knock-in in HEK293A cells captures transcriptional features observed in human tumors driven by oncogenic Gαq signaling, we performed a TCGA-based comparative analysis adapted from a previously reported UM-centered framework, with UM as the tumor cohort and skin cutaneous melanoma (SKCM) as the reference cohort 14 . Gene expression profiles and copy number alteration data of TCGA-UM and TCGA-SKCM cases were retrieved from cBioPortal on March 18, 2026. The analysis included 80 UM samples and 448 SKCM samples. cBioPortal was used because it harmonizes genomic annotations across TCGA contributing centers and thereby reduces potential center-dependent inconsistencies in mutation calls. Tumors were classified as Gαq-positive (Gαq + ) if at least one Gαq-pathway gene ( GNAQ , GNA11 , or CYSLTR2 ) harbored either a mutation or copy-number gain. In this study, copy-number gain was defined as a GISTIC score ≥ 1. Tumors lacking mutation or amplification in all three genes were classified as Gαq-negative (Gαq − ). We then applied a two-step expression filter to identify UM-associated genes linked to oncogenic Gαq signaling. First, gene expression in Gαq + UM tumors ( n = 77; excluding 3 Gαq − UM cases) was compared with that in Gαq − SKCM tumors ( n = 287) using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, and genes with p &lt; 0.05 were retained, yielding 5746 candidates. Second, to minimize lineage-related differences unrelated to Gαq status, we excluded genes that were also differentially expressed between UM and the full SKCM cohort regardless of Gαq classification; specifically, only genes that were not significantly different in this comparison (Wilcoxon rank-sum p &gt; 0.05) were retained. This filtering step resulted in 687 genes out of 19,433 tested genes. To determine whether the UM-derived Gαq-associated gene set significantly overlapped with the transcriptional program induced by Gαq(Q209P) in HEK293A cells, we compared these 687 genes with the 997 genes upregulated in HEK293A Gαq(Q209P) knock-in cells relative to parental HEK293A cells. The significance of overlap was assessed using a hypergeometric test with the 19,433 tested genes as the background set. ATAC sequencing and analysis HEK293A cells and HEK293A Gαq (Q209P) knock-in cells were prepared in three biological replicates each. After cell lysis, transposition was performed using Tn5 transposase (Illumina, #FC-121-1030) at 37 °C for 30 min. Transposed DNA was purified using the MinElute PCR Purification Kit (Qiagen, #28004). Libraries were pre-amplified for five cycles using custom Nextera PCR primers and NEBNext Q5 Hot Start HiFi PCR Master Mix (NEB, #M0543S), and the additional number of amplification cycles was determined by qPCR using an aliquot of the pre-amplified product. Final PCR products were purified using Agencourt AMPure XP beads (Beckman Colter, #A63881) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq X Plus platform (PE150; Novogene Biotech, China). Raw reads were adapter-trimmed using Trim Galore and aligned to the hg38 genome using Bowtie2. Aligned reads were filtered for low-quality reads and duplicates using SAMtools. Peaks shared across biological replicates were identified using BEDtools intersect. Heatmaps and average profile plots were generated using deepTools, and principal component analysis (PCA) was performed using the R package ggplot2. Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP), ChIP-seq and ChIP-qPCR For ChIP-seq, stable 92.1 cells expressing doxycycline-inducible non-targeting or p300 shRNAs were treated with doxycycline (200 ng/ml) for 96 h before the assay. Animals Nude mice were purchased from Guangdong Medical Laboratory Animal Center (Guangzhou, China), and NCG mice were obtained from GemPharmatech (Jiangsu, China). The mice were housed in the specific pathogen-free (SPF) animal facility at Southern Medical University. Prior to the initiation of the experiments, a one-week acclimation period was allowed for the mice to adjust to the laboratory environment. All mice were housed under controlled conditions with a 12 h light/dark cycle, a temperature range of 22–24 °C, and 50–60% humidity. Throughout the study, they had continuous free access to food and water. Only male mice were used in this study to minimize variability associated with sex and to maintain consistency across experiments. Accordingly, sex was not included as an experimental variable in the study design or analysis. The maximal tumor volume permitted by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee was 2000 mm 3 , and this limit was not exceeded in any of the animal experiments. Wild-type AB zebrafish were purchased from China Zebrafish Resource Center (Wuhan, China). Zebrafish embryos were maintained at 28 °C under standard laboratory conditions. Tumor xenograft experiments To evaluate the effect of p300 knockdown on tumor growth, stable 92.1 cells expressing doxycycline-inducible p300 shRNA#1 (5 × 10 6 cells), stable M14 cells expressing doxycycline-inducible p300 shRNA#1 (3 × 10 6 cells), and stable 92.1 cells expressing doxycycline-inducible non-targeting shRNA (5 × 10 6 cells) were subcutaneously injected into the left back flanks of 6-week-old male nude mice. Once tumors became palpable, mice were randomly assigned to either the vehicle control group or the doxycycline treatment group. Mice in the vehicle control group received drinking water supplemented with 5% sucrose, whereas mice in the treatment group received 0.5 mg/ml doxycycline in drinking water supplemented with 5% sucrose. The doxycycline-containing water was changed every 2 days. Tumor height and width were measured using a caliper, and tumor volume was calculated as width 2 × height × 0.5. Mice were euthanized after 24 days of doxycycline treatment. Tumor xenografts were excised, recorded and fixed for further analysis. To assess the effect of p300 knockdown on tumor cell dissemination, stable 92.1 cells expressing doxycycline-inducible p300 shRNA#1 were fluorescently labeled with CM-DiI (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #C7000) and injected into the posterior eye region of zebrafish embryos (48 h post-fertilization). Misinjected embryos were removed, and the remaining embryos were treated with or without 10 µg/ml doxycycline and maintained at 34 °C. At 4 days post-injection (dpi), tumor cell dissemination was assessed using an Olympus MVX10 Zoom Fluorescence Macro System Microscope. Tumor cells migrating from the injection site (eye) to distal regions (head, trunk, and tail) were manually counted and defined as disseminated cells. Liver metastasis mouse model Six-week-old male NCG mice were injected intrasplenically with stable MM28-Luc (1 × 10 6 ) or 92.1-Luc (1 × 10 6 ) cells expressing doxycycline-inducible p300 shRNA#1 or OMM2.3-Luc (1 × 10 6 ) cells. Tumor implantation was monitored weekly via bioluminescence imaging using the In Vivo Imaging System (IVIS) (PerkinElmer). Before imaging, mice were intraperitoneally injected with 150 mg/kg D-luciferin potassium salt (PerkinElmer, #122799) dissolved in sterile PBS (Gibco, #10010023) 15 min prior to imaging. On day 4 post-surgery, mice were randomized into treatment groups. For the MM28-Luc and 92.1-Luc models, mice were given 0.5 mg/ml doxycycline in drinking water supplemented with 5% sucrose, while the control group received vehicle (5% sucrose in drinking water). For the OMM2.3-Luc model, mice were treated daily with 30 mg/kg CCS1477 (TargetMol, # T10717 ) via oral gavage, while the control group received vehicle (200 μl of 0.5% CMC-Na solution). Mice were euthanized at the indicated time points, and tumors were collected for histological and IHC analysis. Statistical analysis Data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) unless otherwise specified. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software. Statistical significance was determined using two-sided Student’s t tests for comparisons between two groups and analysis of variance (ANOVA) for comparisons among multiple groups. The specific ANOVA models, post hoc tests and other statistical methods are indicated in the corresponding figure legends. P &lt; 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
