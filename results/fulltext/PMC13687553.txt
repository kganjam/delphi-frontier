Introduction The capacity to regenerate upon injury varies widely across tissues and organisms, and understanding the mechanisms that govern this regenerative potential remains a central question in developmental biology. One example of this variability is found in sensory systems. Mechanosensory hair cells in the vertebrate inner ear are essential for hearing and balance, but their loss is irreversible in mammals. In contrast, the zebrafish sensory lateral line system robustly regenerates hair cells after injury 1 – 3 , making it a powerful model to study the cellular and molecular basis of regeneration. The lateral line is composed of mechanosensory organs, called neuromasts, that are organized in linear patterns along the head and trunk (Fig. 1a ). During development, trunk neuromasts are periodically deposited by a lateral line primordium that originates posterior to the otic placode and migrates to the tip of the tail 4 . At 5 days post fertilization (dpf), mature neuromasts consist of 50–70 cells and contain mechanosensory hair cells that resemble the mammalian inner ear hair cells in morphology and function 5 . These hair cells are surrounded by distinct populations of support cells and an outer ring of mantle cells that form the boundary of the organ (Fig. 1b, c ) 6 , 7 . Central support cells, located in the center of the organ below the hair cells, serve as the primary hair cell progenitor population and divide symmetrically to give rise to new hair cells during both homeostasis and regeneration 7 , 8 . Support cells adjacent to mantle cells also proliferate symmetrically but give rise to support cells 6 , 7 . Although several signaling pathways such as Notch, Fgf, and Wnt have been implicated in the hair cell regeneration process 7 , 9 – 11 , the transcriptional regulators that control progenitor activation and regenerative competence are not fully understood. LIM-homeodomain (LIM-HD) transcription factors represent an interesting starting point to understand the transcriptional changes associated with successful regeneration, as they have been implicated in the regulation of cell proliferation, differentiation, and survival across tissues during development 12 – 14 . Within this family, Islet-1 (Isl1) is of particular interest due to its critical role in auditory neuron development 15 – 17 and the regulation of hair cell differentiation and survival in the mouse auditory system. Ectopically induced co-expression of Isl1 and Atoh1 in supporting cells enhances their ability to transdifferentiate into hair cells 18 . Moreover, overexpression of Isl1 in the postnatal hair cells also promotes hair cell survival and prevents hearing loss caused by aging or acoustic overstimulation 19 . However, the molecular mechanism by which Isl1 regulates these functions is not clear. Recent single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) of zebrafish neuromasts revealed that isl1a is selectively expressed in central support cells and dynamically responds to hair cell death 9 , 20 . Because of its dynamic expression pattern and roles in progenitor regulation, isl1a is an excellent candidate as a regulator of the regeneration process. We explored if isl1a acts as a central modulator of the transcriptional programs leading to successful regeneration, and more specifically, whether isl1a integrates injury signals with downstream transcriptional responses involved in cell proliferation and differentiation. Using CRISPR/Cas12a-mediated genome editing, we generated a whole-gene deletion of isl1a and investigated its role in regulating neuromast cell number, cell type composition, regenerative dynamics, and transcriptional and epigenomic responses in the zebrafish lateral line. We show that loss of isl1a leads to an expansion of both central support cell and hair cell populations, with little to no effect in other cell populations, demonstrating that isl1a acts as a negative regulator of the progenitor pool size in neuromasts. We also show that loss of isl1a results in enhanced chromatin accessibility that leads to the prolonged transcriptional activation of pro-proliferation- and regeneration-related genes. The cumulative impact of these broad transcriptional changes results in a striking increase in support cell numbers. Our data demonstrate that isl1a is a critical negative regulator of regeneration, providing new insights into the transcriptional and epigenomic constraints that orchestrate the regenerative response. Results isl1a is expressed in central support cells and hair cell progenitors during development and regeneration Our scRNA-seq analysis showed that mature 5 dpf neuromasts highly express isl1a in central support cells, which give rise to hair cell progenitors 9 (Fig. 1d, e ). Using in situ hybridization chain reaction (HCR-FISH) in developing wild-type embryos, we found that isl1a was not expressed in the migrating primordium or the first deposited neuromast at 32 h post fertilization (hpf) (Figs. 1f and S1a ). isl1a starts to be expressed in the central region of deposited neuromasts at 2 dpf (Fig. 1g ). As neuromasts mature, isl1a expression gradually becomes more restricted to the central support cell population at 5 dpf (Fig. 1h ). Given the robust expression of isl1a in central support cells during development, we wondered how isl1a expression changed throughout the regeneration process. scRNA-seq regeneration data throughout hair cell regeneration showed that isl1a is dynamically regulated during the first 10 h of regeneration after neomycin-induced hair cell death 20 . Its expression exhibited rapid changes in response to injury, with a marked upregulation observed specifically in central support cells and hair cell progenitors within 3 h following neomycin treatment (Fig. 1i ). As hair cell progenitors differentiate into young and then mature hair cells, the expression of isl1a becomes progressively downregulated (Figs. 1i and S1b ). No significant changes in expression were observed in other cell populations after injury (Fig. S1c ). To validate the changes in mRNA expression levels in the neuromast during regeneration, we performed HCR-FISH after neomycin-induced hair cell death (Fig. 1j–p ). Consistent with the transcriptomic data, isl1a expression was initially downregulated after hair cell death and was subsequently upregulated between 1–3 h after neomycin treatment (Fig. 1m, n and S1d ). This corresponds to the key time window of regeneration immediately before hair cell specification and progenitor proliferation commences 20 . Taken together, these data show that isl1a is expressed in homeostatic central support cells and hair cell progenitors after neuromasts have been deposited and is dynamically regulated during regeneration. Loss of isl1a expression leads to an increase in central support cell and hair cell numbers in homeostasis To assess the function of isl1a in neuromast development and regeneration, we analyzed an isl1a stop codon mutation ( isl1a sa29/sa29 ) 21 , 22 and generated a whole-gene deletion mutant using CRISPR/Cas12a to avoid possible effects on the phenotype by transcriptional compensation 23 ( isl1a psi97/psi97 , Fig. S2a ). The isl1a sa29/sa29 mutation removes part of the LIM-domain and the entire homeobox, but truncated isl1a mRNA is still transcribed (Fig. S2f, g ). In contrast, no isl1a mRNA is transcribed in the whole gene deletion larvae (Fig. S2h, i ). Because we did not detect differences in the severity of the phenotypes, we subsequently performed all experiments with the isl1a psi97/psi97 null allele, hereafter referred to as isl1a −/− . At 5 dpf, the isl1a −/− larvae were characterized by a severe heart edema, bradycardia and irregular heartbeat. Mutants also had a small jaw, failed to extend axonal projections (Fig. S2b–e ), and showed motility defects and a failure to inflate their swim bladder. Nonetheless, the sensory lateral line formed normally in isl1a −/− larvae, with a similar number and position of neuromasts as their sibling counterpart (Fig. 2a, b ). To test the role of isl1a in lateral line primordium migration and neuromast formation, we analyzed isl1a −/− larvae with the reporter line Tg(cxcr4b:H2A-GFP) 24 . This transgenic line labels all nuclei in the lateral line primordium and its deposited neuromasts. We quantified the cell number in the migrating primordium and the first deposited neuromast at 32 hpf in isl1a −/− larvae and sibling controls (Fig. S2j, k ) and found no significant differences between sibling and mutant larvae at this stage (Fig. S2l, m ). These results demonstrate that neither primordium migration nor newly deposited neuromasts were affected by the loss of isl1a . To determine if isl1a influenced neuromast development as the organs mature, we analyzed a lateral line-specific reporter line (Tg(she:H2B-EGFP)) 25 and a hair cell-specific reporter line (Tg(myo6b:H2B-mScarlet-I)) 25 . Quantification of the total number of neuromast cells and hair cells in isl1a −/− larvae revealed a gradual increase in neuromast cell number and hair cell number in the isl1a −/− larvae that became significantly different from siblings at 5 dpf (Fig. 2c–f ). However, when we calculated the ratio of hair cells to total neuromast cell number (hair cell ratio), there was no significant difference (Fig. 2g ). This shows that, although isl1a −/− neuromasts possess significantly more hair cells by 5 dpf, this increase is proportional to the increase in total neuromast cell numbers. Since neuromasts are not only comprised of hair cells, we examined whether the increase in neuromast cell number in isl1a −/− larvae also affected other cell populations. Using a combination of transgenic lines, we quantified hair cells (Tg (myo6b:H2B-mScarlet-I)) , central support cells (Tg(fgf3:H2B-mturquoise2)) 9 , and mantle cells (ET20:EGFP) 26 in 5 dpf homeostatic sibling and isl1a −/− neuromasts (Fig. 2h, i ). isl1a −/− neuromasts showed an increase in hair cells and central support cells (Fig. 2j, k ), but not in mantle cells or other support cell types (Fig. 2l, m ). Altogether, and consistent with its spatial and temporal expression, these data indicate that isl1a is not required for posterior lateral line primordium migration or neuromast deposition but is essential for regulating the number of central support cells as neuromasts mature. isl1a −/− neuromasts show enhanced hair cell regeneration driven by overproliferation Since isl1a −/− neuromasts are characterized by an increased number of central support cells, we wondered if loss of isl1a would also lead to an enhanced regenerative response following injury. We therefore ablated hair cells at 5 dpf with neomycin and quantified the number of regenerated hair cells (Figs. 3a, b and S3a, b ). At 48 h after ablation, isl1a −/− neuromasts showed a significant increase in total cell and hair cell number (Fig. 3c, d ). We then asked whether the increase of hair cells in isl1a −/− mutants was the result of overproliferation of central support cells specifically. Previous work has demonstrated that during regeneration in the zebrafish lateral line, two distinct support cell populations are proliferating. Peripheral support cells divide and give rise to two new support cells (amplifying divisions), and hair cell progenitor cells divide and give rise to two hair cells (differentiating divisions) 7 . We defined amplifying divisions as EdU+ support cells in direct contact with mantle cells, which have been proposed to be the niche for amplifying cells 7 . We performed EdU incorporation assays for the first 24 h after hair cell death and calculated the EdU indexes (Fig. 3e, f ). We observed a significant increase in both differentiating and amplifying cell divisions in isl1a −/− mutants (Fig. 3g ). These differences in the EdU indexes were preserved when incorporating EdU from 24 h to 48 h after hair cell death (Fig. S3c–f ). Together, these data indicate that the increased hair cell production in isl1a mutants is driven by increased proliferation of central support cells, the direct progenitors of hair cells, as well as increased amplifying divisions that can give rise to hair cells if medially displaced. The increase in amplifying cell proliferation is likely a non-cell-autonomous effect as isl1a expression in amplifying support cells is negligible (Fig. S1e ). To further test if isl1a normally inhibits cell proliferation in neuromasts, we generated a heat shock isl1a overexpression line Tg(hsp70l:isl1a;myl7:EGFP) , hereafter (hs:isl1a OE) (Fig. S3g–j ). We heat-shocked 5 dpf larvae and performed a regeneration assay with EdU incorporation for 24 h (Fig. 3h, i ). Calculation of the EdU indexes revealed a significant decrease in both differentiating and amplifying cell divisions in hs:isl1a OE neuromasts when compared to siblings, with an overall decrease in cell proliferation (Fig. 3j ). In summary, we demonstrated by both loss- and gain-of-function experiments that during regeneration, isl1a negatively regulates support cell proliferation. The role of isl1a in regeneration is mediated by its neuromast-autonomous activity in the neuromast The EdU incorporation assay in regenerating isl1a −/− neuromasts showed not only an increase in cell proliferation (EdU + cells) in the neuromast but also in the surrounding skin (Figs. 3f and S3e ). Although isl1a does not appear to be expressed in the skin cells in zebrafish larvae at 5 dpf, it is expressed in sensory neurons 27 – 29 that are mostly missing in isl1a −/− larvae. Work by others has shown that axonal terminations can regulate epidermal adhesion 30 , which in turn can regulate proliferation in the skin 31 . We therefore wondered if the proliferation defect in neuromasts was secondary to the requirement of isl1a in the surrounding neurons/skin or if the neuromast proliferation defect was due to a neuromast-autonomous role of isl1a . To generate a neuromast-specific isl1a deletion, we transplanted cells from isl1a −/− and sibling embryos, expressing two fluorescent reporters that label lateral line cell membranes (Tg(cldnb:lynEGFP)) 32 and nuclei (Tg(cldnb:H2A-mCherry)) 7 , into TU wild-type embryos (Fig. 4a ). We then ablated hair cells in transplanted larvae at 5 dpf and quantified hair cell and neuromast cell numbers 48 h after injury (Fig. 4b–e ). Transplanted isl1a −/− cells led to a significant increase in neuromast cell number and hair cell number when compared to transplanted sibling cells (Fig. 4f, g ). This recapitulates the observed phenotype in non-manipulated larvae, demonstrating that the neuromast phenotype is due to the requirement of isl1a in neuromasts and not in the surrounding tissues. The increase in size and hair cell number persisted in transplanted fish raised to 30 dpf (Fig. S4a–d ). Given that loss of isl1a leads to a specific increase in the central support cell and hair cell pools in homeostasis, we next asked whether isl1a affects only these cell populations in regeneration. We again used a combination of transgenic lines and HCR-FISH to label different neuromast cell populations and quantified these populations in isl1a −/− and sibling neuromasts 48 h after hair cell death (Fig. 4h, i ). Consistent with the homeostatic analysis, only the central support cells and hair cells displayed a significant increase in isl1a −/− neuromasts in regeneration (Fig. 4j, k ), whereas dorso-ventral (DV), anterior-posterior (AP) and mantle cell populations remained unaffected in mutants (Fig. 4l, m ), indicating an expansion of the isl1a -positive population. Importantly, although we detected a small statistical difference, the overall ratio of hair cells to central support cells remained comparable between genotypes (Fig. 4n ), suggesting that the increased hair cell number reflects proportional expansion of the central support cell pool rather than enhanced differentiation of support cells into hair cells. Together, the transplantation experiments and mutant analyses of neuromast cell populations demonstrate that during regeneration, isl1a limits central support cell proliferation cell-autonomously. Amplifying cell proliferation is upregulated secondarily by loss of isl1a , as isl1a is not expressed in this cell population. How central support cells signal to amplifying cells remains to be determined. isl1a represses pro-proliferative signaling and modulates regeneration-associated gene expression To investigate how isl1a regulates gene expression in the lateral line, we performed bulk RNA sequencing (bulk RNA-seq) on FAC-Sorted neuromast cells isolated from isl1a −/− and sibling 5 dpf larvae in homeostasis and 3 h post-neomycin treatment, when isl1a is most highly re-expressed after hair cell death (Figs. 5a and 1n ). Differential gene expression analysis revealed that during homeostasis, approximately 400 genes are differentially expressed between isl1a mutants and siblings, with the number of upregulated genes strongly increasing 3 h after hair cell death (Fig. 5b ). This supports the idea that isl1a regulates regeneration of the lateral line by mostly repressing gene expression (Fig. S5a–c and Supplementary data 1 ). Heatmaps of the top up- and downregulated genes illustrate the broad transcriptional shift in isl1a mutants in regeneration (Fig. 5c, d ). To characterize the role of isl1a during regeneration, we performed pathway enrichment analysis 33 (Fig. S5d ) using the differentially expressed gene list at 3 h after hair cell death. Most differentially upregulated genes in the isl1a −/− regeneration dataset were associated with pathways involved in the regulation of cell proliferation. Among the most highly enriched terms were tight junctions and focal adhesion junctions, MAPK, Wnt, and ErbB signaling, supporting the hypothesis that isl1a negatively regulates cell proliferation. A similar pathway analysis with the downregulated genes did not reveal any enriched pathways. We next asked when, during regeneration, isl1a plays its most critical role. Our previous scRNA-seq regeneration analysis identified three sequentially activated modules in regeneration: an immediate inflammatory response along with the downregulation of progenitor cell maintenance genes (Module 1), followed by transient regeneration-specific gene activation (Module 2), and, finally, a robust reactivation of developmental programs (Module 3) (Fig. 5e ). To determine whether isl1a plays a role during a specific transcriptional phase, we systematically compared our bulk RNA-seq differential expression data at 3 h after neomycin to each of the module-specific gene sets defined in the scRNA-seq regeneration dataset. Comparison with Module 1 revealed that several injury and inflammatory response genes, that are normally only expressed for 30 min, failed to be downregulated in isl1a −/− neuromasts at 3 h post-neomycin (Fig. 5f ). We validated this prolonged mRNA expression in isl1a −/− neuromasts with HCR-FISH for junba and dusp1 in a regeneration time course that matches the scRNA-seq data (Figs. 5g–j and S5e, f ). This suggests that isl1a normally limits the duration of expression of Module 1 genes, although these genes are eventually downregulated even in the mutant. Similarly, we found that several Module 2 regeneration-specific genes were also upregulated in isl1a −/− neuromasts at 3 h after neomycin treatment (Fig. 5k ), a result that we confirmed by HCR-FISH for lima1a and cbx7a (Figs. 5l–o and S5g, h ). Fewer Module 3 genes were differentially expressed in the isl1a −/− neuromasts (Fig. 5p ), and among these, several were associated with the Wnt signaling pathway, such as ctnnb1 (Figs. 5q, r and S5i, j ) . We and others previously demonstrated that Wnt, Notch, and Fgf signaling are crucial for hair cell regeneration in the lateral line 7 , 9 – 11 . We therefore examined if members of these signaling pathways were differentially expressed in the 3 h post-neomycin transcriptomic data. Our data showed that there were only subtle differences in mRNA expression of Notch and Fgf signaling candidate genes between sibling and mutant neuromasts (Fig. S5k, l ), suggesting that isl1a is not regulating these pathways. In summary, comparison with genes belonging to pre-established regeneration modules demonstrates that isl1a plays a role in regulating the temporal dynamics of gene activation during the early phase of hair cell regeneration. Also, isl1a is regulating proliferation partially through Wnt signaling, and isl1a broadly restricts regenerative gene expression dynamics in parallel to Notch and Fgf signaling. isl1a likely restricts chromatin accessibility of proliferation-associated regulatory regions early in regeneration Given that in mouse cardiac cell progenitors, Isl1 acts as a pioneer transcription factor with the ability to drive chromatin opening 34 , 35 , we asked whether isl1a could similarly modulate chromatin accessibility during neuromast regeneration. To address this, we conducted ATAC-seq on FAC-Sorted neuromast cells from sibling and mutant larvae at 5 dpf in homeostasis, 1 h, and 3 h post-neomycin treatment (Fig. 6a ). Differential chromatin accessibility analysis revealed that isl1a −/− neuromasts exhibit a greater number of differentially accessible regions (DARs) at 1 h post injury when compared to homeostasis and 3 h post-neomycin timepoints, with a particularly strong increase in accessibility at this timepoint (Fig. 6b and Supplementary data 2 ). This finding suggests that isl1a regulates neuromast regeneration by reducing chromatin accessibility and limiting access to the underlying regulatory DNA. We therefore asked if the observed opening of the chromatin in isl1a −/− larvae impacted the transcriptional regulation captured at 3 h in regeneration. First, we assigned DARs to genes across timepoints by associating each DAR to its nearest promoter. Cross-referencing the DARs from 1 h post-neomycin with differentially expressed genes at 3 h post-neomycin revealed a strong correspondence between early chromatin opening in isl1a -/- and later upregulation of regeneration-related genes (Fig. 6c ). This suggests that epigenomic regulation at 1 h post-neomycin indeed drives gene expression changes captured at 3 h post-neomycin. To uncover broader transcription factor activity in our dataset independent of direct isl1a regulation, we analyzed the most accessible regions in isl1a −/− neuromasts to identify enriched transcription factor motifs. We found a strong enrichment of AP-1 family motifs (including jun, jund, junb, atf3, fos, fosl1 and fosl2 (Fig. S6a )), as well as motifs that belong to the ETS and homeodomain superfamilies (Fig. 6d ). Many of the transcription factors enriched in the isl1a −/− ATAC-seq data, such as atf3, jun, bach2b , and junba , were themselves significantly more accessible and upregulated in the isl1a −/− transcriptomic data in regeneration (Fig. 6e ). These results suggest that the loss of isl1a facilitates the expression of AP-1 family members to amplify the pro-proliferative and injury-responsive gene expression programs during regeneration. To test whether isl1a potentially represses chromatin accessibility directly, we curated a list of only differentially accessible ATAC-seq peaks that were associated with a differentially expressed gene in the 3 h post-neomycin transcriptomic data. Of the 805 DARs from this curated list, we retrieved 112 DARs that contained at least one Isl1 motif and were therefore presumed to be direct targets of isl1a . We classified these regions into four categories based on whether the DAR is more open or more closed in isl1a −/− mutants compared to siblings, and on whether the gene associated with it is transcriptionally up- or downregulated (Fig. 6f ). Among the 112 DARs with an Isl1 motif, 86 were more open and associated with an upregulated gene in isl1a mutants. This result suggests that isl1a acts mostly as a repressor by restricting chromatin accessibility and thereby downregulating the target genes during regeneration. To place isl1a in the regeneration gene regulatory network, we identified the transcription factors associated with the 112 DARs in regeneration that contained at least one Isl1 motif (Fig. 6g ). prdm1a, bach2b, nr3c1, jund, tfe3a and smad3a stood out for showing both increased chromatin accessibility at 1 h post-neomycin and transcriptional upregulation at 3 h post-neomycin in isl1a −/− larvae (Fig. 6h, i and S6b, c ). These transcription factors have been linked to cell proliferation, and they promote or restrict proliferation in a context-dependent manner 36 – 42 . Out of the DARs associated with prdm1a in our ATAC-seq data, only one intergenic region contains at least one Isl1 motif (Fig. 6h ). This regulatory region could be responsible for the transient central support cell expression driving central support cell proliferation 43 , which remains to be validated experimentally. The presence of Isl1 motifs in proliferation-associated transcription factors that become more accessible and upregulated in the isl1a −/− larvae suggests that isl1a may directly repress their activation during homeostasis by limiting their chromatin accessibility. Collectively, these results indicate that isl1a acts as an activator and repressor during homeostasis, as its loss leads to up- and downregulation of genes. However, during regeneration, it mainly acts as a repressor that needs to be rapidly downregulated after hair cell loss to allow upregulation of AP-1 factors and chromatin opening of pro-proliferative loci, leading to support cell proliferation and hair cell regeneration. The dynamic regulation of isl1a modulates the initiation of regeneration but also limits the magnitude and duration of the regenerative response. Discussion Although the transcriptional programs underlying hair cell regeneration have been extensively mapped in zebrafish 11 , 20 , the upstream regulators that coordinate the timing and magnitude of this response are not fully understood. Here, we identify isl1a as a component of the gene regulatory network that modulates regeneration in the zebrafish lateral line. We demonstrate that isl1a constrains proliferation during homeostasis and tempers the regenerative response by limiting progenitor expansion and chromatin accessibility at regeneration-associated loci. Regeneration-associated genes (e.g., lima1a, cbx7a, sall1b, dusp5 ), which are normally expressed in a transient, time-restricted manner, remain upregulated for an extended period upon hair cell death in isl1a −/− neuromasts. These cellular and molecular alterations culminate in overproliferation of support cells, leading to increased hair cell numbers. Despite broad upregulation of proliferation-associated genes in isl1a mutants, only populations known to proliferate in neuromasts show increased proliferation, namely central support cells and amplifying cells 7 , 44 . Notably, strong pro-proliferative perturbations such as Wnt activation similarly drive expansion within specific cell populations rather than uniformly across the neuromast 7 . What makes only certain cell populations competent to respond to pro-proliferative factors in neuromasts remains to be determined. In wild-type neuromasts, isl1a mRNA decreases rapidly after injury, coinciding with the onset of regeneration-induced gene expression. It also coincides precisely with the downregulation of Notch and Fgf signaling pathway components 7 , 9 , both of which inhibit proliferation during homeostasis and are reactivated 5 h after hair cell death. This temporal correlation, along with the fact that these pathways inhibit regeneration if overexpressed, raises the question of whether isl1a , Notch, and Fgf interact. Our data suggest that isl1a acts in parallel with Notch and Fgf signaling. Notch pathway members were not differentially expressed in the isl1a −/− regeneration dataset, and pharmacological Notch inhibition did not lead to changes in isl1a mRNA expression. Likewise, the Fgf pathway was not significantly affected in our regeneration data. This begs the question of how isl1a inhibits proliferation. isl1a appears to limit proliferation in part through the coordinated modulation of regenerative signaling cascades as revealed by the upregulation of KEGG pathways (e.g., MAPK, Wnt, ErbB) that have been implicated in the control of proliferation and regeneration 7 , 10 , 45 – 48 . However, whether isl1a limits regeneration through the induction of a specific signaling pathway or through the coordinated control of MAPK, Wnt, ErbB, tight junctions and focal adhesion in parallel remains unclear. Our data support the latter, as many genes across these pathways exhibit modest but significant transcriptional changes in isl1a −/− neuromasts. While the effect on individual genes may be subtle, the cumulative impact of these broad transcriptional changes results in a striking increase in support cell numbers. This highlights the ability of transcription factors to mediate widespread, coordinated regeneration responses through the regulation of many targets. In addition to changes captured in signaling pathways, isl1a −/− neuromasts also exhibit altered temporal dynamics of gene expression. Several regeneration-associated genes previously identified as transiently upregulated for only about 1 h during early stages of regeneration 20 displayed prolonged expression in isl1a −/− neuromasts over the regeneration time course. This prolonged activation mirrors the temporal misregulation seen in other models of enhanced regeneration, where delayed silencing of regeneration-responsive enhancers leads to sustained gene activation that can impact tissue repair 49 , 50 . The ATAC-seq analysis provides mechanistic insight into how isl1a regulates the regenerative capacity. isl1a mutants exhibit an increase in chromatin accessibility at 1 h after neomycin treatment (Fig. 6c ), indicating that isl1a normally restricts the access to regulatory regions that drive the earliest stages of the regenerative gene expression program (Module 1 genes). This model agrees with findings in other contexts in which chromatin accessibility dynamics shape the regenerative potential 51 – 54 . In addition, a targeted approach focusing on DARs that contain predicted Isl1 motifs highlighted potential direct regulatory targets of isl1a . These genes included several transcription factors such as prdm1a, bach2b , and nr3c1 , placing isl1a at the node of a regeneration-responsive gene regulatory network. However, most early opening DARs lack Isl1 motifs, suggesting that many of these regulatory regions are unlikely to be direct targets and instead reflect an indirect mechanism in which loss of isl1a permits early engagement of injury-response factors that can recruit chromatin remodelers to open regenerative regulatory elements 55 . Consistent with this model, many of the identified DARs are enriched for AP-1 transcription factor motifs, including jun, junba, atf3, fosl1, and fosl2 (Fig. S6a ), which are among the earliest transcription factors induced after injury 20 , and even more highly upregulated in the mutants. These AP-1 transcription factors drive proliferation and cellular reprogramming and promote chromatin accessibility during heart regeneration 55 – 58 . Candidate chromatin remodelers and transcription cofactors that may interact with isl1a to promote the activation of AP-1 transcription factors include Smarca4 and Brn3b/Pou4f2. The chromatin remodeler SWI/SNF ATPase Smarca4 regulates progenitor plasticity and differentiation 59 , 60 and directly interacts with Isl1 during cardiogenesis, where it acts as a pioneer factor 34 . However, Smarca4 loss leads to failure of cardiomyocyte differentiation and isl1a mutants do not possess defects in hair cell differentiation. Also, isl1a does not act as a pioneer factor in the lateral line. In the developing retina, Isl1 cooperates with Brn3b/Pou4f2 to regulate genes controlling neuronal differentiation and cell cycle exit 61 , 62 . In the zebrafish lateral line, we did not detect enrichment of Brn3/Pou4 motifs at isl1a -dependent regulatory regions. Also, isl1a mutants do not possess hair cell differentiation defects that would be expected from a decrease in Brn3/Pou4 levels. Nevertheless, isl1a possibly acts with other cofactors in the lateral line. Identifying these cofactors of isl1a during regeneration will require future biochemical and genetic studies. Previous studies have established the importance of central support cells as a progenitor pool for hair cell regeneration in zebrafish neuromasts 2 , 6 . Here, we show that isl1a limits the regeneration response by constraining progenitor proliferation and hair cell production, largely through modulation of chromatin accessibility. Although isl1a is commonly described as an activator of proliferation, differentiation, and survival across tissues during development 34 , 63 , our findings reveal that during lateral line regeneration, it acts mostly as a repressor, similar to its function during chick dorsal root ganglion formation 64 . In the mouse heart, Isl1 promotes proliferation and cardiomyocyte fate specification by acting as a pioneer transcription factor in cardiac cell progenitors 34 . Isl1 also controls neuronal differentiation during retinal development, where it promotes cell cycle exit and specification of distinct neuronal subtypes 65 . Therefore, the question arose whether isl1a could similarly promote hair cell differentiation while suppressing proliferation in neuromasts. However, our data show that isl1a is not a differentiation factor during lateral line regeneration. In isl1a mutants, support cells retain the ability to differentiate into hair cells and even generate more hair cells during regeneration, arguing against a model in which loss of isl1a maintains cells in an undifferentiated proliferative state. Consistent with previous work showing that proliferation and differentiation are uncoupled in the zebrafish lateral line 44 , our findings demonstrate a previously underappreciated role for isl1a in restricting the magnitude and duration of regenerative proliferation, rather than cell differentiation. In the mammalian auditory system, postnatal overexpression of Isl1 in hair cells protects them against age and noise-induced damage and improves survival 19 , while co-overexpression of Isl1 and Atoh1 enhances supporting-to-hair-cell conversion 18 . The latter finding suggests a role for Isl1 in hair cell differentiation in the mouse cochlea that is different from its role in the lateral line. To date, the consequences of Isl1 loss of function on chromatin accessibility or proliferation in mouse supporting cells following injury have not been described. Together, these observations show that Isl1 activity is context-dependent and that it can function as an activator or repressor depending on the cellular environment and developmental stage. In zebrafish lateral line regeneration, isl1a constrains progenitor expansion via chromatin regulation. Whether the effects of Isl1 during development and regeneration in other organisms and tissues are also caused by chromatin accessibility changes remains to be determined. Our findings demonstrate that isl1a plays an unexpected, pivotal role in limiting the regenerative response of the zebrafish lateral line by constraining chromatin accessibility and transcription of proliferation- and regeneration-specific genes. Injury-induced downregulation of isl1a promotes the initiation of regeneration, while its subsequent re-expression restricts the duration of the response, thereby fine-tuning hair cell regeneration and maintaining appropriate support cell numbers. ATAC-seq data processing ATAC-seq data were processed using a previously described pipeline 74 with minor modifications, as detailed below. Paired-end reads were aligned to the Danio rerio genome assembly danRer11 using Bowtie2 77 (v2.3.5.1). Picard 78 (v2.23.8) was then used to mark aligned BAM files and remove PCR duplicates based on unique fragment coordinates. BAM files were reoriented to correct for the Tn5 enzymatic offset introduced during transposition by shifting the reads +4/−4 and were then filtered to remove any fragments greater than 600 bp. Coverage bigwig files were generated in R, and normalized coverage was concurrently generated using reads-per-million (RPM) normalization by scaling the coverage to the total number of reads in the sample divided by 1 million. ATAC-seq peaks were called using MACS2 79 (2.2.7.1) with default parameters. For all downstream analyses, only called peaks that were reproducible across replicates after applying the Irreproducible Discovery Rate filter 80 were used. ATAC-seq coverage tracks were displayed using pyGenomeTracks (v3.8). Differential chromatin accessibility analysis To determine the differentially accessible regions (DARs) between siblings and isl1a −/− mutants at all time points, we performed DESeq2 analysis with default parameters and FDR = 0.05 81 . First, we calculated the total ATAC-seq signal in a 2000 bp window centered on each ATAC-seq peak for all replicates of each experimental condition. In this manner, we produced a DESeq2 model encompassing ATAC-seq counts from all experiments. We then used DESeq to conduct pairwise comparisons between sibling and isl1a −/− mutant conditions at each time point and calculated the log2FC ( isl1a −/− /sibling) values. Peaks with a log2FC value &gt; 0.5 and an adjusted p -value of &lt;0.05 were classified as a gain in chromatin accessibility, while peaks with a log2FC value &lt; −0.5 and an adjusted p -value of &lt;0.05 were classified as a loss in chromatin accessibility. Aggregate ATAC-seq signal plots were generated in R using ggplot2 and cowplot packages, and heatmaps were generated using the pheatmap package. ATAC-seq motif enrichment analysis Using the list of DARs at 1 h post-neomycin, a FASTA file was generated for the top 200 peaks with increased accessibility in isl1a −/− . The MEME suite tool AME 82 (v5.3.0) was then used to search for enriched TF motifs, with a background sequence set consisting of a sample of 200 non-differential accessible peaks for the same timepoint. The JASPAR 2022 vertebrate core TF motif database 83 was used as a reference for AME. Motif enrichment bar plot was generated in R using the ggplot2 package. To predict potential isl1a direct regulation, the lists of DARs for homeostasis and 1 h post-neomycin were cross-referenced to the differentially expressed gene list at 3 h post-neomycin. Only the regions that matched transcriptomic differentially expressed genes were kept. The resulting lists of DARs were subsequently scanned for Isl1 binding sites (JASPAR2024 Motif ID: MA1608.2, derived from mouse ChIP-seq data). DARs containing at least one Isl1 motif were considered putative Isl1 targets. Each motif-containing DAR was annotated for chromatin accessibility change, direction of associated gene expression changes and whether the associated gene encoded a transcription factor or cofactor 84 . This classification allowed for systematic assessment of potential Isl1-dependent regulation. Image analysis Image processing and analysis were performed in ImageJ 85 . For quantification of HCR-FISH fluorescence intensity in lateral line neuromasts, images were Z-projected (maximum intensity projection), and background was subtracted using the “Subtract Background” function with a rolling ball radius of 50 pixels. An ROI was drawn around the neuromast, and the fluorescence intensity was measured using the “Measure” function. The mean fluorescence intensity values were used for statistical analysis and displayed in GraphPad Prism 10 (v10.4.1). Statistical analysis All statistical tests and plots, unless specified, were done in GraphPad Prism 10 (v10.4.1). Normal distribution of data was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. For pairwise comparisons between sibling and mutant groups, unpaired two-tailed t -tests were used, Welch’s correction was applied when variances were unequal, and two-tailed Mann–Whitney U tests when normality was not met. p -values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Holm-Šidák method. For multiple timepoint comparisons, data from sibling and mutant groups were analyzed using two-way ANOVA (genotype, timepoint) with Šidák’s multiple comparison test. For comparison across multiple groups with unequal variances, Brown-Forsythe and Welch ANOVA followed by Dunnett’s T3 multiple comparisons test was applied.
