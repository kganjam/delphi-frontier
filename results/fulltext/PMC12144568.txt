1. Introduction The ability to repair and regenerate damaged tissues represents one of the most fundamental challenges in modern medicine, with profound implications for human health and longevity. Tissue regeneration encompasses a complex array of biological processes through which cells and tissues naturally repair and restore themselves following injury or disease [ 1 ]. This intrinsic capability is important for maintaining homeostasis and facilitating recovery, allowing organisms to heal wounds, replace damaged cells, and, in some cases, regrow entire tissues or organs [ 2 ]. Current strategies, including stem cell therapy [ 3 , 4 ], grafting [ 5 , 6 ], and bioengineered materials [ 1 , 7 – 9 ], offer promising solutions but also face significant challenges such as the risk of rejection [ 10 ], invasive procedures [ 11 ], limited efficacy [ 12 ], and non-specificity [ 13 – 15 ]. These limitations have driven the need for more innovative and effective therapeutic approaches. Bioelectronics represents an interdisciplinary domain at the confluence of biology, electronics, and materials science. The integration of bioelectronics with tissue regeneration has driven significant advancements in repairing and replacing damaged tissues [ 16 – 20 ]. In the context of tissue regeneration, bioelectronics offers promising strategies to enhance the repair or replacement of damaged tissues across various systems. The foundation of bioelectronics in tissue regeneration is built on two primary mechanisms: bioelectric and physicochemical pathways [ 21 – 24 ]. Bioelectric strategies involve the use of electrical fields or currents to influence cellular behavior, crucial for processes such as cell migration, proliferation, and differentiation [ 25 ]. Concurrently, the field of bioelectronics heavily relies on the development and application of advanced materials with specific physicochemical properties tailored to interact optimally with biological tissues [ 26 ]. These materials are designed to mimic the natural cellular environment, thus supporting the integration and functioning of regenerated tissues. Building on these foundational principles and recent advances in flexible electronics and nanotechnology, device-based strategies now integrate bioelectric and physicochemical cues to guide cellular behavior and improve healing outcomes. For example, bioelectronic devices can deliver controlled electrical impulses to target tissues, enhancing cellular activity and accelerating repair [ 27 – 30 ]. When combined with biosensors, these systems facilitate real-time monitoring of physiological responses and tissue regeneration progress, allowing clinicians to fine-tune treatment protocols as needed [ 14 , 31 , 32 ]. Bioelectronic scaffolds further incorporate conductive materials to support cellular growth and guide tissue formation through electrical signals [ 33 , 34 ]. To overcome size limitations and avoid surgical removal, ongoing research explores energy harvesting methods that convert endogenous sources, such as movement or heat, into power for these implants, ensuring sustained functionality without external power inputs [ 35 , 36 ]. Collectively, these strategies leverage bioelectric and physicochemical principles to optimize cellular processes and support functional recovery in regenerative medicine. In parallel, materials engineering for bioelectronics focuses on improving conductive properties to reinforce the electrical and biochemical signaling vital for tissue repair and regeneration [ 37 , 38 ]. Enhanced conductivity facilitates more effective communication pathways, ensuring the promotion and guidance of essential cellular activities. The mechanical properties of these materials can also be customized to mirror those of the target tissue, maintaining compatibility and minimizing adverse reactions [ 39 ]. Biocompatibility is central to fostering harmonious integration within the biological environment, and incorporating bioresorbable features allows these materials to degrade at a pace aligned with new tissue growth [ 40 , 41 ]. By refining these material characteristics, emerging material-based strategies aim to support interventions that restore, maintain, or enhance tissue function in a controlled and sustainable manner. Considering these developments and challenges, this review examines the fundamental bioelectric and physicochemical mechanisms in bioelectronics and their application in tissue regeneration, followed by an analysis of innovative materials for tissue-electronics interfaces. The exploration extends to both material-based and device-based applications of these mechanisms in regenerating diverse tissue types, including neuronal, cardiac, skin, musculoskeletal, and gastrointestinal tissues, with an emphasis on adapting strategies to the unique environmental demands of each tissue type. We critically examine current challenges, including scale-up manufacturing, long-term stability, and regulatory considerations. This review concludes by synthesizing the prospects and challenges in the field of bioelectronics for tissue regeneration, proposing several viable directions for future research to advance the discipline. 2. Mechanisms, principles, and material innovations in tissue regeneration Bioelectric signaling, driven by transmembrane potentials and ion fluxes, provides essential cues guiding tissue regeneration, influencing cell behavior, pattern formation, and structural organization across diverse biological systems. This intricate control mechanism integrates with the physicochemical properties of materials at the nano-bio interface, where surface chemistry, ionic environments, and electric fields shape cellular responses. Emerging strategies harness these bioelectric and physicochemical principles, employing electroactive, photoactive, and conductive materials to direct cellular activities and initiate regenerative cascades. 2.1. Bioelectric signaling 2.1.1. Fundamental bioelectric signaling Bioelectric signaling serves as a critical control mechanism in tissue regeneration, utilizing voltage gradients and ionic currents as critical instructive signals for cellular organization and tissue repair. This fundamental process involves the integrated activities of ion channels and pumps that establish transmembrane potentials ( V mem ), thereby creating spatial voltage gradients across tissues, given by the Goldman–Hodgkin–Katz equation (1) [ 22 , 42 ]. (1) V m e m = R T F l n ∑ i N P M i + M i + o u t + ∑ j M P X j - X j - i n ∑ i N P M i + M i + i n + ∑ j M P X j - X j - o u t where R is the ideal gas constant, T is the absolute temperature, and F is the Faraday constant. P M + and M + , and P X - and X - denote the permeability and concentration of monovalent cation and anion, respectively. These voltage gradients are crucial for encoding and transmitting morphogenetic information that dictates regenerative parameters such as cell type, tissue size, positional information, axial polarity, and organ identity [ 43 – 45 ]. Within these regenerative systems, bioelectric circuits exhibit significant computational capabilities, functioning as analogous to biological transistors with inherent historicity—meaning past events can influence current signaling states [ 22 ]. Such computational capability is exemplified by neuroplasticity, an adaptive mechanism that modulates neuronal growth, synaptic organization, and functional plasticity [ 46 ]. This complex signaling network is facilitated by ion channels and gap junctions, the latter acting as electrochemical synapses that allow for the propagation of signals between cells, enabling the transfer of electrical currents and small chemical messengers [ 42 , 47 ]. Furthermore, bioelectric networks utilize voltage-sensitive mechanisms that modulate cell behavior, regulating critical cellular behaviors [ 48 ]. Electric fields exert forces on charged particles, directing migration along electric field lines, a phenomenon also applicable to macromolecules like proteins based on their charge and size [ 49 ]. Cells utilize endogenous intracellular electric fields (oriented from nucleus to membrane) to regulate protein distribution, enabling Coulombic interactions that facilitate targeted trafficking of signaling proteins between cellular compartments, bypassing stochastic diffusion processes [ 50 , 51 ]. This intracellular electrophoresis greatly increases the speed and precision of signal transduction, as evidenced by studies showing membrane-associated proteins rapidly translocating to the nucleus after phosphorylation and returning after dephosphorylation. Action potentials observed in neurons and muscles are fast-acting events. In contrast, ion pumps, channels, and pores orchestrate the slower distribution of specific ion species across cellular plasma membranes, creating spatial patterns of resting potential. These patterns, crucial for intercellular communication, are transmitted through electrical synapses known as gap junctions, promoting both electrical coupling and the diffusion of small molecules between cells [ 7 ]. Electrical synapses are fundamentally distinct from chemical synapses: while chemical synapses rely on neurotransmitter release and receptor binding across a synaptic cleft to transmit signals, electrical synapses directly connect adjacent cells via gap junction channels, enabling rapid and bidirectional exchange of ions and signaling molecules. This direct electrical coupling allows for rapid, synchronized signaling among groups of cells [ 52 ]. Physiological electric fields can directly influence protein conformation by stabilizing specific low-energy states [ 51 ]. Bioelectric signaling mechanisms exhibit broad applicability across diverse tissue regeneration contexts. For example, in neural tissues, transient electrical stimulation (ES) has been shown to accelerate axon regeneration and enhance neurotrophic factor expression, offering therapeutic outcomes comparable to prolonged stimulation [ 53 , 54 ]. In cardiac tissues, semiconductor-based photoelectrochemical systems generate precise spatiotemporal electric currents for cardiac pacing, demonstrating effectiveness in both isolated cardiac tissue and intact animal models [ 55 , 56 ]. Bioelectric cues also synergize with biochemical signaling networks through multiple transduction mechanisms. In neural regeneration contexts, ES influences cell cycles, supports directed axonal growth, and modulates microglial distribution, thereby creating a regenerative microenvironment [ 57 , 58 ]. The established voltage gradients act as a regulatory system, guiding critical cellular activities such as migration, proliferation, and differentiation. This regulatory capability is particularly evident in stem cell populations, where changes in transmembrane potential can selectively control cellular proliferation and differentiation [ 59 , 60 ]. Recent technological advancements have further enhanced the ability to manipulate bioelectric signals, broadening the scope of regenerative medicine applications. Experimental studies have shown that modulating bioelectric states can induce the regeneration of complex structures, such as limbs in typically non-regenerative species, through targeted ion channel modulation [ 61 – 63 ], Moreover, normalization of aberrant cellular electrical networks has been demonstrated to influence cancer progression, demonstrating the potential of bioelectric interventions to initiate morphogenetic cascades resulting in structured and functional tissue regeneration [ 64 , 65 ]. 2.1.2. Cellular mechanisms The molecular mechanisms translating bioelectric signals into cellular responses have been increasingly elucidated through recent research. These mechanisms operate both upstream and downstream of traditionally studied genetic and biochemical elements, highlighting diverse mechanisms by which voltage changes influence cellular behavior ( Fig. 1a ). One of the primary transduction mechanisms involves calcium signaling mediated by voltage-gated calcium channels [ 66 ]. Intracellular calcium levels are stringently regulated, enabling precise modulation of numerous cellular responses. Voltage-gated calcium entry activates intracellular pathways such as calmodulin and calcineurin signaling cascades, which sustain cellular responses beyond the initial calcium transient [ 67 ]. Beyond Ca 2+ , electric fields also modulate gradients of other critical physiological ions such as sodium (Na + ), potassium (K + ), chloride (Cl − ), and protons (H + ). For instance, endogenous electric fields established by Na + transport create trans-epithelial voltage gradients significant enough (~1–5 V/cm) to direct cell migration and morphogenesis in developmental and regenerative processes [ 68 ]. Such fields also orient cell movements during wound healing, driven largely by the directional movement of Na + and Cl − ions [ 69 ]. Furthermore, rapid alterations in membrane potential, exemplified by action potentials, critically depend on voltage-gated Na + influx and K + efflux. This coupling between ionic currents and bioelectric signals initiates downstream cascades involving secondary ions like Ca 2+ and contributes directly to cellular responses in regenerative contexts [ 68 , 69 ]. Additionally, voltage-sensitive phosphatases (VSPs) constitute another key mechanism linking electrical signals to biochemical pathways. Voltage-sensitive phosphatases hydrolyze phosphoinositides upon membrane depolarization, directly linking membrane potential changes to intracellular signaling cascades involved in cell growth and differentiation [ 70 – 72 ]. Injury-induced Cl − currents shape endogenous electric fields critical for wound healing and regeneration [ 73 ]. Such Cl − currents are regulated as an active response to tissue injury, underscoring their role in bioelectric regeneration signaling [ 73 ]. Proton flux through voltage-gated channels (e.g., Hv1) modulates intracellular pH and metabolic pathways, linking electrical cues to biochemical signaling. Thus, proton flux couples electric signals to downstream metabolic and signaling pathways relevant in regeneration, development, and immune responses [ 74 , 75 ]. Bioelectric gradients also regulate neurotransmitter distribution, such as serotonin, through voltage-dependent transport mechanisms [ 76 ]. For example, serotonin transport mediated by bioelectric gradients critically influences developmental patterning, such as left-right axis establishment in frog embryos [ 77 – 79 ]. Bioelectric signaling also interfaces with epigenetic regulation, exemplified by voltage-dependent uptake of butyrate via SLC5A8 transporters, influencing chromatin modifications and gene transcription critical in regeneration and tumor suppression [ 80 – 82 ]. 2.1.3. Tissue-level integration Bioelectric networks facilitate sophisticated tissue-level integration, coordinating regenerative processes over large anatomical distances crucial for complex biological functions and developmental patterning ( Fig. 1a ). Early research by Burr and colleagues demonstrated that voltage gradients could predict developmental patterns across various organisms, suggesting that bioelectric patterns contain essential information that guides tissue organization [ 83 , 84 ]. At the tissue level, bioelectric signaling is crucial for axial patterning during regeneration. Experimental work by Marsh and Beams demonstrated that applying external electric fields to regenerating planarian fragments can reverse anterior-posterior polarity, with the orientation of the cut relative to the electric field influencing the regeneration outcome, leading to normal regeneration or the formation of double-headed worms depending on the polarity [ 85 , 86 ]. Molecular studies further indicate that such head-versus-tail identities are controlled by H + /K + -ATPase-mediated membrane depolarization, highlighting the molecular basis for bioelectric control of axial polarity [ 87 ]. Galvanotaxis, or electrotaxis—the directed migration of cells in response to electric fields—is a critical mechanism enabling tissue-level bioelectric integration. Galvanotaxis guides cells toward injury sites or growth areas during regeneration. Endogenous electric fields naturally form at sites of injury, directing epithelial cells, neural progenitors, mesenchymal stem cells, and immune cells toward wounds or regenerating tissues [ 88 ]. These electric fields induce directional migration through polarization and electrophoretic redistribution of membrane receptors and ion channels, creating intracellular signaling asymmetry that establishes a front-rear polarity for cell migration [ 88 , 89 ]. For instance, epithelial cells at wound edges migrate toward the cathode (negative pole), driven by polarized activation of EGFR, integrins, and PI3K signaling cascades [ 89 , 90 ]. Redistribution of ion channels, particularly calcium-permeable and potassium channels, further supports galvanotactic sensing and promotes actin cytoskeletal reorganization for sustained directional migration [ 88 , 91 , 92 ]. Thus, galvanotaxis emerges as a fundamental mechanism through which bioelectric signals achieve coordinated, large-scale tissue regeneration by steering cellular behaviors over substantial anatomical distances. Bioelectric coherence at the tissue level is particularly evident during regenerative processes that require coordinated cell behaviors across significant distances, such as in planarians, where bioelectric circuits manage complex morphological outcomes [ 93 ]. Additionally, bioelectric prepatterns in organisms like Xenopus play crucial roles in demarcating future organ fields before the onset of organ-specific gene expression, thus influencing organ development and positioning [ 94 ]. Bioelectric signals facilitate complex problem-solving capabilities within regenerative contexts. Exposure of planarians to gap junction blockers, for instance, has been shown to induce head morphologies characteristic of distantly related species, demonstrating access to diverse morphological repertoires solely via bioelectric modulation [ 95 , 96 ]. The robust decision-making capabilities of bioelectric networks are also evident in scenarios where tissue damage or removal occurs. Classical studies demonstrated adaptive tissue responses in regeneration; for example, newt kidney tubules maintain correct tubule diameter despite variations in cell size, illustrating bioelectric coordination at the tissue level [ 97 ]. Bioelectric networks also exhibit robust pattern memory capabilities at the tissue level. Recent research has shown that modulation of gap junction connectivity can induce a permanent two-headed morphology in genetically wild-type planaria, persisting through subsequent regeneration cycles without further manipulation [ 98 ]. Such pattern memory is encoded within the global bioelectric gradient and can be reset via manipulation of H + /K + -ATPase-mediated membrane potentials [ 99 ]. Furthermore, bioelectric signals have also been implicated in craniofacial morphogenesis, where disruptions in bioelectric prepatterns can shift gene expression boundaries, affecting the resulting morphology [ 100 , 101 ]. These findings underscore the instructive and regulatory roles of bioelectric signals in tissue organization and regeneration. The tissue-level integration of bioelectric signals enables “cognitive scaling”—the capacity of cellular collectives to accomplish tissue-level objectives beyond the scope of individual cells [ 102 ]. This creates a system where no single cell needs to understand the complete pattern, yet the collective reliably achieves complex anatomical patterns through coordinated bioelectric interactions. 2.1.4. Applications in regenerative medicine The exploration of bioelectric signaling mechanisms has catalyzed significant advancements in regenerative medicine, leading to novel therapeutic strategies for complex tissue restoration. These applications effectively harness bioelectric signals to modulate cellular behaviors and orchestrate large-scale tissue and organ dynamics [ 22 , 44 , 103 , 104 ]. A particularly promising application has been demonstrated in amphibian regeneration studies, where specific bioelectric states can induce the regeneration of complex structures. Amphibian tails, including spinal cord tissue, have been stimulated to regenerate through targeted ion channel modulation and optogenetic stimulation [ 15 , 16 ]. Such interventions highlight the capacity of transient bioelectric signals to initiate extensive regenerative processes, reflecting mechanisms analogous to natural bioelectric controls involved in developmental patterning and regeneration [ 65 ]. In neural regeneration, bioelectric approaches have shown remarkable success primarily in animal models, conceptualized by the analogy of biological “hardware versus software” [ 44 , 105 ]. In this analogy, the biological “hardware” refers to structural defects in tissue architecture or permanent genetic mutations affecting neural development—these are akin to physical or genetic constraints embedded within the biological system [ 105 – 107 ]. In contrast, the biological “software” is represented by dynamic bioelectric signals, such as membrane voltage patterns, ion channel activities, and intercellular electrical communication, which can be readily modulated without altering the underlying genetic code or structural makeup [ 44 , 104 ]. Studies conducted in Xenopus laevis (African clawed frog) embryos provide clear illustrations of this analogy: bioelectric interventions using “software-level” manipulations, such as targeted hyperpolarization through ectopic expression of ion channels, successfully reversed severe brain morphology defects induced by disruption of neurogenesis genes such as Notch [ 105 – 107 ]. Similarly, bioelectric interventions enhancing the activity of specific ion channels (e.g., HCN2) have effectively reversed severe neurodevelopmental defects induced by teratogens like nicotine in frog embryos, demonstrating bioelectric modulation’s potential to correct developmental defects at embryonic stages [ 107 ]. This compelling evidence indicates that bioelectric signals, acting as modifiable “software,” possess a powerful capacity to reprogram or compensate for faulty “hardware” without genetic editing or physical reconstruction. However, direct translation of these approaches to human neural regeneration remains preliminary. Recent studies using human induced pluripotent stem cells (hiPSCs) demonstrated responsiveness to electrical stimuli, where electrical stimulation alone can effectively guide differentiation toward neural lineages without chemical morphogens [ 108 ]. Despite these encouraging early-stage findings, bioelectric protocols have yet to demonstrate complete regeneration of complex brain structures in human neural organoids or clinical contexts. This highlights the need for further translational research to bridge powerful animal-model outcomes to practical human therapeutic applications. The applications extend to cancer treatment, where bioelectric modulation serves as a strategy to suppress pathological regeneration, such as cancer, contrasting its regenerative use in tissue repair. Unlike approaches aimed at promoting controlled tissue growth, bioelectric cancer therapies seek to suppress uncontrolled proliferation by restoring cancer cells to normal bioelectric states [ 64 , 109 , 110 ]. Techniques such as membrane hyperpolarization or gap junction restoration can normalize aberrant bioelectric states, thereby reducing tumor growth and promoting cell quiescence [ 111 , 112 ]. This strategy provides a promising complement or alternative to conventional chemotherapy, potentially reducing toxic side effects by selectively correcting the underlying bioelectric dysregulation of cancer cells. Furthermore, electroporation—an electric-field-based technique widely used in clinical contexts— represents a clinically relevant application of bioelectric principles. Electroporation employs short, high-voltage pulses to transiently permeabilize cellular membranes, enabling precise delivery of genetic materials, drugs, or therapeutic molecules directly into targeted cells or tissues. Clinically, electroporation has been extensively employed in cancer treatments, notably in electrochemotherapy, where localized electric pulses enhance drug uptake specifically in tumor tissues, thereby minimizing systemic toxicity [ 113 , 114 ]. Additionally, irreversible electroporation (IRE) has gained attention as a non-thermal ablation technique used to treat tumors of the liver, pancreas, kidney, and prostate in human patients, showing effectiveness with fewer adverse effects than conventional thermal ablation methods [ 113 , 115 ]. Computational tools, applying neuroscience principles at multiple organizational levels, increasingly guide these therapeutic approaches [ 116 , 117 ]. However, current biomedical approaches predominantly target molecular and genetic pathways (hardware), potentially overlooking the benefits of integrating behaviorally inspired, bioelectric interventions (software) [ 102 ]. This insight suggests that significant advances in regenerative medicine may be achieved by the comprehensive integration of molecular and bioelectric interventions. 2.2. Physicochemical properties 2.2.1. Surface chemistry and material-tissue interface The physicochemical properties of material surfaces play a fundamental role in determining successful tissue integration and regenerative outcomes. The surface chemistry of bioelectronic materials establishes interfaces that significantly modulate protein adsorption, cell adhesion, and tissue integration processes [ 118 – 120 ]. These interactions establish what is known as the “nano-bio interface”, comprising physicochemical exchanges between material surfaces and biological components [ 121 – 124 ] ( Fig. 1b ). Impedance properties are particularly crucial for bioelectronic performance. High electrochemical impedance reduces signal quality during recording and limits charge delivery during stimulation [ 125 , 126 ]. The impedance at bioelectronic interfaces depends significantly on electrode material composition, surface morphology, and the formation of the electric double layer at the electrode-tissue boundary [ 127 , 128 ]. Conducting polymers have emerged as valuable interface materials due to their hybrid electronic-ionic conductivity, which facilitates efficient charge transfer across the biological-electronic boundary [ 129 , 130 ]. Unlike traditional metal electrodes, which only support electronic conduction, conducting polymer interfaces enable both electronic and ionic signal pathways, substantially reducing interface impedance [ 131 ]. Surface charge properties directly impact bioelectronic interface performance by influencing protein adsorption and cellular interactions. Charged surfaces influence the formation of the protein corona that mediates subsequent cellular responses [ 132 ]. Conducting polymer interfaces with controlled surface charge characteristics can enhance electrode performance while minimizing inflammatory responses [ 133 , 134 ]. The mechanical mismatch between rigid electronic materials (Young’s moduli in the GPa range) and soft tissue (kPa range) represents a significant challenge for bioelectronic interfaces [ 135 , 136 ]. This mismatch can induce inflammatory responses and compromise long-term device functionality. Hydrogel-based interfaces, particularly those incorporating conducting polymers, provide mechanical properties more compatible with biological tissues while maintaining necessary electrical characteristics [ 137 ]. These materials offer high water content (like biological tissues), tissue-like elasticity, and improved conformability to complex tissue surfaces [ 138 ]. Surface topography at nano and microscales significantly affects cellular responses at bioelectronic interfaces. Nanoscale features can guide protein adsorption patterns, cell adhesion, and reduce inflammatory responses [ 139 , 140 ]. Porous structures can increase the effective surface area, reducing impedance while providing spaces for tissue integration [ 141 , 142 ]. Modern bioelectronic interfaces often employ strategic surface modifications, including nanopatterning and microstructuring, to achieve optimal electrical characteristics and biocompatibility [ 143 , 144 ]. At the biofluid-material interface, the electric double layer (EDL) forms that mediate ionic interactions and interfacial charge distribution [ 127 , 145 ]. These interfacial electric fields significantly influence molecular behavior by affecting molecular alignment and bond ionicity [ 146 , 147 ], facilitating spontaneous redox reactions [ 148 – 150 ], and modulating reactive oxygen species generation [ 151 , 152 ]. This relationship has important implications for tissue regeneration, as smaller structures typically demonstrate enhanced electrochemical activity due to their larger interfacial volume ratio [ 153 , 154 ]. Critically, the presence of the electric double layer profoundly impacts bioelectronics’ capabilities for electrical recording and stimulation. The EDL functions as a nanoscale capacitor formed at the electrode-electrolyte boundary, with electrons on the electrode surface balanced by counter-ions in solution separated by a thin solvent layer. This arrangement governs the impedance at the interface, significantly affecting signal coupling, sensitivity, and noise characteristics during electrical recordings [ 155 , 156 ]. Electrodes with a larger double-layer capacitance present lower impedance, facilitating improved biological signal coupling and higher signal-to-noise ratio (SNR). For instance, neural microelectrodes with increased surface roughness or conductive polymer coatings have demonstrated enhanced recording quality by reducing impedance and increasing SNR [ 126 , 155 , 156 ]. During electrical stimulation, the double layer’s capacitive characteristics determine the safe and effective charge injection limits. The charge delivered by bioelectrodes initially accumulates within the EDL through reversible, non-Faradaic processes, thus preventing harmful electrochemical reactions within safe voltage ranges [ 157 ]. Electrodes engineered to have high EDL capacitance—achieved through surface modifications such as increased porosity or specialized coatings (e.g., iridium oxide, platinum black)—can deliver larger stimulation currents safely and efficiently [ 157 , 158 ]. Conversely, low-capacitance electrodes undergo greater polarization, raising the risk of irreversible electrochemical reactions [ 158 ]. Thus, optimizing the EDL through careful selection of material properties (e.g., roughness, porosity, coating materials) is a critical design parameter for enhancing the performance and safety of bioelectronic implants such as neural prostheses, cardiac pacemakers, and deep-brain stimulators [ 126 , 158 ]. Surface properties act synergistically, establishing chemical-biological feedback loops critical to successful tissue regeneration [ 159 ]. Thus, precise control over surface chemistry and material-tissue interfaces is critical for advancing bioelectronic regenerative strategies. 2.2.2. Interfacial forces and cellular response The interaction between bioelectronic materials and biological systems involves multiple forces operating at different scales and ranges, creating intricate environments essential for tissue regeneration. At the molecular level, these interactions involve both associative and segregating phase transitions [ 160 ], resulting in density gradients that alter intermolecular thermodynamic equilibria. The transition from solvent-protein to protein-protein interactions creates distinct chemical environments, particularly in interfacial regions [ 161 , 162 ]. A fundamental aspect of these interfaces is the formation of electrical double layers, which create unique electrochemical features affecting cellular behavior [ 127 , 145 ]. When oppositely charged macromolecules associate, they form polyion pairs, leading to the release of corresponding counterions [ 163 , 164 ]. This process typically results in the dense phase containing fewer ions than the dilute phase [ 165 , 166 ], establishing ion concentration gradients that directly influence interfacial properties [ 167 ]. This phenomenon has been demonstrated in experimental studies using polypeptide systems, where research with poly-l-lysine and poly-d,l-glutamic acid has shown marked differences in conductivity between dilute and dense phases [ 166 ]. Interfacial electric fields further modulate molecular behavior by influencing electron density distributions and inducing polarization [ 146 , 147 ]. Van der Waals forces and electrostatic interactions serve as fundamental forces at the bio-interface, with electrostatic forces being screened within a few nanometers of the surface in biological fluids where ionic strength is typically around 150 mM [ 168 – 170 ]. The Derjaguin-Landau-Verwey-Overbeek (DLVO) theory describes these interactions adequately at larger scales, special considerations are necessary for nanoparticles due to their limited number of atoms [ 170 ]. Hydration and dehydration forces significantly influence material-biological interactions. Hydration forces increase particle stability through “hydration pressure” or “hydrophilic repulsion”, while rapid dehydration and aggregation occur when the relative affinity between interacting surfaces for water molecules is lower than between water molecules themselves [ 168 , 171 ]. These interfacial forces can promote spontaneous redox reactions [ 172 ], as evidenced in studies of stress granules showing that interfacial electric fields can facilitate reactive oxygen species production [ 173 ] and mediate protein disulfide bond formation [ 159 ]. The strength and nature of these interfacial forces evolve over time through aging processes. As molecular assemblies age, enhanced inter-residue interactions can alter the solvent environment [ 174 ], potentially leading to changes in ion translocation and physicochemical functions.. Research has shown that interfacial electric fields can significantly influence molecular behavior, affecting binding energies between enzymes and substrates [ 175 ] and catalyzing various chemical reactions [ 176 ]. Protein corona formation represents another dynamic aspect of material-biological interactions. When materials enter biological fluids, they rapidly become coated with proteins that may undergo conformational changes, leading to new epitope exposure, altered function, and avidity effects [ 177 – 179 ]. The kinetics of material-protein association and dissociation, along with protein exchange with the media, critically influence interactions with biological surfaces and receptors, with factors such as surface properties, protein concentrations, binding affinities, and environmental conditions all playing crucial roles [ 178 ]. These complex interfacial forces and their dynamic nature are fundamental to tissue regeneration processes. Recent research has revealed that these interactions extend beyond traditional surface chemistry to include sophisticated electrochemical signaling mechanisms that influence cellular behavior [ 180 ]. Precise control over these interactions holds substantial promise for advancing bioelectronic device design, targeted molecular delivery, and improved tissue engineering strategies [ 181 , 182 ]. 2.2.3. Scale-dependent effects and tissue integration The scale-dependent effects in bioelectronic material interactions with tissues critically influence regenerative outcomes. The ratio of interfacial volume to total volume increases with decreasing particle size [ 153 ]. Smaller structures exhibit increased electrochemical reactivity due to greater surface-to-volume ratios, as shown in amyloid aggregation studies where smaller (~100 nm) protofibrils showed significantly higher hydrogen peroxide generation compared to larger (~100 μm) fibril structures [ 154 ]. However, it is important to differentiate clearly between nanoscale materials and electrode device dimensions. While nanoscale materials (e.g., nanoparticles, nanostructured coatings) exhibit enhanced electrochemical reactivity due to increased interfacial area, smaller electrode dimensions (reduced geometric area) typically increase electrical impedance, reducing efficiency for bioelectronic applications. This contradiction is well-documented: decreasing electrode size inherently increases impedance unless counteracted by nano-structuring, unless specific nano-structuring or coatings are applied to enhance the effective surface area and mitigate these electrical limitations [ 125 , 183 ]. For instance, smaller silicon electrodes exhibit higher impedance and greater thermal sensitivity relative to larger devices, directly impacting their electrical performance in bioelectronic applications [ 183 ]. Thus, nano-structuring electrodes, such as adding porous coatings, fractal surfaces, or conductive polymer films, is essential to sustain low impedance and effective charge injection at reduced electrode dimensions [ 125 , 142 , 184 ]. The integration of bioelectronic materials with biological tissues is also strongly influenced by nanoscale and microscale interactions at the material-tissue interface. Upon implantation, biomaterials quickly adsorb proteins from surrounding biological fluids, forming a “protein corona” whose composition and orientation depend strongly on nanoscale surface properties [ 178 , 180 , 181 ]. The protein corona mediates subsequent cellular interactions, influencing adhesion, signaling, and the immune response. Nano-topography can direct specific protein orientations and enhance cellular attachment, and reduce inflammation [ 139 ]. For instance, neurons and glial cells exhibit improved adhesion and neurite outgrowth on nano-structured titanium nitride electrodes compared to flat surfaces due to favorable protein adsorption patterns and improved cell-material interactions at the nanoscale [ 140 ]. Electrodes scaled to cellular dimensions significantly influence foreign body responses, improving chronic integration of implants. Smaller electrodes present less disruptive interfaces, reducing glial scarring and inflammation due to decreased mechanical mismatch and smaller foreign-body footprint [ 185 , 186 ]. Ultra-fine neural electrodes have demonstrated long-term stability and low inflammatory responses in vivo due to their cellular-scale dimensions and compliant mechanics [ 144 , 185 ]. Similarly, microneedle array electrodes, with individual needle dimensions on the micron scale, significantly reduce skin-electrode impedance by bypassing the resistive outer epidermal layers. This substantially enhances electrophysiological recording quality and mechanical stability compared to traditional flat electrodes [ 143 ]. Overall, bioelectronic interfaces require a coordinated multi-scale design approach, balancing nanoscale surface engineering to optimize electrochemical performance and cellular interactions at the interface, with microscale or smaller device dimensions to reduce tissue disruption and chronic inflammation. By strategically utilizing scale-dependent electrochemical and interfacial properties, bioelectronic devices can achieve enhanced electrical performance and more effective long-term tissue integration, critical for successful regenerative applications. 3. Neural tissue regeneration 3.1. Overview of neural regenerative capacities 3.1.1. Peripheral nervous system (PNS) regeneration The peripheral nervous system possesses intrinsic bioelectric properties that enable remarkable regenerative capacity following injury. This regeneration relies on a coordinated bioelectric cascade known as Wallerian degeneration, a process in which ionic shifts and electrical potentials trigger cellular and molecular changes where innate-immune cells and their functions convert damaged tissue into an environment conducive to regeneration [ 187 , 188 ]. A critical bioelectric mechanism during this process involves the rapid depolarization of injured axons, generating injury potentials that activate regeneration-associated genes and stimulate Schwann cells to transform from myelinating phenotypes into proliferative repair cells [ 187 ]. Conventional clinical treatments for peripheral nerve injuries, primarily microsurgical reconnection of nerve ends or autologous nerve grafting, suffer from significant drawbacks rooted in their failure to address the altered bioelectric microenvironment after injury Achieving functional repair remains problematic despite advances in surgical technique, with questions persisting about the variable capacity of nerves to regenerate based on environmental influence [ 189 , 190 ]. Autologous nerve grafts, while considered the gold standard, present substantial limitations including donor site morbidity, restricted graft availability, and formation of suboptimal bioelectric interfaces at the graft-host junction [ 191 ]. Similarly, synthetic nerve conduits often fail to replicate the native bioelectric properties of the PNS, resulting in diminished axonal growth and impaired functional recovery [ 192 ]. Emerging bioelectronic strategies that directly modulate the electrical environment of injured nerves represent a paradigm shift in peripheral nerve regeneration. These approaches utilize electrospun piezoelectric nanofibers to deliver mechanical actuation-activated electrical stimulation to nerve cells/tissues in a non-invasive manner [ 193 – 195 ]. Brief electrical stimulation applied immediately after nerve injury accelerates axon outgrowth by upregulating regeneration-associated transcription factors and enhancing the transport of cytoskeletal proteins to growing axon tips, thereby addressing the fundamental bioelectric disruption that limits conventional approaches [ 193 , 194 ]. 3.1.2. Central nervous system (CNS) regeneration In stark contrast to the PNS, the central nervous system exhibits severely limited regenerative capacity, primarily due to the profound disruption of its intrinsic bioelectric environment following traumatic injury to the mammalian CNS leads to minimal functional recovery because wound repair generally inhibits regeneration through the formation of a cystic cavity and glial scar [ 196 , 197 ]. CNS injuries lead to immediate alterations in bioelectric gradients and ion channel expression patterns that trigger secondary damage cascades, including widespread neuronal depolarization, calcium influx, and excitotoxicity [ 198 ]. The most significant barrier to CNS regeneration is the formation of inhibitory glial scars, which establish aberrant bioelectric fields that actively prevent axonal extension While this scar was traditionally believed to be primarily responsible for axonal regeneration failure, current knowledge takes a more holistic approach considering both the scar and intrinsic growth capacity of axons [ 196 , 199 ]. Unlike the pro-regenerative Schwann cells of the PNS, CNS astrocytes respond to injury by forming a dense, electrochemically inhibitory barrier rich in chondroitin sulfate proteoglycans that disrupt growth cone membrane potentials and ion channel activity [ 198 ]. Current CNS treatment approaches focus primarily on minimizing secondary damage through anti-inflammatory interventions rather than restoring the proper bioelectric environment [ 200 ]. Novel bioelectronic therapies, including epidural stimulation and deep brain stimulation, are showing promise in bypassing these inhibitory barriers by directly modulating neuronal circuits. These innovations in bioelectric technologies provide increasing selectivity in stimulating distinct nerve fiber populations [ 201 , 202 ], offering the potential to restore functional connections even without extensive axonal regrowth. 3.2. Material-based approaches in neural regeneration Neural tissues exhibit distinct regenerative capacities: the PNS has considerable regenerative potential driven by bioelectric mechanisms like Wallerian degeneration and Schwann cell activation; in contrast, the CNS demonstrates limited regenerative ability due to inhibitory glial scar formation and disrupted bioelectric environments following injury. Consequently, biomaterials for neural regeneration must closely replicate neural conductivity, mechanical flexibility, and provide bioelectric stimulation to overcome these specific challenges. 3.2.1. Conductive hydrogels Conductive hydrogels represent a significant advancement in neural bioelectronics by integrating biomimetic mechanical properties with electrical conductivity essential for neural function. Their mixed ionic-electronic conduction facilitates bidirectional bioelectric signaling between neural tissue and electronic interfaces, while their softness minimizes inflammatory responses typical of rigid implants [ 203 , 204 ]. These materials create customizable conductivity and tissue-mimicking mechanical properties for more efficient neural interfaces [ 203 , 205 ]. In peripheral nerve repair applications, conductive hydrogels excel by providing both mechanical support and bioelectric stimulation essential for Schwann cell activity. For instance, innovative water-responsive supercontractile polymer films have been designed to conformally wrap around neural tissues upon wetting, providing intimate contact for effective stimulation and recording [ 206 ]. These films contract rapidly when exposed to water, transforming into soft, stretchable hydrogel thin films that facilitate seamless integration with neural tissues ( Fig. 2a ). Similarly, an injectable tissue prosthesis composed of a biocompatible hydrogel with phenylborate-mediated multiple crosslinking has been developed [ 207 ]. This prosthesis facilitates instantaneous bidirectional electrical conduction in the neuromuscular system, demonstrating accelerated tissue repair in severe muscle injury models ( Fig. 2b ). For CNS applications, conductive hydrogels address different bioelectric challenges, primarily overcoming the inhibitory post-injury environment dominated by glial scarring. Black phosphorus hydrogel loaded with neuregulin 1 promotes axon remyelination [ 208 ], while injectable hydrogels incorporating black phosphorus nanosheets facilitate angiogenesis and neurogenesis at spinal cord injury sites [ 209 , 210 ]. Advanced compositions, including plasticine-like conductive hydrogels with self-healing properties [ 211 ], biomimetic silk fibroin hydrogels with graphene oxide and fibroblast exosomes [ 212 ], and zwitterionic conductive hydrogels [ 213 ], offer novel bioelectronic approaches for neural regeneration. These hydrogels activate critical signaling pathways including PI3K/Akt, JAK/STAT3, and VEGF/NOTCH [ 212 , 214 ], creating permissive bioelectric microenvironments for regeneration where conventional approaches typically fail [ 215 , 216 ]. 3.2.2. Carbon nanotube and graphene scaffolds Carbon-based scaffolds provide unique advantages for neural regeneration through their exceptional electrical properties and morphological similarity to neural structures. Carbon nanotubes (CNTs) possess dimensions similar to dendrites, enabling them to intimately interface with neurons [ 217 , 218 ]. Their extended conjugated sp 2 carbon network creates a π-electron system that yields either highly conducting or semiconducting properties compatible with neural activity [ 217 ]. In CNS applications, carbon scaffolds address the limitations of natural regeneration. Pampaloni et al. demonstrated that optically transparent CNT-based substrates (tCNTs) effectively support the growth of intact or lesioned entorhinal-hippocampal complex organotypic cultures, inducing functional fiber sprouting across lesioned areas [ 219 ]. For instance, a study utilizing a three-dimensional graphene foam scaffold achieved reconnection of completely severed rat spinal cords, promoting neural tissue regeneration and functional recovery [ 220 ] ( Fig. 2c ). These materials interact directly with neurons, with CNTs “pinching” neuronal membranes to create electrical shortcuts that boost neuronal electrical performance [ 218 ]. For PNS applications, where natural regenerative capacity exists, carbon scaffolds enhance and accelerate recovery. Ahn et al. developed CNT-interfaced phosphate glass microfiber (PGF) scaffolds that effectively guided neuron regeneration when wrapped around poly-( l / d -lactic acid) electrospun nanofibers, demonstrating effectiveness in restoring motor functions in rat sciatic nerve models [ 221 ]. This complements work by Liu et al., where CNT yarn bridges spanning 15-mm sciatic nerve defects restored muscle function within 8 weeks [ 222 ]. Mottaghitalab et al. further advanced this approach with silk fibroin/single-walled carbon nanotube nerve guide conduits incorporating fibronectin, which resulted in more myelinated axons and higher nerve conduction velocities in sciatic nerve defects [ 223 ]. 3.2.3. Piezoelectric and triboelectric scaffolds Piezoelectric and triboelectric scaffolds represent a paradigm shift in bioelectronic neural regeneration by generating electrical stimuli without external power sources. Piezoelectric materials generate electrical energy in response to mechanical stress, enabling noninvasive stimulation through vibration plates, sound, or ultrasound without inflammation-inducing electrodes [ 224 ]. In PNS applications, where intrinsic regenerative capacity exists, these materials enhance natural healing processes. A three-dimensional piezoelectric poly(vinylidene fluoride)/polycaprolactone (PVDF/PCL) self-powered nerve guidance conduit demonstrated significant promotion of Schwann cell adhesion and proliferation in vitro, while animals exhibited substantial electrophysiological, morphological, and functional nerve restoration in vivo [ 225 ]. The bioelectric mechanism involves Ca 2+ influx through activated channels, triggering neurite generation via the cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP)-dependent pathway [ 226 , 227 ]. For CNS applications, where regenerative capacity is limited, piezoelectric materials provide precise stimulation to overcome inhibitory environments. PVDF films under 50 Hz mechanical vibration demonstrated 79% increased neurite growth with more than double branch points and 115% increased neuronal density compared to non-stimulated controls [ 228 ]. Similarly, PVDF membranes under ultrasound stimulation triggered neurite generation comparable to nerve growth factor (NGF) treatment [ 229 ]. These materials can be enhanced by incorporating piezoceramics—barium titanate (BaTiO 3 ) nanoparticles within poly(vinylidene fluoride-trifluoroethylene) (P(VDF-TrFE)) films enhanced neurite length under ultrasound due to improved piezoelectric transduction [ 230 ], while boron nitride nanotubes under ultrasound showed 30% longer neurites [ 231 ]. Triboelectric scaffolds generate electrical impulses through contact-separation of materials with opposite triboelectric polarities. This approach has been applied for peripheral nerve stimulation, where scaffolds convert natural body movements into electrical signals that enhance axonal growth, Schwann cell proliferation, and functional recovery [ 232 , 233 ], similar to the conceptual framework explored with piezoelectric P(VDF-TrFE) conduits for transected rat spinal cords [ 234 , 235 ]. 3.3. Device-based approaches for neural regeneration 3.3.1. Implantable electric stimulators for neural repair Implantable electrical stimulators have emerged as promising bioelectronic tools that leverage bioelectric signaling pathways to enhance neural regeneration by promoting axonal growth, synaptic plasticity, and functional recovery. These devices address the fundamental challenge of tissue regeneration through precise delivery of electrical cues that modulate cellular behavior. For PNS applications, where natural regenerative capacity exists but is limited, flexible nerve cuff electrodes have been developed to deliver targeted stimulation. Soft, stretchable multielectrode cuffs (sMECs) containing gold nanowire/polydimethylsiloxane composites interconnected with platinum-coated electrodes demonstrate superior conformability for wrapping nerves and achieve 90% selective stimulation of pig sciatic nerves [ 236 ]. Self-closing stretchable cuff electrodes triggered by water can also self-wrap around nerve bundles, enabling both stimulation and electromyographic (EMG) signal monitoring without mechanical locking structures [ 237 ]. For CNS regeneration, where natural regenerative capacity is severely limited, epidural spinal cord stimulators employ soft, compliant designs to safely deliver therapeutic stimulation. The electronic dura mater (“e-dura”) by Minev et al. integrates gold electrodes and fluidic channels in stretchable silicone, maintaining intimate spinal contact without inflammation [ 238 ]. Advanced closed-loop systems like those from Bonizzato et al. synchronize cortical activity with epidural stimulation, significantly improving neural plasticity post-injury [ 239 ]. Recent developments include liquid-metal-based 3D microelectrode arrays integrated with ultrathin retinal prostheses, utilizing flexible photosensitive transistors and eutectic gallium–indium electrodes for precise neural stimulation with minimal tissue damage [ 240 ] ( Fig. 2d ). The effectiveness of these stimulators depends critically on their electrochemical performance and mechanical properties. Electrical parameters such as stimulus waveform, current amplitude, and frequency must be carefully controlled to achieve optimal therapeutic effects while preventing tissue damage [ 241 ]. The Shannon equation provides initial safety guidelines for large diameter macroelectrodes, but microelectrodes require more comprehensive safety considerations that account for electrode geometry, materials, and pulse width [ 242 , 243 ]. Current steering technology creates virtual electrodes by varying the proportions of charge to each physical electrode, effectively increasing spatial resolution without adding more contacts [ 244 – 246 ]. This approach has been successfully implemented in cochlear implants and shows promise for fine visual prostheses. 3.3.2. Non-invasive neuromodulation devices for neural repair Unlike implantable stimulators, wearable and non-invasive neuromodulation devices promote neural repair without surgical intervention, offering patient-focused therapeutic options while minimizing procedural risks. These devices employ various energy modalities to target neural circuits through intact tissue, leveraging bioelectric principles to enhance regeneration in both PNS and CNS applications [ 247 , 248 ]. For PNS applications, transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation (TENS) delivers calibrated electrical currents through skin electrodes, accelerating peripheral nerve regeneration by activating cAMP signaling pathways and upregulating neurotrophic factors [ 247 , 249 ]. A recent example includes a wireless, soft, implant-free nerve stimulator powered by high-frequency magnetic fields, enabling precise activation of peripheral nerves without constraining natural limb movement or requiring direct tissue penetration ( Fig. 2e ) [ 250 ]. This approach enables precise neuromodulation without the mechanical constraints of implanted electrodes, allowing natural tissue movement during healing. Electrophoretic transdermal delivery systems further enhance regenerative outcomes by combining electrical stimulation with targeted pharmaceutical delivery [ 251 , 252 ]. In the CNS, where regenerative capacity is inherently limited by inhibitory microenvironments, non-invasive approaches focus on modulating neural circuits to create regeneration-friendly conditions. Ultra-thin photovoltaic interfaces applied to the cortical surface effectively activate specific motor regions, eliciting controlled neural responses without penetrating brain tissue [ 253 ]. These interfaces demonstrate superior biocompatibility compared to conventional electrodes while maintaining stimulation efficacy. Transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) and transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS) similarly promote neural plasticity by upregulating brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) and modulating inflammation [ 253 – 255 ]. Recent advances in flexible electronics have produced conformable interfaces that adapt to body movement. Skin-interfacing electronics with carbon nanotube/polydimethylsiloxane (CNT/PDMS) composites create ultra-thin, flexible electrodes that maintain stable contact during motion [ 256 ]. Similarly, “morphing electronics” accommodate tissue growth, enabling dynamic neuromodulation in developing systems [ 257 ]. Stretchable neuromorphic devices with proprioceptive feedback have demonstrated remarkable efficacy in restoring coordinated leg movements in neurological disorders, highlighting the therapeutic potential of minimally invasive approaches [ 258 ]. 4. Cardiac tissue regeneration 4.1. Overview of cardiac regenerative capacities Cardiac tissue regeneration focuses on repairing or replacing damaged heart tissue caused by a variety of heart diseases. These conditions, including coronary artery disease (CAD), myocardial infarction (MI), heart failure (HF), arrhythmia, valvular heart disease, cardiomyopathies, and congenital heart defects (CHDs), severely impact the heart’s structure and function. These diseases represent a significant global health challenge and are the leading cause of death worldwide, underscoring the critical need for effective regenerative therapies [ 2 , 259 ]. Unlike certain animals such as newts and zebrafish, which possess the ability to regenerate injured cardiac tissues [ 260 , 261 ], the regenerative ability of the human heart is notably limited due to its terminally differentiated state [ 2 ]. In humans, once cardiomyocytes are lost due to injury, such as myocardial infarction, they are primarily replaced by scar tissue rather than newly regenerated cardiomyocytes. This scarring can impair the heart’s ability to pump efficiently, leading to weakened heart function or heart failure [ 262 ]. Although evidence has been found for human cardiomyocyte regeneration, the renewal process occurs at a limited rate, which is insufficient to support full functional recovery [ 259 , 263 ]. Current therapeutic approaches, such as stem cell-based therapies [ 264 – 267 ], secretory factors [ 268 – 270 ], direct cardiac reprogramming [ 271 , 272 ], and stimulation of endogenous cardiac repair [ 273 , 274 ] have shown considerable promise in cardiac regeneration. Unfortunately, clinical trials in humans have not seen the same success as these preclinical studies, potentially due to inadequate delivery of therapeutic factors to cardiac tissues [ 2 ]. Tissue engineering strategies that leverage biomaterials are designed to create biocompatible platforms. These platforms are crucial for preserving and localizing various therapeutic factors, addressing the need for controlled release systems, and real-time monitoring in cardiac therapies. This approach ensures targeted and efficient delivery of treatments, enhancing the efficacy of interventions for heart-related conditions. Bioelectronics integrates bioelectric and physicochemical strategies to tackle the challenges of regenerating cardiac tissues. It can be combined with innovative materials such as semiconductors [ 55 , 56 ], conductive hydrogels [ 275 , 276 ], and nanomaterials [ 277 – 279 ] to enhance cardiac therapies through advanced monitoring, sensing, and precise control. This integration facilitates the gathering of critical data that can inform and guide cardiac therapeutic strategies. By leveraging both bioelectric and physicochemical processes, these devices offer a novel method to support and enhance heart function, positioning bioelectronics at the forefront of innovations in cardiac therapy. 4.2. Material-based approaches for cardiac regeneration Scaffold-based approaches for cardiac regeneration leverage advanced materials to support the bioelectric environment of cardiac tissues. In the field of tissue engineering, cardiac patches are typically designed to emulate the physicochemical properties of the natural extracellular matrix (ECM) [ 280 ]. They enhance the efficacy of both cell-based and non-cell-based therapies by serving as carriers for various therapeutic factors [ 279 ], demonstrating the potential to address the heart’s limited regenerative capacity. The convergence of cutting-edge materials and bioelectronics paves the way for more refined and effective treatments in cardiac care. 4.2.1. Flexible electronic hydrogels A fundamental aspect of cardiac patch functionality is to replicate the heart’s electrophysiological properties. Without proper electrical integration, the patch might not effectively propagate the electrical signals necessary for coordinated contractions, potentially leading to inefficient cardiac function. Interfacing electronics with cardiac tissue presents challenges related to biocompatibility, adhesion stability, conformity, and invasiveness [ 14 ]. Flexible electronic hydrogels serve as excellent scaffold materials for cardiac regeneration because they possess unique electrochemical properties, high biocompatibility, and mechanical conformity to dynamic cardiac tissues. These hydrogels, particularly those integrated with functionalized conductive polymers (CPs), including polyaniline (PANi), polypyrrole (PPy), polythiophene (PT) and poly(3,4-ethylenedioxythiophene) (PEDOT), are utilized for their electrical conductivity and biocompatibility [ 281 ]. The mechanical flexibility of CPs allows them to withstand the dynamic movements of the heart, maintaining structural integrity during cardiac cycles. These hybrid materials offer the capability to precisely monitor cardiac mechanophysiological micro-deformations and simultaneously deliver electrical signals, which facilitates electrocoupling therapy. For example, Song et al. developed POG1, a conductive hydrogel incorporating polyacrylic acid (PAA) into an oxidized alginate/gelatin matrix [ 282 ]. POG1 matches cardiac tissue mechanics, exhibiting extensive stretchability and compressibility, and maintains structural integrity through self-healing capabilities. Its uniform conductive network enhances cardiomyocyte alignment and maturation, reduces ventricular remodeling post-myocardial infarction, and demonstrates potential as an effective therapeutic tool. Recently, Yu et al. designed a chronological adhesive hydrogel patch (CAHP) based on functionalized PANi material [ 275 ] ( Fig. 3a ). The patch possesses self-healing capabilities and enables conformal and selective adherence to the epicardial surface without invasive sutures or bioadhesives. This approach minimizes stress on the myocardial tissue, supporting its natural environment and aiding in the healing process. 4.2.2. Filamentary serpentine structures The utilization of filamentary serpentine structures in scaffold materials for cardiac regeneration endows the scaffold with exceptional stretchability and flexibility, allowing it to conform seamlessly to the heart’s intricate geometries and dynamic movements. This intimate contact is crucial for maintaining effective electrical and mechanical integration with the myocardial tissue [ 283 ]. The open, mesh-like configuration of the serpentine structures allows for the incorporation of conductive materials, enhancing the scaffold’s ability to support the propagation of electrical signals across the cardiac tissue. This feature is vital for the coordinated contraction of cardiomyocytes and overall heart function. In the investigation of structures exhibiting these characteristics, Kim et al. designed flexible electronic webs for cardiac mapping and therapy, which are fabricated through sophisticated microfabrication processes that allow for the precise patterning of nanomaterials into the required serpentine and mesh layouts ( Fig. 3b ) [ 284 ]. The use of polyimide and silk ensures that the devices are biocompatible and can conform to the soft, irregular surface of the heart even during vigorous motion. 4.2.3. Bioresorbable materials Bioresorbable materials can significantly reduce the risk of surgical removal. The degradation process aligns with the healing process, allowing the patch to support the heart during critical recovery periods and then harmlessly dissolve, reducing long-term complications associated with permanent implants. Recently, Rogers and coworkers reported a fully bioresorbable, battery-free, and leadless cardiac pacemaker [ 32 ]. The constituent materials, including poly(lactide-co-glycolide) (PLGA), magnesium, silicon nanomembranes, and tungsten, undergo complete bioresorption through metabolic and hydrolytic processes. Besides, Aydemir et al. developed an injectable system that could form an in situ cardiac-electrode material interface [ 276 ]. When delivered into the pericardial cavity via a small-diameter capillary, the precursor material rapidly self-assembles into a mixed ionic–electronic conducting hydrogel encapsulating the heart. This behavior facilitates rapid aggregation and robust adhesion to the cardiac surface, ensuring efficient integration of the hydrogel with the heart tissue. After fulfilling its functional purpose, the scaffold is gradually degraded and safely cleared from the body. 4.2.4. Porous carbon membranes Hierarchical porous carbon membranes are effective scaffold materials for cardiac regeneration due to their unique combination of multi-scale porosity, mechanical compliance, biocompatibility, and high electrochemical performance. Specifically, their mesoporous and macroporous structures facilitate efficient capacitive charge injection without faradaic reactions, thus reducing risks of toxicity or tissue damage. Fang et al. developed a protocol for fabricating porous and monolithic carbon membranes [ 285 ]. This process is facilitated by micelle-enabled self-assembly, which yields a highly ordered carbon structure with hierarchical porosity ( Fig. 3c ). This porous structure ensures intimate contact with cardiac tissues, promoting stable and efficient bioelectric interactions. 4.3. Device-based approaches for cardiac regeneration 4.3.1. ES-induced cardiomyocyte maturation for cardiac regeneration ES provides essential cues that regulate cardiomyocyte functionality, cellular alignment, and maturation processes during cardiac development and within engineered cardiac tissues. In the native heart, rhythmic electrical impulses synchronize excitation–contraction coupling, driving the coordinated beating of cardiomyocytes. In vitro studies have shown that mimicking these electrical cues can direct engineered heart tissues toward a more mature, functional state. For example, applying pulsatile electrical fields to neonatal cardiomyocytes in 3D scaffolds induced synchronous contractions and dramatically improved ultrastructural organization (e.g., sarcomere assembly, gap junction formation) within 8 days [ 286 ]. Another notable example was provided by Ronaldson-Bouchard et al., where human-induced pluripotent stem cell-derived cardiomyocytes (hiPS-CMs) embedded in fibrin hydrogels were subjected to progressively increased intensity ES (“intensity training”) [ 287 ]. This approach led to the rapid development of adult-like gene expression profiles, sarcomere structure, mitochondrial density, oxidative metabolism, and robust calcium handling. Importantly, these tissues displayed a positive force-frequency relationship—a hallmark of mature myocardium—after only four weeks of cultivation ( Fig. 3d ). This work underscored the necessity of applying ES at carefully controlled intensities and timing to accelerate cardiac tissue maturation towards clinically relevant phenotypes. Further studies by Vunjak-Novakovic and colleagues detailed optimized parameters for ES, defining ideal conditions (frequency, amplitude, waveform) crucial for improving cardiomyocyte maturation and engineered tissue function [ 288 , 289 ]. In their optimization studies, continuous low-frequency stimulation significantly improved contractile properties and promoted organized tissue formation and electrical integration. Tandon et al. systematically examined these parameters and established specific guidelines for electrical stimulation regimes that significantly enhance cardiomyocyte maturation [ 288 ], thus laying critical groundwork for standardized protocols in cardiac tissue engineering. While in vitro studies have demonstrated that ES enhances cardiomyocyte maturation and function, translating these benefits into in vivo settings faces significant challenges. Developing biocompatible scaffolds that effectively deliver electrical cues within the heart’s dynamic environment is complex [ 290 ]. These scaffolds must support cellular attachment and growth while facilitating electrical conductivity to synchronize with the heart’s natural rhythms. Achieving this integration without inducing adverse immune responses or compromising mechanical properties is a significant hurdle. Besides, while human pluripotent stem cells (hPSCs) have enabled the derivation of cardiomyocytes, endothelial cells, smooth muscle cells, and fibroblasts, the efficiency, purity, and reliability of these cell populations remain a concern. Early differentiation protocols resulted in low yields, high variability, and a lack of control over the subtype identity of cardiomyocytes. [ 291 ]. For engineered cardiac tissues to be viable post-implantation, they must establish functional vasculature to ensure sufficient oxygen and nutrient delivery. The dense capillary networks in native myocardial tissue are difficult to replicate in engineered constructs. While endothelial cell inclusion and prevascularization strategies have shown promise, the challenge of integrating these vascular networks with host circulation in vivo persists [ 291 , 292 ]. Therefore, interdisciplinary efforts to design biomaterials and stimulation protocols are urgently needed. 4.3.2. Monitoring devices for enhanced heart therapy The emergence of flexible bioelectronics has significantly advanced the field of cardiac tissue regeneration by simultaneously facilitating ES-assisted cardiac repair and sensing functions. Nano-level technologies, including microelectrode arrays (MEA) and nanoscale field effect transistors (FETs) have been developed for localized sensing and recording of tissue with minimally invasive insertion into cells [ 277 , 293 ]. Tian et al. reported innovative nanoelectronic scaffolds (nanoES) that integrate silicon nanowire field-effect transistors within a flexible, three-dimensional, macroporous structure to monitor cardiac tissue responses [ 278 ]. This combination of advanced bioelectronics within synthetic tissues paves the way for creating more accurate and effective cardiac models for research and therapeutic applications. Additionally, Feiner et al. reported a hybrid cardiac patch integrated with multifunctional electronics for online sensing and therapeutic modulation of tissue activity in real-time [ 294 ] ( Fig. 3e ). Controlled release of drug-loaded on electroactive polymers can be precisely controlled by adjusting the intensity of ES. This strategy facilitates data collection at regenerative tissue interfaces and provides critical information on tissues that require repair, enabling controlled cardiac therapy. 5. Musculoskeletal regeneration 5.1. Overview of musculoskeletal regenerative capacities 5.1.1. Bone tissue regeneration Bone uniquely exhibits robust regenerative potential, with fractures often healing completely through intrinsic repair mechanisms without significant scar formation [ 295 , 296 ]. However, large bone defects frequently exceed this inherent regenerative capacity, necessitating clinical interventions. Conventional autologous bone grafting, while considered the gold standard, is severely limited by donor site morbidity, inadequate graft availability, and poor integration outcomes [ 295 ]. These limitations drive the need for alternative therapeutic strategies. Bioelectronic approaches leverage bone’s inherent responsiveness to electrical stimuli, which has shown significant efficacy in accelerating osteogenesis and improving fracture healing rates [ 297 ]. Recent innovations in self-powered electrical stimulation implants, integrating piezoelectric nanogenerators with conductive hydrogels, have demonstrated remarkable success in bone regeneration by converting physiological mechanical stimuli into targeted electrical signals, significantly enhancing healing in critical-sized defects without external power sources [ 298 – 301 ]. 5.1.2. Cartilage tissue regeneration Articular cartilage demonstrates limited intrinsic regenerative capability due to its avascular and aneural characteristics [ 302 ]. Conventional interventions such as microfracture or autologous chondrocyte implantation commonly result in fibrocartilage formation that lacks the mechanical integrity and durability of native hyaline cartilage [ 302 – 304 ]. Additionally, these approaches often involve complicated surgical procedures, extended rehabilitation periods, and inconsistent outcomes [ 301 , 305 ]. Bioelectronic strategies overcome these limitations by harnessing cartilage cells’ sensitivity to electrical signals, which has been shown to increase chondrocyte proliferation and enhance extracellular matrix synthesis [ 306 , 307 ]. Piezoelectric biomaterials generate electrical stimulation through physiological joint movements without external power sources, eliminating the need for implanted electronics. Recent in vivo studies employing exercise-driven piezoelectric scaffolds have demonstrated enhanced cartilage regeneration, producing repair tissue structurally and functionally closer to native cartilage [ 305 , 308 ]. 5.1.3. Muscle tissue regeneration Skeletal muscle possesses moderate regenerative capacity through satellite cells capable of forming new myofibers after minor injuries [ 309 ]. However, severe injuries causing volumetric muscle loss (VML) overwhelm this regenerative potential, resulting in fibrosis and permanent functional impairment [ 310 , 311 ]. Conventional treatments, including autologous muscle transfers, are significantly limited by scarring, incomplete reinnervation, and substantial donor-site morbidity [ 312 , 313 ]. Furthermore, traditional physical rehabilitation approaches have shown limited efficacy in addressing the neuromuscular deficits associated with VML injuries [ 313 , 314 ]. Bioelectronic modalities provide superior therapeutic avenues by directly modulating muscle cell behavior. Electrical stimulation effectively promotes satellite cell activation, myoblast differentiation, and functional maturation, augmenting muscle regeneration and functional recovery [ 315 , 316 ]. Electrically conductive biomaterial scaffolds incorporating conductive polymers or graphene-based materials provide built-in stimulation, enhancing muscle cell alignment, differentiation, and functional integration [ 317 ]. Combining electrical stimuli with biomaterial scaffolds has demonstrated significant improvements in muscle fiber regeneration and contractile force recovery, highlighting bioelectronic strategies’ considerable promise for treating severe muscular injuries [ 318 , 319 ]. 5.2. Bioelectronic material for musculoskeletal regeneration Musculoskeletal tissues exhibit varying regenerative capacities: bone can effectively heal minor fractures intrinsically but struggles with larger defects; cartilage regeneration is limited due to its avascular nature and typically results in inferior fibrocartilage; muscle tissue can regenerate small injuries but suffers fibrosis and functional impairment from severe volumetric loss. Therefore, biomaterials for musculoskeletal regeneration must specifically address these challenges by providing mechanical stability, bioelectric stimulation, controlled degradation rates, and minimal inflammation. 5.2.1. Mechanically tough adhesive hydrogels Conventional musculoskeletal treatments such as autologous grafts suffer from significant drawbacks, including donor site morbidity, insufficient integration, and inability to provide bioelectric cues essential for tissue regeneration. These limitations arise because traditional materials fail to simulate the native electrical microenvironment crucial for cell signaling, proliferation, and differentiation [ 320 , 321 ]. The human body naturally generates bioelectric fields across cell membranes that trigger cellular signals influencing tissue regeneration. This intrinsic bioelectricity is particularly important for electrically responsive cells, including osteoblasts, chondrocytes, and myoblasts [ 321 ]. Mechanically tough adhesive hydrogels overcome these limitations through unique physicochemical properties combining structural support with bioelectric functionality. Vinikoor et al. developed an injectable, biodegradable piezoelectric hydrogel that, when activated by ultrasound, generates electrical cues enhancing chondrogenesis [ 322 ]. In rabbit models with osteochondral defects, this hydrogel promoted cartilage regeneration, yielding structures similar to native cartilage. [ 322 ] ( Fig. 4a ). Similarly, Zhao et al. introduced a nanozyme-reinforced hydrogel that produces oxygen in response to hydrogen peroxide, alleviating hypoxia and improving prosthetic interface osseointegration in rheumatoid arthritis models [ 323 ] ( Fig. 4b ). The bioelectric advantage extends to conductive hydrogels like GelMA-silver nanowire composites that facilitate controlled electrical stimulation, enabling organized muscle regeneration and substantial functional recovery in defect models [ 324 ]. These materials provide both structural integrity and physiologically relevant electrical conductivity that traditional implants lack. Recent innovations include anisotropic conductive hydrogels that serve as biomimetic mechanical sensors [ 325 ] and piezoelectric-conductive systems that convert mechanical stimuli into bioelectrical signals, directing spatially controlled stem cell differentiation for enhanced osteochondral regeneration [ 326 ] —capabilities fundamentally impossible with conventional treatments that lack bioelectric properties. 5.2.2. Piezoelectric composites Conventional musculoskeletal treatments often fail due to their inability to replicate the intrinsic bioelectric properties essential for tissue function and regeneration traditional scaffolds disrupt natural signaling pathways due to their rigidity towards electrical signal conduction, despite their structural advantages [ 327 ]. Magnetoelectric composite membranes remotely activated by magnetic fields illustrate bioelectronic advantages, generating internal electric stimuli that significantly enhance bone regeneration even under inflammatory conditions, overcoming limitations of traditional scaffolds [ 328 ] ( Fig. 4c ). This fundamental limitation is particularly critical as native tissues like bone and cartilage depend on bioelectric signals for proper remodeling and repair. Piezoelectric composite scaffolds address this key limitation by converting physiological mechanical forces into electrical stimulation without requiring external power sources or invasive electrodes. These bioelectronic materials harness the natural piezoelectric phenomenon observed in biological tissues where endogenous electric fields play crucial roles in cell function and tissue regeneration [ 329 ]. In cartilage repair, biodegradable poly-L-lactic acid (PLLA) nanofiber scaffolds exemplify this advantage, generating electrical charges under mechanical loading that activate calcium-dependent signaling pathways and TGF-β release [ 330 ]. When implanted in osteochondral defects, these scaffolds produced superior hyaline cartilage formation compared to non-piezoelectric alternatives [ 330 ]. For bone regeneration, biomimetic piezoelectric scaffolds combining polycaprolactone with piezoelectric whitlockite nanoparticles generate intrinsic electric fields under mechanical loading, synergistically enhancing osteogenesis, vascularization, and neurogenesis in skull defects [ 331 ]. Similarly, aligned PVDF-TrFE fibrous scaffolds for tendon repair mimic natural electromechanical responsiveness, leveraging mechanical loading to produce biomimetic electrical cues that conventional treatments cannot generate [ 332 ]. 5.2.3. Electroactive scaffolds and conducting polymers Conventional biomaterial scaffolds, despite their structural support capabilities, present critical limitations for musculoskeletal regeneration due to their inability to replicate the native bioelectric microenvironment. Traditional non-conducting scaffolds fail to facilitate essential electrical communication between cells that governs cellular alignment, proliferation, migration, and differentiation [ 333 ]. Additionally, conventional approaches suffer from poor electrical signal conduction to deeper tissues, limiting their therapeutic efficacy. Clinical electrical stimulation remains ineffective for deeper tissues without appropriate conductive interfaces [ 334 ]. Conducting polymers, including polypyrrole (PPy), polyaniline (PANI), and poly(3,4-ethylenedioxythiophene) sulfonate (PEDOT:PSS), address these fundamental limitations through their intrinsic conductivity and biocompatibility. Barbosa et al. demonstrated this advantage with electrospun PEDOT/polyacrylonitrile composite nanofibers that mimic bone’s native electrical conductivity, significantly enhancing osteogenic differentiation by facilitating intercellular electrical signaling and upregulating key markers, including alkaline phosphatase and Runx2 [ 335 ]. Unlike conventional scaffolds, these electrically conductive materials enable direct modulation of cellular behavior through bioelectrical stimulation However, mechanical brittleness and poor processability initially restricted their application until composite approaches with biocompatible polymers were developed [ 336 ]. The advancement of electroactive scaffolds has led to systems capable of generating intrinsic electrical stimuli in response to physiological conditions. Li et al. created galvanic electroactive scaffolds using silver nanowire-coated mineralized collagen that generate electrical currents through mild electrochemical reactions, activating voltage-gated calcium channels and osteogenic pathways while simultaneously providing antimicrobial protection[ 337 ]. Additionally, Qin et al. introduced a mechanically adaptive metamaterial scaffold that achieves optimal mechanical properties for bone regeneration through a two-stage deformation mechanism [ 338 ]. This scaffold induces beneficial callus strain, stimulating calcium signaling and enhancing osteogenesis and angiogenesis, significantly surpassing traditional scaffolds in regenerating critical bone defects ( Fig. 4d ). These bioelectronic interfaces actively regulate cellular behaviors, surpassing conventional passive scaffold limitations [ 339 ]. 5.3. Device-based approaches for musculoskeletal regeneration 5.3.1. Monitoring devices for musculoskeletal regeneration Conventional imaging approaches for musculoskeletal healing (X-ray, MRI, CT) suffer from fundamental limitations that impede optimal recovery: they provide only periodic snapshots rather than continuous data, detect changes only after significant tissue remodeling has occurred, and fail to capture crucial bioelectric signals essential for cellular communication during healing This lack of real-time feedback results in rehabilitation protocols that remain largely subjective, with no integrated method to monitor tissue stability throughout the healing process [ 340 , 341 ]. Bioelectronic monitoring systems address these critical limitations by enabling continuous, dynamic tracking of physiological parameters through sensors that directly measure bioelectrical signals, mechanical strains, and temperatures. In tendon repair, stretchable suturable fiber sensors developed by Lee et al. exemplify this advantage, featuring flexible conductive fibers arranged in double-helix structures that can be sewn directly into tissues [ 342 ]. Unlike static imaging that cannot detect early failure, these sensors wirelessly transmit strain data through inductive coupling, identifying subtle changes indicative of graft loosening well before clinical symptoms arise [ 342 ]. For bone regeneration, “osseosurface electronics” developed by Cai et al. showcase the bioelectronic advantage through ultra-thin, wireless sensors placed directly on bone surfaces [ 343 ]. These battery-free devices record microtemperature changes with millikelvin precision, providing insights into healing status that conventional temperature-dependent systems cannot match [ 343 , 344 ]. Similarly, smart orthopedic implants using electrical impedance spectroscopy effectively differentiate between normal healing and non-union fractures in vivo [ 345 ]. enabling personalized intervention during critical healing phases when conventional monitoring would show no detectable changes [ 346 ]. Additional bioelectronic innovations include energy-harvesting triboelectric nanogenerators in joint implants that convert biomechanical energy into power for continuous monitoring [ 347 ], and stretchable EMG arrays that capture muscle activity with precision unavailable to conventional systems [ 348 ]. 5.3.2. Electric stimulators for musculoskeletal regeneration Conventional approaches to musculoskeletal healing rely primarily on passive scaffolds and external stimulation devices that suffer from critical limitations: poor electrical signal transduction to deeper tissues, inability to provide sustained stimulation without external power sources, and failure to mimic the dynamic bioelectric environment essential for proper tissue regeneration Traditional conductive materials used in biomedical implants are typically thermosets that are brittle, non-degradable, and exhibit declining conductivity in physiological conditions [ 349 , 350 ]. Bioelectronic regeneration devices address these fundamental drawbacks by utilizing engineered materials that actively generate and deliver electrical, magnetic, or mechanical cues to stimulate healing. The symbiotic bone defect electrical stimulation (BD-ES) system exemplifies this advantage through its flexible, battery-free triboelectric-piezoelectric nanogenerator design [ 349 ]. Unlike conventional external stimulators that cannot provide continuous therapy, this system harnesses physiological movements to generate localized electrical pulses, converting various physical stimuli into electrical signaling cues that regulate cellular behavior and promote tissue regeneration [ 351 ], resulting in complete bone healing in challenging femoral defects within six weeks. Li et al. further advanced this concept with 3D-printed shape-memory scaffolds containing piezoelectric nanofibers [ 352 ], which autonomously respond to body temperature by expanding and generating electrical signals without requiring patient movement. This addresses the limitation of conventional treatments that cannot provide stimulation during immobilization periods. Recently, Sun et al. introduced a biodegradable, elastic conductive hydrogel-based bioelectronic patch capable of delivering wireless electrical stimulation to deep tissues via inductive coupling [ 353 ]. This device provides prolonged, stable bioelectronic stimulation, significantly enhancing healing outcomes in musculoskeletal injuries without the constraints associated with external power supplies or invasive wires ( Fig. 4e ). Through leveraging the endogenous bioelectric signals critical for bone regeneration, these electroactive materials restore the electrical microenvironment essential for healing [ 354 ]. 6. Skin regeneration 6.1. Overview of skin regenerative capacities Skin regeneration is primarily focused on restoring the barrier function of skin tissue, which consists of three main layers: the epidermis, dermis, and hypodermis. For extensive wounds that penetrate deeply and involve significant loss of dermal tissue, autologous skin repair becomes challenging. This difficulty arises from the insufficient availability of keratinocytes required to reestablish the epithelial layer [ 355 ]. Therefore, strategies for skin regeneration are needed to treat skin damage, such as acute burns or trauma, chronic wounds, genetic disorders, severe infections, etc. The external accessibility of skin has greatly facilitated the development of regeneration technologies, allowing this field to mature faster than for less accessible organs. Because skin wounds and defects can be treated or studied directly on the body’s surface, numerous wound healing and skin replacement therapies have been developed and translated to practice [ 355 ]. skin tissue engineering is one of the most advanced areas of regenerative medicine, with dozens of commercially available skin substitutes and dressings already in clinical use [ 356 – 358 ]. The most basic approach for skin regeneration involves applying dressings to wounds. These dressings facilitate natural skin recovery by creating a moist, infection-reduced, and mechanically favorable environment for skin tissue healing. The range of dressing materials extends from traditional and convenient options like gauze, cotton wool, and bandages to more advanced materials such as hydrocolloids, hydrogels, and alginates [ 359 ]. For deeper and more extensive skin damage, skin grafting remains the conventional method for skin regeneration. This technique is classified into autografts, allografts, and xenografts, depending on the source of the graft tissue [ 355 ]. However, in cases requiring extensive skin grafting, such as for managing large area burns, the patient’s available healthy skin may be inadequate for autograft purposes; concurrently, there is a constrained availability of donor skin for allografts. In contrast, regenerating internal tissues (like myocardium or neural tissue) has progressed more slowly, hampered by the difficulty of delivering cells, biomaterials, or stimuli into those tissues non-invasively [ 360 ]. For example, brain and cardiac tissues are encased in protective structures (skull and ribcage), so accessing them typically necessitates surgical procedures, such as craniotomy or thoracotomy. For example, deep brain stimulation to treat neurological disorders requires an invasive neurosurgical implant [ 361 ], a risk many patients understandably seek to avoid. Similarly, even PNS often lie beneath skin and muscle layers, meaning interfacing with them usually involves incisions or implantation of electrodes. The gastrointestinal (GI) tract represents a unique case in terms of accessibility. While it is an internal system, it can be accessed through natural orifices using endoscopic tools like colonoscopies or upper GI endoscopies, which are minimally invasive compared to traditional surgery [ 362 ]. These procedures allow for the direct observation and treatment of internal tissues with significantly less trauma. In the context of tissue-interfacing bioelectronics, achieving precise stimulation or recording in internal organs often requires implanted devices that physically contact the target tissue – for example, pacemaker leads embedded in cardiac muscle or electrode arrays inserted into neural tissue [ 32 , 56 , 363 ]. Such implants provide high spatial precision and effective stimulation/recording, but they come at the cost of invasiveness and pose challenges for long-term use. Although the surface of the GI tract and skin share several similarities [ 364 ], the GI tract’s mucosal lining presents distinct challenges. Unlike the relatively stable surface of the skin, the mucosa of the GI tract is curvilinear and dynamic, characterized by the presence of mucus, rapid cellular turnover, and exposure to a range of biochemical conditions [ 28 , 364 – 366 ]. Consequently, mucosal-interfacing electronics (MIE) require robust designs that utilize materials and structures capable of maintaining effective and stable interactions with these challenging tissue environments. Overall, skin’s ease of access has lowered experimental barriers and clinical risks, speeding up innovation and clinical adoption of regenerative techniques relative to internal organs. Tissue-engineered skin substitutes such as acellular matrix, synthetic/natural biopolymers, and hydrogels combined with cells or growth factors provide alternative solutions for both epidermal and dermal recovery [ 8 , 359 , 367 , 368 ]. Several biofabrication strategies have also been developed for skin regeneration [ 359 ]. For example, electrospinning could facilitate the fabrication of nanoscale fibers onto Tegaderm as a scaffold, markedly enhancing cell adhesion, proliferation, and growth, which are crucial factors for effective skin wound healing [ 369 ]. Although advancements in dressing materials, tissue engineering, and biofabrication methods provide significant benefits for wound healing and regeneration, they lack the capability to respond to physiological signals that indicate the status of the recovery process. Bioelectronics, however, offer platforms that enable real-time monitoring of skin tissue regeneration by electrically responding to changes occurring within impaired skin. In recent years, skin-interfacing electronics have received significant attention for their capabilities in health monitoring, disease diagnosis, and personalized therapy [ 370 ]. Mechanisms like electrostimulation [ 29 ] and photostimulation [ 371 , 372 ] provide bioelectric foundations for the proper design of bioelectronic devices for intimate skin care. Materials such as nanoparticles, two-dimensional materials, liquid metals, and hybrid hydrogels [ 373 ] exhibit outstanding physicochemical properties that make them well-suited for integration with bioelectronic devices. 6.2. Material-Based Approaches for skin regeneration In 2011, Rogers and colleagues first reported the concept of epidermal electronics, which utilize materials and configurations that result in very low effective elastic moduli and bending stiffnesses, allowing them to flexibly conform to the fine topography of the skin [ 374 ]. A thin, gas-permeable elastomeric sheet, crafted from modified polyester, serves as the foundational substrate supporting the electronic components. The utilization of filamentary serpentine patterns plays a crucial role in evenly distributing strain and preserving the functionality of rigid inorganic materials, enhancing the mechanical flexibility and operational reliability. Seeking to form a minimally invasive, adhesive, biocompatible interface, different strategies based on materials have been developed to support bioelectronic devices on the skin. 6.2.1. Conductive polymers Intrinsically conductive polymers (CPs) such as polypyrrole (PPy), polyaniline (PANI), and poly(3,4-ethylenedioxythiophene):polystyrene sulfonate (PEDOT:PSS) are widely explored in skin regeneration. They can be blended into dressings or electrospun into scaffolds to confer electrical conductivity, enabling direct ES of wound sites and even electrically controlled drug release [ 375 ]. Because these polymers on their own may lack cell-compatibility, they are frequently combined with natural polymers (like gelatin or hyaluronic acid) or doped with bioactive molecules to improve their biocompatibility and mechanical properties. Overall, CPs-based materials act as active scaffolds and electrodes in wound healing, bridging the gap between electronic devices and biological tissue by delivering electrical signals to guide cell behavior [ 370 , 375 ]. 6.2.2. Liquid metals Liquid metal (LM), particularly gallium-based alloys, has emerged as a promising material for skin electronics due to its unique combination of high electrical conductivity, intrinsic stretchability, and biocompatibility [ 376 ]. Its fluidic nature allows seamless integration with flexible substrates while avoiding mechanical failures caused by strain-induced fractures, which are common in conventional metal-based conductors. Additionally, LM exhibits excellent wettability and adhesion to various substrates. Its low cytotoxicity and biocompatibility further support its long-term use in wearable healthcare devices, ensuring minimal irritation and adverse reactions when applied to human skin [ 377 ]. For example, integrating LM into the hydrogel network provides superior flexibility compared to traditional conductive ingredients based on metal nanoparticles. Xu et al. developed a gallium-indium (EGaIn) liquid alloy, polysaccharides, and conductive biopolymers integrated hydrogel, allowing for 3D-printing and injection, which could be potentially used for skin-bioelectronics interface formation [ 34 ]. Another study introduced a printed liquid metal (LM) electronic skin (e-skin) for wireless, controlled tumor therapy. Using oxidized gallium-indium (O-GaIn), the e-skin offers high conductivity, flexibility, and strong skin adhesion. It enables precise, non-invasive hyperthermia treatment under an alternating magnetic field (AMF) to suppress tumor growth. 6.2.3. Piezoelectric materials Piezoelectric biomaterials generate electric charges in response to mechanical stress, an attractive feature for self-stimulating wound scaffolds. Both synthetic piezoelectric polymers and ceramic nanoparticles have been applied in skin tissue engineering. A notable example is PLLA nanofiber mats aligned to be piezoelectric: these scaffolds can harvest mechanical energy (from body motion or an external ultrasound source) and convert it into localized electrical stimulation of the wound site [ 378 ]. Other piezoelectric materials like PVDF (polyvinylidene difluoride) or ZnO nanoarrays have similarly been investigated for wound healing [ 358 , 378 ]; they can be integrated into flexible bandages or dressings that generate micro-currents upon patient movement, thereby continuously aiding the healing process without external power. Such piezoelectric systems highlight how mechanical forces can be harnessed to provide therapeutic electrical cues in skin regeneration. 6.2.4. Nano-enabled materials By manipulating the size, shape, and surface chemistry of materials, researchers can develop skin interfaces with capabilities that are difficult or impossible to attain without the incorporation of nanostructures. Metal nanoparticles or nanowires are the most common nanostructures applied for on-skin electronics. For example, Fan et al. fabricated a transparent, scaffold-reinforced conductive nanonetwork (SRCN), consisting of silver nanowires (Ag NWs) embedded into a polyamide nanofiber scaffold [ 379 ]. The scaffold has a porous structure that allows air to pass through, facilitating the evaporation of perspiration. This structural configuration ensures that the electrode maintains skin compatibility and comfort during extended wear. Carbon-based nanomaterials also demonstrate exceptional flexibility and superior conductivity, which make them ideally suited for the development of skin-interfacing electronics for ES and sensing applications. Jung et al. developed a carbon nanotube/polydimethylsiloxane (CNT/PDMS)-based composite material for skin electrodes [ 380 ]. This method supports the skin environment by ensuring the electrode materials are ultra-thin, flexible, and stretchable, resulting in a non-invasive, self-healing, and conformal interface with the skin. 6.2.5. Granular-releasing hydrogels While most efforts have been made to design materials to form monolithic biointerfaces, Shi et al. reported a strategy to form focal interfaces by synthesizing a granule-releasing polymer network [ 381 ] ( Fig. 5a ). When interfacing with skin wounds, this material is engineered to transition from monolithic forms to dispersed granular interfaces. This transformation enhances adhesion and localization to targeted areas for skin therapy, optimizing therapeutic outcomes. Moreover, the material can be seamlessly integrated with bioelectronic devices, enabling precise electrophysiological recording. 6.3. Device-based approaches for skin regeneration 6.3.1. Wearable electric stimulators for skin wound healing Endogenous electric fields (EFs) play a crucial role in wound healing by directing cell migration through a process known as electrotaxis [ 29 , 382 ]. These electric fields can influence several cellular behaviors essential for repair, including cell migration, proliferation, and orientation. However, in chronic wounds, these natural electric fields are often disrupted or insufficient, impeding the natural healing process. ES is powerful for enhancing wound healing by providing external electrical signals that mimic these natural electric fields, usually divided into direct current (DC) and pulsed current (PC) [ 29 ]. It influences the migration patterns of cells, including lymphocytes, fibroblasts, macrophages, and keratinocytes, critical for wound healing. The antimicrobial effect of ES has also been demonstrated, which gives this method the potential for reducing infection during skin regeneration. This platform represents a breakthrough in merging biological entities with hydrogel-based electronics, creating seamless biointerfaces, and providing a solution for addressing longstanding challenges in integrating synthetic materials with biological systems for effective signal transduction and tissue modulation. As an example, Ershad et al. reported the synthesis of an electronic ink that could be used to directly write on the skin [ 383 ] ( Fig. 5b ). Fabricated from mixing silver flakes and conductive polymers, this ink demonstrates strong adhesion and ultra-conformality to the skin, allowing for ES and precise sensing to assist wound healing. Jiang et al. developed a wireless “smart” bandage for wound ES and sensing [ 384 ] ( Fig. 5c ). The dual-conducting hydrogel matrix used to support the device achieves lower impedance across the entire frequency domain by combining electronic conductivity, provided by PEDOT:PSS, with ionic conductivity from its hydrated polymer matrix, enabling efficient charge transfer during ES. Recently, Kang et al. reported a novel approach to addressing severe skin damage and tactile dysfunction through a wireless-powered tactile sensory system (WTSA) combined with artificial skin [ 385 ] ( Fig. 5d ). This system integrates collagen- and fibrin-based artificial skin for wound healing, a crack-based tactile sensor for pressure detection, and hydrogel-coated neural electrodes to minimize fibrosis and facilitate effective nerve ES. Besides the application of regulating tissue cells in the skin, ES can also be used for delivering electric signals to bacterial cells, which can modulate the skin regeneration process. Shi et al. developed a “living” bioelectronic platform for skin inflammation management [ 386 ] ( Fig. 5e ). In this platform, a specific bacterium, Staphylococcus epidermidis , is embedded within a hydrogel matrix that interfaces with an electronic component designed to monitor and assist in the treatment of skin diseases. The hydrogel matrix supports the viability of the bacteria, which can also be eliminated on-demand through ES provided by the bioelectronic system. 6.3.2. Photostimulation devices for skin regeneration Phototherapeutic methods such as photodynamic therapy (PDT) [ 371 ], photothermal therapy (PTT) [ 357 , 387 ], and photobiomodulation (PBM) [ 372 , 388 ] hold promise for treating skin diseases and wound healing. Specifically, energy from near-infrared (NIR) light sources could be converted into heat to physically destroy microorganisms that cause infection of wounds. Achieving photothermal ablation of bacteria without harming surrounding healthy and regenerating tissues presents significant challenges [ 389 ], as the excessive heat can cause unwanted damage. By precisely controlling the intensity of the light source, exposure time, and the concentration of photothermal agents, researchers can limit the extent of thermal diffusion and focus the heat generation strictly on the intended target area. Using nanoparticles as efficient light absorbers enables the precise localization of the heating effect during therapeutic procedures. These nanoparticles are designed to absorb NIR light and convert it into focused heat, thereby minimizing damage to adjacent healthy tissues during treatments such as wound healing. Furthermore, hydrogels also play a crucial role in supporting tissue recovery. For example, He et al. reported a multifunctional, conductive, and self-healing nanocomposite hydrogel for PTT of skin wounds [ 357 ]. Under NIR light, CNTs achieve localized hyperthermia that disrupts bacterial membranes and biofilms, effectively demonstrating antimicrobial activity. In the work of Ma et al., hydrogel is used to form a composite with neodymium-calcium-silicate (Nd-Ca-Si) glasses, enhancing the treatment by offering both photothermal properties and fluorescence thermometry. Its fluorescence thermometry capability allows for real-time, precise temperature monitoring during the treatment, and its bioactive components play a crucial role in minimizing normal tissue damage [ 390 ]. In recent years, wearable devices have gained significant attention for their potential applications in phototherapy, offering a convenient alternative to traditional hospital-based treatments. Compared to traditional laser-based methods, LEDs provide a cost-effective, durable, and compact solution for generating the necessary light for skin therapy [ 372 ]. For example, Lee et al. applied wearable LED patches embedded in wound dressing materials for PBM [ 391 ]. These patches use NIR as the light source, efficient for accelerating the wound-healing process. Blue LEDs have remarkable applications in biological systems, as their wavelengths align with those of key biomolecules such as hemoglobin and cytochromes [ 392 ], which are essential for biological signaling pathways that can potentially promote skin recovery. Notably, Li et al. designed a wearable LED patch combined with a sprayable fibrin gel (F-gel) [ 393 ] ( Fig. 5f ). This LED patch can generate both red and blue lights to combat infections and accelerate wound healing, while the F-gel provides synergistic antimicrobial and wound-healing effects through targeted drug delivery and mitochondrial enhancement. Efforts have also been directed toward integrating photostimulation and electrostimulation into a single patch to enhance wound healing [ 394 ]. This combined approach aims to leverage the synergistic effects of light and electrical stimuli, promoting more effective and efficient skin regeneration. 6.3.3. Microneedles for enhancing skin therapy A crucial strategy for achieving minimally invasive integration of skin electronics involves the use of microneedles (MNs). MNs can be fabricated from a diverse range of materials, including silicon, metals, ceramics, glass, and various polymers [ 395 ]. The tiny, needle-like structures penetrate the skin’s outer barrier to deliver drugs directly into the dermis, where they can be absorbed more efficiently into the bloodstream. This direct delivery method bypasses the skin’s stratum corneum, enhancing the bioavailability of medications, particularly for large molecular weight drugs that cannot be efficiently absorbed through the gastrointestinal tract [ 396 , 397 ]. Additionally, the minimally invasive nature of MNs reduces the risk of infection and skin irritation compared to traditional hypodermic needles, improving patient compliance. Their ability to be integrated into wearable devices further enhances functionality by allowing continuous drug delivery and monitoring, thus maintaining a consistent therapeutic level and providing a seamless interface with the body’s physiological systems. MNs can support various bioelectric approaches, such as electrostimulation and photostimulation, to promote wound healing. These strategies leverage the unique properties of MNs to enhance therapeutic outcomes, making them a versatile tool in skin regeneration technologies. For example, by combining a triboelectric nanogenerator and MNs patches, Yang et al. created a bioelectronic device that enables minimally invasive, sustained epidermal growth factor (EGF) delivery while generating an electrical stimulus to improve drug stability and efficacy [ 398 ]. Additionally, devices that integrate MNs with optical technologies like microlens arrays could optimize deep skin delivery of therapeutic light. These innovations improve both the depth and uniformity of light distribution, essential for effective treatments such as antimicrobial, blue-LED-based PBM, and PDT for skin [ 399 , 400 ]. By targeting therapeutic light more precisely, MNs devices improve skin treatment outcomes and offer convenient options for home use, marking a substantial advancement in minimally invasive skin treatment technologies. 7. Gastrointestinal tract regeneration 7.1. Overview of gastrointestinal tissue regenerative capacities Gastrointestinal (GI) tract regeneration primarily addresses the restoration of the mucosal barrier and overall structural integrity of the GI tract, which includes various regions from the esophagus to the colon [ 401 ]. Disorders such as inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), including Crohn’s disease and ulcerative colitis [ 402 ], gastrointestinal cancers [ 403 , 404 ], peptic ulcers [ 405 ], and celiac disease [ 406 ], compromise the tract’s function and structural composition, making the development of effective regenerative therapies essential. Such therapies aim to repair or replace damaged tissues, restore normal function, and alleviate the symptoms of gastrointestinal diseases, thereby improving patient outcomes and quality of life. Surgical intervention remains the dominant treatment for severe gastrointestinal (GI) tissue damage, encompassing conditions such as chronic inflammation and gastrointestinal cancers [ 407 ]. However, this approach often leads to complications, including bleeding, perforation, bowel obstruction, and stricture formation. Post-operative recovery may also present challenges such as skin irritation, leakage, and unpleasant odors, which can impair patient mobility and significantly heighten psychological stress. These factors collectively can lead to a substantial decline in the patient’s quality of life. In response, conventional regenerative medicine targeting the GI tract has developed strategies that utilize synthetic and natural scaffolds to replicate the extracellular matrix (ECM). This scaffolding supports a variety of tissue-specific regenerative techniques aimed at restoring smooth muscle, epithelial layers, and the enteric nervous system (ENS). Emerging research is now focusing on enhancing the efficacy of these biomaterials by integrating them with probiotics and protective coatings. These coatings are designed to shield the biomaterials from the GI tract’s harsh conditions, thereby promoting gut health [ 408 , 409 ]. Despite these advancements, significant challenges remain. These include preserving the contractile phenotype of smooth muscle, ensuring the integration of transplanted neurons with the existing ENS to maintain motility and digestive functions, and effectively regenerating various cell types simultaneously [ 7 ]. Each of these issues must be addressed to fully realize the potential of regenerative medical approaches in improving GI health and patient outcomes. Bioelectronics offers innovative alternatives for GI tissue regeneration through bioelectric and physicochemical pathways. For example, electrical vagus nerve stimulation (VNS) can activate cholinergic anti-inflammatory pathways (CAIP) to modulate intestinal inflammation [ 410 , 411 ], and gastric electrical stimulation (GES) therapy has been demonstrated as an effective treatment for gastroparesis (GP) and other gastrointestinal disorders [ 412 ]. Moreover, the fusion of bioelectronics with regenerative medicine provides numerous strategies to improve gastrointestinal health. These developments encompass the design of biocompatible, flexible materials tailored to adhere and stay affixed to the gastrointestinal mucosa, as well as innovative materials that foster a conducive environment for microbiota colonies [ 413 ]. Additionally, there is ongoing development of both implantable and ingestible devices tailored for various therapeutic applications. These devices are designed for precise drug delivery, real-time sensing, and targeted ES, thereby expanding the potential applications and effectiveness of treatment modalities [ 362 , 414 ]. Equipped with the capability to monitor a range of physiological signals, including pH levels, temperature, and concentrations of small molecules, bioelectronic devices provide insights that are crucial for diagnosing gastrointestinal diseases and effectively tracking the recovery processes, enhancing the regeneration of GI tissues. 7.2. Material-Based Approaches for gastrointestinal regeneration The microenvironment of the gastrointestinal mucosa is complex and dynamic, and the retention of bioelectronic devices is significantly challenged by the complexity of the GI tract structure [ 7 ], the flow of body fluid, rapid renewal of mucosal epithelia [ 415 ], and continuous peristalsis occurring in it [ 366 ]. Consequently, there is a critical need for innovative materials that can seamlessly interface with the mucosa to support implanted devices. 7.2.1. Mucoadhesive materials To address the above challenges, various strategies have been developed to enhance the adhesion between the gastrointestinal mucosa and electronic devices [ 362 ]. A prevalent approach involves the use of layered patches designed specifically for mucoadhesion [ 365 , 416 , 417 ]. For example, Wu et al. designed a hydrogel-based multifunctional origami patch for intestinal wound healing [ 416 ]. This patch features a unique multilayer composition, incorporating a microtextured bioadhesive layer, a dynamic, blood-repellent hydrophobic fluid layer, and an antifouling zwitterionic non-adhesive layer, exhibiting the ability to maintain strong adhesion in fluid-rich environments. Alternatively, Liu et al. utilized hydrogels embedded with magnetic materials to achieve sustained retention on the GI mucosa [ 418 ]. These hydrogels comprise a polyvinyl alcohol matrix interspersed with neodymium-iron-boron (NdFeB) microparticles, each coated with a silica nanolayer to enhance durability and biocompatibility within the gastrointestinal environment. The mucoadhesion of the material is achieved by the application of a wearable external magnet, strategically placed on the abdominal skin. This magnet serves to localize and retain the hydrogel at specific sites along the GI tract, effectively counteracting the natural peristaltic movements. To address the challenge posed by the substantial pH difference between the stomach and intestines, Zhang et al. presented the development of a supramolecular polymer gel [ 419 ], which addresses critical safety challenges in mucosa-interfacing materials by combining two essential properties: elasticity in acidic environments and controlled dissolution in neutral pH conditions. Lee et al. utilized materials composed of sucrose octasulfate and a cationic polyaluminium complex (PAC) to develop a coating for the gastrointestinal mucosa [ 420 ], which can be potentially applied to shield bioelectronic devices from the corrosive effects of gastric acid. 7.2.2. Microelectronic fibers Innovative materials are also crucial in enhancing the bioelectric functionality of bioelectronic devices used for gastrointestinal sensing and therapy. Among these materials, microelectronic fibers play a pivotal role in gastrointestinal electronics due to their multifunctional capabilities and superior adaptability to the dynamic environment of the GI tract [ 364 ]. These fibers integrate sensing, actuation, and drug delivery functionalities within a flexible and stretchable form factor, ensuring compatibility with the peristaltic movements of the gastrointestinal system. For example, Li et al. fabricated novel microelectronic fibers for a gut-electronics interface called NeuroString [ 421 ] ( Fig. 6a ). It is synthesized using laser-induced graphene networks decorated with transition metal nanoparticles embedded in a polystyrene-block-poly(ethylene-ran-butylene)-block-polystyrene (SEBS) elastomer, which is significantly softer than the materials used in similar applications. This combination provides mechanical softness, essential stretchability, and the unique physicochemical properties of graphene, allowing the NeuroString to adapt to the dynamic environments of the CNS and GI tract without disrupting normal physiological functions or inducing significant tissue response. Similarly, Sahasrabudhe et al. used a thermal drawing process to fabricate multifunctional SEBS fibers [ 422 ], which support the modulation of neural circuits in the GI tract, crucial for studying and potentially treating various gastrointestinal disorders. 7.2.3. Microbiome-modulating materials Gut microbiota holds significant importance in gastrointestinal tissue regeneration due to its critical influence on the gut’s immune response and barrier integrity [ 423 ]. Research efforts are focused on developing novel materials that can create a conducive environment for microbiota colonies using bioelectric approaches. In this context, Lin et al. designed an innovative soil-inspired material based on a composite of montmorillonite nanoclay, starch granules, and liquid-metal particles to modulate gut microbiota [ 424 ] ( Fig. 6b ). This unique combination of materials creates a porous, mechanically, and chemically active matrix, supporting the growth and activity of microbiota to improve the regeneration of gut tissues. 7.3. Device-based approaches for gastrointestinal regeneration 7.3.1. Implantable and ingestible devices for gastrointestinal ES therapy The GI tract is composed of hollow structures lined with mucosa. This mucosal lining shares notable similarities with skin tissue, which plays a critical role in the functionality of the GI tract, paralleling the protective and regenerative functions observed in the skin [ 364 , 425 ]. ES has been extensively researched as a technique for the regeneration of skin and other superficial tissues. However, the application of ES in the healing of gastrointestinal mucosa wounds is less explored, primarily due to the challenging environment within the GI tract and the difficulties associated with targeted delivery and adhesion of electronic devices. To overcome these challenges, several device-based strategies have been developed, including electronic bandages, ingestible electronics, and bioelectronics-integrated endoscopes. These technologies are designed to interface effectively with the mucosa in the GI tract, enabling the precise delivery of ES therapy and facilitating sensing capabilities. Implantable and ingestible electronic devices enable a transition from hospital-centric to patient-centric care, enhancing the convenience and timeliness of GI treatment. However, current devices for GES often encounter challenges, including battery failures, infection risks, and dimensional constraints. To avoid surgical removal of the implantable device, Wu et al. developed a self-powered, biodegradable electronic bandage (E-bandage) for intestinal wound healing [ 426 ] ( Fig. 6c ). This E-bandage employs a Mg/Mo interdigital microelectrode pair as the power source and can generate pulsed ES to promote epithelial cell transfection and direct current ES to boost healing factor secretion. It can completely degrade within a five-week period. Ingestible devices, which can be directly swallowed and delivered to targeted tissues, have traditionally been utilized for drug delivery and monitoring gastrointestinal signals. However, their potential as a non-invasive method for delivering electrical pulses to the GI tract remains largely untapped. Research in this area includes the work of Abramson et al., who developed a capsule that can anchor itself at targeted locations within the GI tract through electrically conductive, hooked probes [ 427 ]. The device remains attached to the tissue by delivering timed electrical pulses, which also function to stimulate the stomach muscle, potentially offering therapeutic benefits for gastric motility disorders. Additionally, Nan et al. designed an ingestible, battery-free electronic device encapsulated in a gelatin shell for ES of the gut [ 428 ] ( Fig. 6d ). The capsule is loaded with a small neodymium magnet, which allows for the position of the device to be controlled by an external, hand-held magnet. In a pig model, this device successfully demonstrates its efficacy in electrically stimulating the gut. 7.3.2. Monitoring devices for GI tract healthcare and regeneration The potential of bioelectronics to enable timely and targeted interventions, such as electrically actuated drug release, electrically powered phototherapy, and real-time sensing of gut signals, significantly enhances gastrointestinal healthcare and GI tract regeneration. For example, Madhvapathy et al. developed miniature, wireless temperature sensors, measuring 12.8×8.2×5.8 mm 3 , for long-term implantation in the gastrointestinal tract [ 429 ]. These devices provide continuous, real-time monitoring of intestinal temperatures that can indicate changes associated with Crohn’s disease and potentially predict flare-ups. Lee et al. developed a multifunctional endoscopic system that combines transparent bioelectronics with theranostic nanoparticles for enhanced colon cancer treatment [ 430 ]. This integration allows for accurate colon cancer detection and targeted therapy within a single device. The bioelectronic component facilitates real-time sensing and precise radiofrequency (RF) photo, while the light-activated nanoparticles deliver localized photodynamic and chemotherapeutic treatments. You et al. reported an ingestible, encapsulated electronic device for non-invasive monitoring of the stomach [ 431 ]. The sensing electrode ribbon inside the capsule unrolls inside the stomach, ensuring close contact with the gastric mucosa for high-quality signal recording. Notably, Mimee et al. presented an innovative ingestible device, using engineered probiotic sensor bacteria to enable real-time biomolecular monitoring in the GI tract [ 432 ] ( Fig. 6e ). The platform’s modularity was demonstrated through the successful integration of additional biosensors detecting thiosulfate and acyl-homoserine lactone (AHL). This versatility suggests broad potential applications of this ingestible device in gastrointestinal diagnostics and monitoring. Other sensing devices for monitoring biomarkers related to GI inflammation, such as pH [ 433 ], pressure [ 434 ], and gas molecules [ 435 ], have also been developed. Additionally, Wan et al. developed a novel therapeutic approach for managing gut microbiota-related disorders using an implantable mechanoelectronic system named iMASSAGE [ 436 ] ( Fig. 6f ). This system enables the on-demand production of therapeutic extracellular vesicles (EVs) by mechanically stimulating embedded macrophages within a hydrogel matrix, ensuring minimal invasiveness and risk of infection, and providing a promising platform for treating gastrointestinal diseases such as IBD by modulating gut microbiota. 8. Conclusion and future perspectives The rapidly advancing field of bioelectronics has profoundly influenced the domain of tissue regeneration, heralding the advent of innovative medical therapies that not only accelerate healing processes but also substantially improve the quality of life for patients afflicted with diverse tissue-related conditions. Bioelectronics merges with biological and electronic sciences to create devices capable of interacting seamlessly with biological tissues. These devices, ranging from sensors to stimulators, are meticulously designed to integrate with biological systems at the cellular or tissue level, thereby influencing bioelectric and physicochemical processes in a controlled manner, which can significantly enhance the repair and replacement of damaged tissues. Bioelectric signaling serves as a fundamental mechanism in bioelectronics for tissue regeneration, utilizing voltage gradients and ionic currents as essential cues for cellular organization and tissue repair. This signaling involves ion channels and pumps that establish transmembrane potentials, creating voltage gradients across tissues to transmit vital information governing regenerative parameters such as cell type and organ identity. These bioelectric circuits, akin to biological transistors, could influence current signaling based on past events and facilitate signal propagation between cells through electrochemical synapses known as gap junctions. Furthermore, advancements in manipulating bioelectric states have broadened the scope of regenerative medicine, enabling the regeneration of complex structures and offering new approaches to cancer treatment through the modulation of cellular behavior and bioelectric pathways. Understanding the physicochemical interactions between materials and biological systems is crucial for bioelectronics-based tissue regeneration techniques. The surface properties of bioelectronic materials, crucial for protein adsorption and cell adhesion, dictate their integration and functionality within biological systems. Additionally, morphological features like roughness and porosity also play a critical role in modulating cellular responses, stressing the manipulation of surface characteristics to align with the complex biochemical signals of living tissues, driving the next generation of bioelectronic devices. Tissue types, ranging from nerve and cardiac to skin, musculoskeletal, and gastrointestinal tissues, can all be modulated by bioelectric signaling. General strategies such as electrostimulation and photostimulation are broadly applicable across these tissue types. Electrostimulation involves applying electrical currents or fields directly to tissues to elicit specific cellular responses, such as promoting wound healing in skin tissues or restoring rhythm in cardiac tissues. Photostimulation, on the other hand, uses light to activate photosensitive materials or cells, which in turn can initiate cellular functions or modulate tissue responses. This method is particularly useful in nerve tissues where light-sensitive channels can be activated to control neural activity without the need for direct electrical contact. Moreover, the complexity and diversity of tissue structures necessitate the development of tissue-specific strategies. These strategies are tailored to address the unique structural, physicochemical, and mechanical properties of each tissue type, ensuring optimal integration and functionality of bioelectric treatments. These tailored approaches ensure optimal integration and functionality of bioelectric treatments. Device-based strategies for tissue regeneration leverage innovative bioengineering technologies to interface seamlessly with diverse biological tissues, offering broad applications across various therapeutic areas. Advances in soft electronic interfaces have introduced devices that closely conform to soft tissues, such as nerves, heart, skin, and mucosa in the human body, minimizing inflammatory responses and improving biocompatibility and functionality. Furthermore, osseointegrated electronics that anchor directly to bone tissues offer long-term stability and functionality, crucial for orthopedic and musculoskeletal applications. Bioresorbable electronics represent another significant advancement, designed to degrade post-therapy, thereby eliminating long-term implant risks and supporting temporary therapeutic needs during tissue healing. Such integrative and versatile device-based approaches are poised to revolutionize therapeutic strategies in tissue regeneration, offering more personalized and effective solutions to a wide array of clinical challenges. Additionally, advancements in biomaterials and nanoengineering have facilitated the development of interfaces that can more effectively interact with specific tissue types. These include supporting materials such as hydrogels and other polymer-based matrices that can conform to the surrounding biological environment, providing a scaffold for tissue growth while also being capable of delivering on-demand electrical stimuli. Besides, material-based adhesion strategies play a pivotal role in ensuring the sustainability and functionality of bioelectronic devices on tissue surfaces. These strategies include microneedles that penetrate the outer layers of skin or tissue to provide stable mechanical support and enhance electrical contact; mechanical anchoring systems that leverage macroscopic features to lock devices in place; magnetic interactions that utilize magnetic forces to maintain positioning without physical penetration; and both non-covalent and covalent bonding techniques that ensure molecular-level attachment tailored to the biochemical environment of the tissue. Moreover, the conductivity of the materials used in constructing these interfaces is critical for the efficient transmission of bioelectric signals, which are fundamental for the devices’ operational efficacy in applications such as disease diagnosis and real-time health monitoring. Conductive polymers, metallic nanoparticles, carbon-based conductors, liquid metals, etc., are often selected for their excellent electrical properties and compatibility with biological tissues, facilitating not only signal transmission but also ensuring biocompatibility and mechanical flexibility. The integration of such conductive materials with advanced fabrication technologies allows for the development of devices that can conform to the complex three-dimensional structures of biological tissues. This adaptability is crucial for maintaining stable interfaces that do not impede natural tissue function or trigger adverse biological responses. While bioelectronics provides promising outcomes in the field of tissue regeneration, there are also numerous challenges inherent in these methods that are discussed. The issue of biocompatibility and toxicity represents a critical challenge that must be navigated to harness the full therapeutic potential of these devices. For instance, CNTs exhibit exceptional conductivity and flexibility, making them promising candidates for bioelectronic components; however, concerns about their biocompatibility have raised significant safety issues [ 437 ]. In future clinical trials, it is imperative to meticulously assess the toxicity of materials used, particularly considering the diverse environments encountered by different tissue types. Meanwhile, the immune system’s response to foreign objects, including bioelectronic devices, can lead to chronic inflammation and fibrotic tissue formation, potentially encapsulating the device and isolating it from the targeted tissues [ 14 ]. Developing bioelectronic devices that can be manufactured at scale while retaining high precision and functionality presents significant challenges. These challenges stem not only from the intricate designs and sophisticated functionalities expected of such devices but also from the need for consistency and reliability in their performance [ 438 ]. The production process for bioelectronic devices must conform to rigorous sterilization protocols and quality control standards, which are mandatory for all medical devices. This adherence ensures that the devices are safe for patient use and function as intended. However, meeting these stringent standards often requires sophisticated manufacturing technologies and highly controlled production environments, which can substantially increase both the complexity and the cost of production. Moreover, the materials used in bioelectronics must be selected not only for their functional properties and compatibility with biological tissues but also for their ability to withstand the sterilization processes without degradation. Size restriction is another critical consideration in the fabrication of therapeutic electronic devices. These devices must be compact enough to integrate seamlessly with the human body without causing discomfort or impeding normal physiological functions. The challenge lies in designing devices that are small enough to be minimally invasive yet robust enough to house all necessary electronic components, such as sensors, power sources, and data processing units. For instance, ingestible electronics present potential risks such as gastrointestinal obstruction [ 439 ], necessitating a careful balance between the size of ingestible capsules and the versatility of their functions [ 362 ]. Power supply and energy harvesting are critical aspects of the functionality and integration of bioelectronic devices within biological systems. These devices typically require a consistent and reliable source of energy to operate effectively. However, the challenges associated with providing power to such devices are multifaceted, primarily revolving around the constraints of size, efficiency, and the potential biological impact. Currently, strategies utilizing biofuel cells [ 440 ], piezoelectric materials [ 35 , 441 , 442 ], triboelectric materials [ 36 , 443 ], solar-driven semiconductors [ 55 , 56 ], and magnetic resonant coupling (MRC) [ 32 , 428 ] have shown promise in addressing some of these challenges. However, further exploration is needed to develop energy sources that combine high energy density with high efficiency and sufficient power output. Signal interference and data integrity are paramount considerations in the development and deployment of implanted bioelectronic devices, especially given the complex and electrically noisy environment of the human body. Accurate signal transmission and processing are crucial to the functionality of these devices, which are often tasked with monitoring health metrics or delivering therapeutic interventions based on real-time data. Besides, the integration of bioelectronics in medical practices introduces complex ethical considerations, especially concerning the long-term effects these devices might have on the human body. There are also concerns about the potential for these technologies to enhance human capabilities beyond what is considered natural, which raises questions about equity, consent, and the societal implications of such enhancements. To effectively address the challenges associated with integrating bioelectronics with tissue regeneration, we outline several strategic directions for future research in this evolving field: Advances in Adaptive Materials The advancement of adaptive materials represents a critical area of innovation in the field of bioelectronics, focusing on the development of materials that are both responsive and biocompatible. These materials are engineered to align with the diverse mechanical and physicochemical environments found within human tissues. This alignment, tailored to match the unique physiological characteristics of different tissue types—such as elasticity, conductivity, and reactivity—enables more precise and effective therapeutic interventions. Currently, materials used in bioelectronics often exhibit limitations in that they typically excel in one property while lacking in others. For example, hydrogel matrices are usually non-conductive and must be integrated with conductive components to function within bioelectric applications. However, these conductive components frequently lack the desired stretchability and biocompatibility, posing significant challenges to their seamless integration into biological systems. Future innovations are expected to enhance these materials to achieve an optimal balance of conductivity, flexibility, stretchability, and biocompatibility. The next generation of materials could feature dynamic bioelectric and physicochemical properties that autonomously adapt to specific tissue-related cues. Such capabilities would ensure not only seamless integration into the biological environment but also optimal functional performance, thereby enhancing the overall effectiveness of bioelectronic devices. These advanced materials are envisioned to actively respond to changes in the biochemical environment or mechanical stress, adjusting their properties in real-time. This adaptability will maintain optimal functionality and therapeutic output, revolutionizing the approach to personalized regenerative therapies and setting a new standard for the integration of bioelectronics in medical applications. Deeper Understanding of Cross-system Interplay Future advancements in bioelectronics for tissue regeneration will necessitate an intricate understanding of biological interactions at the interface where bioelectronic components meet biological tissues. A key aspect of this understanding involves the protein corona, as discussed in previous sections [ 23 ]. This protein layer fundamentally influences how cells interact with the bioelectronic interfaces, affecting properties from biocompatibility to cellular response mechanisms. Deciphering how the protein corona modifies intracellular responses and interactions with cellular organelles will be crucial for enhancing device integration and functionality. Furthermore, the interaction between bioelectric and biomechanical signals is crucial in regulating cellular behaviors. Mechanotransduction, the process through which cells convert mechanical stimuli into biochemical signals, profoundly influences cellular morphology and gene expression. This mechanism has been successfully leveraged to modulate the gut microbiota, thereby treating intestinal disorders [ 436 ]. Enhancing the understanding of this interplay can inform the design of bioelectronic devices that more effectively manipulate cellular functions and facilitate tissue regeneration. Additionally, bioelectric-genetic interactions are pivotal in elucidating how electrical signals can influence genetic pathways essential for cellular repair and regeneration [ 21 , 104 ]. By modulating specific gene pathways, bioelectronic devices can directly affect cellular functions and enhance healing processes. Similarly, the bioelectric-biochemical dynamics, including the modulation of ion channels, play a significant role in tailoring regenerative responses to specific tissue types through targeted signaling pathways [ 444 ]. To fully unravel these complex interactions, integrating multi-omics approaches will be essential. By employing genomics, proteomics, and metabolomics, researchers can obtain a comprehensive view of the bioelectronic effects on cellular and tissue-level responses. These multi-omics strategies will allow for a detailed characterization of the changes induced by bioelectronic devices at the molecular level, providing a holistic understanding of their impact on biological systems. By deepening our grasp of these multifaceted interactions, future bioelectronic devices can be optimally designed to harness the natural biological processes for more effective tissue regeneration, paving the way for advanced therapeutic strategies that are both highly effective and finely attuned to the body’s bioelectric and biomechanical landscapes. Path to Clinical Integration and Autonomy In the field of regenerative medicine, bioelectronic devices are engineered to provide autonomous, real-time therapeutic interventions that seamlessly adapt to diverse tissue environments. This transformative vision requires the development of systems capable of delivering adaptable, real-time responses without manual adjustments, thus supporting a broad spectrum of medical applications. To achieve high levels of integration and autonomy, these devices will leverage cutting-edge technologies in sensors and actuators, combined with sophisticated algorithms capable of interpreting complex biological data in real-time. This will enable them to initiate and modulate therapeutic actions precisely when and where they are needed, enhancing the efficacy and efficiency of regenerative therapies. Moreover, the shift towards minimally invasive and patient-specific approaches highlights the potential of bioelectronic devices to revolutionize clinical practices. By designing devices that are tailored to individual physiological profiles and capable of operating autonomously within the body, healthcare can be significantly personalized, reducing the need for frequent clinical interventions and allowing for continuous recovery processes. Ultimately, the clinical adoption of autonomous bioelectronic devices in regenerative medicine could lead to a new era in medical treatment, where therapies are not only reactive but also proactive, adapting to changes in patient conditions in real time and facilitating a holistic approach to healing and recovery.
