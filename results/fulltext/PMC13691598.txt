Introduction Complex underwater environments remain among the most enigmatic and least explored regions on Earth. These regions hold critical importance for ecological monitoring, scientific discovery, and resource exploration, driving the need for technologies that can access and operate effectively within such harsh conditions. 1 Underwater robots have thus emerged as essential tools, offering the potential to conduct tasks without direct human intervention, thereby reducing risks and expanding our capabilities in deep-sea and confined aquatic settings. 2 , 3 However, significant challenges persist: navigating narrow and winding passages remains a significant challenge, demanding exceptionally high maneuverability and efficient locomotion capabilities from robotic systems. 4 , 5 Furthermore, in ecologically sensitive environments, traditional rotor-based propulsion systems are prone to entanglement in dense vegetation, 6 , 7 while the acoustic noise and hydrodynamic disturbances they generate may disrupt or harm marine organisms. 8 , 9 These limitations underscore the urgent demand for a new generation of underwater robotic platforms that combine high maneuverability in complex terrains with enhanced operational reliability and minimal ecological impact. Biomimetics offers a powerful framework for advancing robotic locomotion, drawing on nature’s evolutionarily refined mechanisms to enhance performance, a potential clearly demonstrated by significant progress in bio-inspired underwater robotics. For instance, Yu et al. 10 developed a highly agile robotic dolphin with exceptional posture control, showcasing sophisticated maneuverability in aquatic locomotion. Katzschmann et al. 11 introduced SoFi, a miniaturized robotic fish operated via acoustic signals, which made substantial strides in compact design, integrated systems, and reliable underwater communication. Li et al. 12 created a high-speed soft robotic fish inspired by manta rays, utilizing dielectric elastomer actuators (DEAs), and later demonstrated extreme environmental resilience with a soft robotic snailfish capable of swimming at hadal depths. 13 He et al. 14 contributed a large-scale robotic manta ray exhibiting long-range endurance and robust attitude stability, facilitating the practical application of sizable biomimetic systems. Expanding operational domains, Lin et al. 15 proposed an amphibious robot inspired by the mudskipper, capable of coordinated movement in both aquatic and terrestrial environments through integrated pectoral and caudal fin motions. Further pushing the boundaries of functionality, Pan et al. 16 collaboratively designed a multifunctional deep-sea biomimetic robot capable of swimming, gliding, crawling, and grasping under extreme oceanic conditions. Collectively, these pioneering studies underscore the transformative potential of bio-inspired design in advancing underwater robotics. While these remarkable contributions have established a solid foundation for the field of bionic robotic fish, research focusing on navigation within narrow and complex aquatic environments remains relatively limited, indicating a significant area for future development and refinement. The black ghost knifefish ( Apteronotus albifrons ), as shown in Figure 1 A, native to the Amazon and Guianas River basins, serves as an exceptional biological model for bio-inspired robotics due to its remarkable maneuverability in narrow, confined aquatic environments. This slender fish achieves agile movements—including forward/backward swimming, hovering, and vertical motion—primarily through undulations of a long, ribbon-like anal fin composed of numerous fin rays. 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 Research on knifefish-inspired locomotion and ribbon-fin propulsion has mainly centered on four aspects. First, biological observations and theoretical analyses have clarified the main locomotor principles of the black ghost knifefish. 20 , 21 , 22 , 23 , 24 For example, Liang et al. 22 quantified anal-fin morphology and fin-wave kinematics, showing how wave direction, unidirectional or bidirectional wave modes, frequency, wavelength, and wave speed are regulated during maneuvering. Second, CFD and numerical studies have examined the hydrodynamic mechanisms of biomimetic ribbon fins and the effects of wave parameters. 25 , 26 , 27 , 28 , 29 Shi et al. 28 analyzed an undulating-fin robot under different water conditions and showed how fin aspect ratio, frequency, amplitude, and wavenumber influence thrust generation and hydrodynamic performance. Third, robotic platforms inspired by the morphology and motion patterns of knifefish have been developed to evaluate locomotion performance in both tethered water-tank settings and free-swimming experiments. 30 , 31 , 32 , 33 , 34 In particular, Liu and Curet 32 demonstrated an untethered robotic vessel driven by a single undulating fin and characterized forward and reverse swimming, diving, station keeping, vertical swimming, cost of transport (COT), and flow structures. Fourth, control-oriented studies have also investigated locomotion control in knifefish-inspired ribbon-fin-driven robots, including closed-loop motion regulation, multimodal gait generation, and obstacle-avoidance strategies. 35 , 36 , 37 Uddin et al. 35 implemented three potential-field-based collision-avoidance strategies on a robotic vessel propelled by an undulating elongated fin and showed that fin-kinematic modulation could support directional control and obstacle avoidance, with a circular sensing envelope enabling smoother transition back to the mission path after obstacle avoidance. However, the locomotion of biological knifefish arises not only from ribbon-fin undulation, but also from coordinated interactions between fin-wave modulation and body deformation, which have been shown to improve maneuverability and locomotor performance across diverse gait patterns and complex aquatic environments. 21 , 23 Nevertheless, compared with the extensive studies on fin-only ribbon-fin propulsion, knifefish-inspired robotic platforms that integrate an undulatory anal fin with an actively bendable body remain largely unexplored. Replicating this integrated body-fin coordination therefore represents an important step toward robotic fish capable of agile locomotion in complex and confined underwater environments. Here, we present Naiad, a multi-joint robotic platform that abstracts key locomotor features of the black ghost knifefish. Naiad integrates a distributed servo-driven ribbon fin, inspired by the species’ long anal fin, with a flexible segmented body to produce body-fin coordinated undulatory propulsion. We evaluated its free-swimming performance by varying fin wavelength, undulation amplitude, undulation frequency, and body curvature. The parameter sweeps showed how these variables affected straight-line swimming speed, COT, turning rate, turning radius, and vertical ascent. Naiad achieved a maximum swimming speed of 0.20 BL/s, a minimum COT of 3.42, a maximum turning rate of 23.67°/s (0.41 rad/s), and a minimum turning radius of 0.31 BL. Demonstrations in a confined obstacle course and in an outdoor lake further evaluated commanded multi-gait maneuvering and onboard imaging. This study highlights the potential of combining undulatory anal-fin propulsion with body reconfiguration for underwater robots operating in constrained and ecologically sensitive aquatic environments. Results Bioinspired structure and gait design of Naiad The structure and gait design of Naiad were inspired by the morphology and locomotor behaviors of the black ghost knifefish ( Apteronotus albifrons ), but were not intended to reproduce its anatomy and kinematics exactly. Micro-CT and anatomical observations show that the biological anal fin extends along approximately three-quarters of the ventral body and comprises approximately 140–160 segmented fin rays connected by a thin, flexible membrane. 20 Guided by this ray-supported membrane organization and by established knifefish-inspired robotic designs, 32 , 35 , 38 , 39 Naiad comprises four functional modules: a sensing head, a hardware-containing flexible body, a multi-jointed tail, and an elongated ribbon-fin actuator ( Figure 1 A). The robotic anal fin is a reduced-order functional abstraction consisting of 11 independently actuated rigid metal rays connected by a continuous Ecoflex 00–30 silicone membrane. This configuration retains the elongated ray-supported surface and distributed basal actuation required for traveling-wave generation, but does not reproduce the biological fin-ray number, bilaminar segmented-ray architecture, active ray-curvature control, or tissue material properties. The gait design was informed by previous biological studies 20 , 21 , 24 , 25 and by our high-speed recordings of black ghost knifefish swimming freely in a transparent tank ( Video S1 ). Frame-by-frame analysis captured anal-fin motion during straight-line swimming, reverse swimming, and turning. Biological observations show that forward and backward swimming are generated primarily by unidirectional anal-fin waves propagating in opposite directions, 20 , 24 turning involves coordinated body bending and modulation of ribbon-fin kinematics, 21 and hovering or heave motion involves inward counter-propagating waves. 24 , 25 Naiad implements these mode-level principles through prescribed servo commands. Phase-shifted sinusoidal commands to adjacent rays generate unidirectional traveling waves for rectilinear swimming, with the propagation direction reversed for forward and backward locomotion. Turning combines the distributed fin actuation with a prescribed curved-body configuration, whereas vertical ascent is generated by superimposing inward-traveling waves from the anterior and posterior ends of the fin. Figure 1 B compares the observed biological anal-fin wave with the corresponding unidirectional traveling-wave gait implemented on Naiad. The correspondence is therefore functional rather than anatomically or dynamically identical. The hardware system is divided into an onboard robotic unit and an external control unit. The robotic unit consists of a 3D-printed flexible body, a multi-joint tail linked by elastic steel strips, and servos for body flexion, forming the main structural frame. The propulsion system (Driver Layer) incorporates an array of 11 waterproof servos, mechanically connected metal fin rays, and a ribbon-like anal fin cast from the soft silicone material Ecoflex-0030. The perception system (Sensor Layer) includes an underwater camera and DC sensors. The hardware configuration is detailed in Figure 1 C. The external control unit features a Decision Center built around a Raspberry Pi and PCA9685 controllers, and a Power Supply Unit (PSU) based on lithium batteries. The overall system framework and information flow are schematically represented in Figure 1 D. A photograph of the fully assembled and tested robotic fish prototype is shown in Figure 1 E. Linear locomotion experiments and analysis of propulsive speed and efficiency To evaluate the propulsive speed and efficiency of Naiad, we conducted linear locomotion experiments in a 6-meter-long water tank, as shown in Figure S3 A. The motion of the robotic fish was recorded in real-time using a camera, and its primary kinematic parameters were obtained through post-processing software. The experimental setup ensured that the robotic fish reached a steady state of motion before entering the measurement zone, thereby reducing transient effects and minimizing errors. Power consumption was continuously recorded using a DC power sensor placed between the servo motor power supply and the actuators. The energy efficiency of locomotion was quantified using the Cost of Transport (COT), defined as: (Equation 1) COT = P i n ¯ m g v ¯ where P in ¯ is the cycle-averaged input power of the robot, obtained by the DC power sensor (see Figures S1 and S2 for experimental setup and verification), m is the robot mass, and g = 9.81 m/s 2 , v ¯ is the cycle-averaged speed of the robot. COT, a dimensionless metric, reflects the energy required to transport a unit mass over a unit distance under gravity. The propulsive wave generated by the undulating fin can be effectively modeled as a sinusoidal waveform, achieved by driving all fin rays to oscillate at a common frequency while introducing controlled phase differences between them. 31 The angular displacement of the n-th fin ray at time t is given by: (Equation 2) θ ( n , t ) = A sin [ 2 π f t − φ ( n ) ] where θ represents the angular deflection from the vertical axis, A denotes the undulation amplitude, and f is the frequency in hertz; φ is the phase delay, which varies as a function of the fin ray number, and is defined as: (Equation 3) φ ( n ) = 2 π n − 1 N − 1 L λ where N is the total number of rays, λ is the wavelength, and L is the total length of the fin. As indicated by the equations, an increase in wavelength reduces the phase difference between adjacent rays. Consequently, a greater number of fin rays contribute to each wave cycle, resulting in a smoother, more sinusoidal fin motion with reduced sawtooth characteristics. To systematically investigate the influence of gait parameters on propulsion performance, we evaluated Naiad’s swimming performance under various combinations of undulation amplitude, wavelength, and frequency generated by the undulating anal fin. In the initial experimental series, the wavelength and frequency were fixed at 490 mm and 0.99 Hz, respectively, while the undulation amplitude was varied from 20° to 50°. The results reveal an approximately linear increase in swimming speed with increasing amplitude. At 20° amplitude, the robotic fish attained an average speed of 9.08 cm/s (0.11 BL/s), whereas at 50°, the speed increased to 16.96 cm/s (0.20 BL/s), corresponding to an 86.78% improvement in velocity. Concurrently, power consumption decreased from 33.65 W at 20° to 28.08 W at 50°, a reduction of 16.55%, as shown in Figure 2 B (top). Consistent with these trends, COT decreased with increasing undulation amplitude, as shown in Figure 2 B (bottom). A comparative visualization of robotic fish motion under different undulation amplitudes is provided in Video S2 . These findings underscore the substantial role of amplitude modulation in enhancing locomotor performance in the knifefish-inspired robot. Based on these outcomes, an undulation amplitude of 50° was selected for subsequent experiments examining the influence of other gait parameters. To systematically evaluate the combined effects of wavelength and frequency on propulsion performance, we conducted experiments using four distinct wavelengths (490 mm, 560 mm, 630 mm, and 700 mm) and five frequencies ranging from 0.42 Hz to 0.99 Hz, resulting in a total of 20 experimental conditions. Each condition was tested over three independent trials to ensure reproducibility. A comparison of swimming velocity under all kinematic conditions is presented in Figure 2 C. The results indicated that velocity increased in an approximately linear manner with frequency across all wavelengths. However, the sensitivity of velocity to frequency variations diminished as the wavelength increased. At a low frequency of 0.42 Hz, longer wavelengths corresponded to higher velocity, whereas at a high frequency of 0.99 Hz, longer wavelengths led to reduced velocity, as shown in Figure 2 D. To quantitatively assess this wavelength-frequency interaction, the raw trial-level data were further analyzed using a two-way ANOVA, as summarized in Figure S5 . The analysis showed a significant main effect of frequency on swimming velocity (F(4,40) = 448.79, p = 1.20 × 10 −32 , partial η 2 = 0.978), whereas the main effect of wavelength was not significant (F(3,40) = 0.38, p = 0.7714, partial η 2 = 0.027). Importantly, the wavelength × frequency interaction was significant (F(12,40) = 5.30, p = 2.93 × 10 −5 , partial η 2 = 0.614), indicating that frequency was the dominant factor governing swimming velocity, whereas wavelength did not show a significant overall main effect but modulated the velocity response in a frequency-dependent manner. The maximum velocity recorded was 17.21 cm/s (0.20 BL/s), achieved under the condition of 490 mm wavelength and 0.99 Hz frequency. To characterize the hydrodynamic regime of Naiad during linear swimming, we calculated the body-scale Reynolds number as R e = U L ν , where U is the measured swimming speed, L is the robot body length, and ν is the kinematic viscosity of water (1 × 10 −6 m 2 /s), and the Strouhal number as S t = f · A U , where f is the ribbon-fin undulation frequency and A is the fin-tip peak-to-peak excursion. Across the tested wavelength-frequency conditions, Re ranged from approximately 5.66×10 4 to 1.45×10 5 , indicating that Naiad operated in an intermediate-to-high Reynolds number regime in which inertial effects were more important than viscous effects. The Reynolds number generally increased with undulation frequency because higher-frequency conditions produced greater forward swimming speeds, as shown in Figure S6 A whereas the influence of wavelength on Re reflected its frequency-dependent effect on swimming speed, as shown in Figure S6 B. The corresponding St values ranged from approximately 0.603 to 0.837 and varied non-monotonically because they depended on both the imposed undulation frequency and the resulting swimming speed, as shown in Figures S6 C and S6D. At lower frequencies, increasing wavelength generally increased swimming speed and reduced St, whereas at the highest tested frequency, increasing wavelength reduced swimming speed and increased St, indicating that the effects of wavelength and frequency on the dimensionless fin-kinematic regime cannot be considered independently. Regarding locomotion efficiency, Figure 2 E summarizes the COT across all tested frequency-wavelength combinations. At shorter wavelengths, COT decreased markedly with increasing frequency, whereas longer wavelengths produced smaller and less consistent frequency-dependent variations ( Figure 2 F). To distinguish the contributions of swimming speed and input power, Figures S7 A–S7C present the corresponding frequency-dependent changes in swimming speed, cycle-averaged electrical input power, and COT. The results show that increasing frequency generally increased both swimming speed and input power. The 630-mm condition generally combined relatively high swimming speeds with comparatively low input power, producing relatively low COT values across the tested frequencies. The minimum measured COT of 3.42 occurred at 630 mm and 0.86 Hz. Because frequency and wavelength jointly determine the fin-wave speed, V wave = fλ , we further examined the association between mean COT and V wave ( Figure S7 F). An exploratory quadratic fit described the observed variation better than a monotonic relationship ( R 2 = 0.62), with a fitted minimum near V _ wave ≈ 0.5 m/s. This result indicates that, within the present parameter range, the lowest COT occurred at an intermediate fin-wave speed rather than at either extreme. However, the fitted minimum represents a condition-specific association for the present robot and does not establish a universal optimal wave speed. Through the above robotic experiments, we systematically investigated the effects of different undulation amplitudes, wavelengths, and frequencies on the motion velocity and efficiency of the knifefish-inspired robotic fish. This study enhanced our understanding and provided a preliminary explanation of the mechanisms through which different propulsive waves influence their locomotor performance. The optimal velocity and COT values obtained in our experiments are at a commendable level when compared to other works on biomimetic robotic fish, 31 , 40 , 41 , 42 , 43 , 44 , 45 , 46 , 47 as detailed in Figure S3 B. Turning experiments and analysis of maneuverability To evaluate the maneuverability of the biomimetic robotic fish Naiad, we designed a series of turning experiments to quantify its turning performance and characterize its response to prescribed body-curvature configurations. The trials were conducted in quiescent water, and an upward-facing camera fixed beneath the transparent tank floor recorded the robot’s horizontal-plane turning trajectories. The turning radius was determined by extracting the centroid trajectory of the robot and fitting a circle to the path using a least-squares regression method. To quantify body curvature, we introduced a body bending coefficient β , 21 defined as: (Equation 4) β = 1 − L c L b where L c is the linear distance between the robot’s head and tail in a deformed (curved) posture, and L b is the total body length, as shown in Figure 3 A This non-dimensional coefficient increases with whole-body curvature and was used to characterize the prescribed yaw-plane body configuration. The model assumes a prescribed quasi-static body configuration and uniform mean loading within each straight fin-body segment. Following the swept-area force-scaling approach used for ribbon-fin propulsion, 48 the peak-to-peak effective sweep height were defined as: (Equation 5) H eff = 2 H f sin A where H f and A are the fin height and angular amplitude, respectively. With H f = 0.08 m, and A = 40°, these relations give H eff = 0.10 m. The time-averaged equivalent thrust density per unit fin length was represented by (Equation 6) f T = 1 2 ρ C T H eff V w a v e 2 where ρ is the water density, and C T is an empirical longitudinal thrust coefficient based on the swept area. This expression defines a time-averaged load scale rather than an instantaneous blade-element force law. The experimental setup and representative turning sequence are shown in Figure 3 C. All turning experiments were conducted using the same ribbon-fin kinematic parameters: an oscillation amplitude of 40°, a frequency of 0.99 Hz, and a wavelength of 490 mm. For active-body-bending turning, five prescribed body configurations ( β = 0.14, 0.17, 0.24, 0.32, and 0.46) were tested. For the rigid-body controls, all three body joints were locked in the straight configuration, while only the posterior ribbon-fin section was asymmetrically biased with a maximum bias angle α = 30°. Two biased-length ratios, X br / L fin = 0.20 and 0.30, were examined under otherwise identical test conditions. Three independent trials were performed for each condition. As shown in Figure 3 D, the rigid-body strategies produced turning rates of 3.48 ± 0.75°/s and 4.16 ± 0.38°/s for X br / L fin = 0.20 and 0.30, respectively. Even at the smallest active body-bending coefficient ( β = 0.14), the turning rate reached 14.97 ± 2.64°/s, corresponding to 4.30-fold and 3.60-fold increases relative to the two rigid-body controls. At β = 0.46, the turning rate further increased to 23.67 ± 1.69°/s, representing 6.80-fold and 5.69-fold increases, respectively. The reduced-order model predictions are compared with the experimental means in Figure 3 D. Across the five β values, the model reproduced the experimentally observed monotonic increase in turning rate, with the predicted values showing good agreement with the experimental means, which supports the model as a reduced-order description of the observed turning-rate trend. These results provide a direct comparison under matched actuation conditions and demonstrate the substantial maneuverability gain enabled by active body bending. The turning-radius comparison is shown in Figure 3 E. The rigid-body strategies yielded turning radii of 0.79 ± 0.07 m and 0.69 ± 0.05 m for X br / L fin = 0.20 and 0.30, respectively. At β = 0.14, active body bending reduced the turning radius to 0.48 ± 0.01 m, corresponding to reductions of 39.24% and 30.43% relative to the two rigid-body controls. At the largest tested bending coefficient ( β = 0.46), the turning radius decreased to 0.26 ± 0.01 m, representing reductions of 67.09% and 62.32%, respectively. Within the active-bending series, increasing β from 0.14 to 0.46 reduced the mean turning radius by 45.83%. Least-squares circular fitting of representative trajectories gave R 2 values above 0.90 for all conditions, supporting the use of a circular-path approximation for extracting the steady turning radius. Figure 3 E also compares the predicted and measured turning radii. The model reproduced the experimentally observed monotonic decrease in turning radius with increasing β , with the predicted values showing good agreement with the experimental means. Together, the turning-rate and turning-radius comparisons support the proposed force-moment reconfiguration mechanism: active body bending redirects distributed ribbon-fin thrust and increases its effective moment arm, thereby generating a larger yaw moment and a tighter turning trajectory than posterior fin-ray biasing alone. The articulated body and distributed actuator array enabled the robot to adopt and maintain these curved configurations during anal-fin undulation. However, changing β simultaneously alters the hydrodynamic planform, mass distribution, rotational inertia, added-mass characteristics, and orientation of the forces generated by the ribbon fin. The measured trends therefore represent the combined system-level effect of adopting different prescribed body configurations rather than an isolated contribution of continuous active body deformation. Naiad occupies an intermediate performance region, demonstrating a turning radius comparable to that of highly agile natural swimmers. Vertical ascending locomotion experiments Vertical motion represents a distinctive locomotor capability of the black ghost knifefish. Anal-fin kinematic measurements in free-swimming black ghost knifefish have further quantified heave and hovering behaviors, providing biological support for the vertical locomotion gait. 20 In 2011, CFD simulations of ribbon fin propulsion conducted by Curet et al. 25 observed downward jet flows generated during fin undulation, indicating continuous lift production during locomotion. Zoological observations further reveal that the species propagates traveling waves from both ends toward the midpoint of the fin to cancel anterior-posterior thrust, thereby utilizing the resulting lift for hovering and ascending maneuvers in water. Investigating this phenomenon holds significant value for enhancing the vertical mobility and control of underwater robots. As illustrated in Figure 4 A, we replicated this vertical motion gait on our robotic fish: the head and tail servo motors oscillate in phase, sending traveling waves toward the center, while the middle servo (No. 6) remains stationary, serving as the termination point for waves from both sides. To minimize significant body oscillation during movement, we ensured that the traveling waves from the head and tail contain complete periodic cycles, with the wavelength set to 490 mm. For the near-neutrally buoyant robot, upward motion occurs when this heave force exceeds the residual effective weight and vertical drag. Before the vertical ascending experiments, the robotic fish was adjusted to a near-neutrally buoyant state using the two-step buoyancy calibration method described in the System architecture section and Figure S10 . The buoyancy-adjusted robotic fish was tested in a transparent water tank with a depth of 70 cm. A camera was positioned laterally to record its vertical ascent. Kinematic data were obtained through post-processing of the video footage. During each trial, the robotic fish was commanded to execute a vertical ascent from the bottom of the tank to near the water surface. The average vertical velocity was calculated by dividing the vertical displacement during the steady-state phase by the time interval between the initial and final frames of that phase, as identified through video analysis. The actual ascent process is illustrated in Figure 4 B. To investigate the relationship between ascent speed and actuation parameters, we conducted two experimental series: 1) under a fixed frequency of 0.99 Hz, amplitudes of 30°, 40°, and 50° were tested; 2) at a fixed amplitude of 40°, frequencies of 0.72 Hz, 0.88 Hz, 0.99 Hz, and 1.44 Hz were applied. Each parameter combination was repeated for three trials to ensure reliability. To identify the optimal amplitude for subsequent experimental phases, a series of vertical locomotion tests was conducted at a fixed frequency of 0.99 Hz, examining the relationship between undulation amplitude and vertical velocity. As shown in Figure 4 C, the measured vertical velocities fell within the centimeter-per-second range. Among the three amplitude conditions tested, the 40° setting yielded the highest average vertical velocity of 3.08 cm/s. The 30° condition followed closely with an average velocity of 2.94 cm/s, whereas the 50° condition resulted in a markedly lower velocity of 1.52 cm/s. It was observed that under the 50° amplitude condition, the robotic fish exhibited pronounced body oscillation, which compromised upward stability and increased energy dissipation. This instability is likely responsible for the reduced velocity recorded under this condition. Based on these results, a subsequent investigation into the frequency-velocity relationship was performed at an amplitude of 40°. As shown in Figure 4 D, a positive correlation was observed between the vertical motion speed and the actuation frequency. The minimum speed of 1.92 cm/s was recorded at 0.72 Hz, while the maximum speed reached 3.80 cm/s at 1.44 Hz. This study confirms that the vertical motion gait of the black ghost knifefish can be effectively translated into robotic design, and provides a preliminary investigation into the kinematic principles of this gait. Given that vertical motion inherently requires stability and reliability, the achieved speed of 3.80 cm/s meets the expected performance for such movements. By optimizing the undulatory parameters of the anal fin, the ascending performance of the biomimetic knifefish robot can be enhanced. These findings hold significant value for advancing three-dimensional underwater mobility and expanding the application scenarios of robotic fish. Complex multi-obstacle environment and field demonstrations To further evaluate whether body-fin coordinated locomotion could support free movement in confined underwater settings, an untethered demonstration prototype was developed, as illustrated in Figure 5 A. This prototype was designed for application-oriented demonstrations because removing the power and communication tether avoided cable-induced interference during free swimming in narrow passages. A functional demonstration was conducted in a custom 2 × 3 m pool containing multiple obstacles arranged to form a narrow, winding passage. Several bends had local curvature radii comparable to, or smaller than, the continuous-turning radius measured in open water, requiring sequential forward, reverse, and turning maneuvers. During each trial, the robot was teleoperated by an operator who selected predefined open-loop gait commands; no real-time autonomous path-following or feedback correction was implemented. Each run was recorded on video, and the transit time was used to describe task completion. The robot completed multiple trials, with transit times ranging from 69 to 75 s. The trajectory of the robotic fish ( Figure 5 B) and the recorded motion process ( Video S5 ) show that the robot could pass through the obstacle course by switching among forward swimming, reverse swimming, and turning gaits. The untethered biomimetic knifefish robot was also used to compare two commanded gait modes on the same demonstration platform: median and/or paired fin propulsion (MPF) and body and/or caudal fin propulsion (BCF). Compared with the main experimental platform used for the parametric locomotion tests, the untethered prototype had a slightly different actuator layout. In particular, the main platform used body joints distributed closer to the body-bending pattern observed in black ghost knifefish, whereas the untethered prototype placed its body-bending joints closer to the posterior body region to simplify onboard integration. The untethered prototype also incorporated one additional fin-ray/fin-servo unit. The servo specifications and open-loop gait-generation strategy were otherwise kept consistent between the two platforms. Accordingly, the MPF-BCF comparison should be interpreted as a preliminary functional comparison conducted on the untethered demonstration prototype, rather than as a direct controlled comparison with the main experimental platform. Repeated measurements of steady-state motion parameters showed that the average straight-line speed under MPF mode was 0.147 m/s, slightly lower than the 0.185 m/s achieved under BCF mode. In contrast, MPF mode showed a higher average turning rate (29.2°/s) and a smaller turning radius (0.64 m) than BCF mode (17.4°/s and 1.32 m), as shown in Figure S9 . These results indicate that the undulating anal-fin drive can improve turning performance on the untethered prototype. However, because the untethered prototype differs from the main experimental platform in actuator distribution and onboard implementation, this comparison supports the functional value of MPF-like maneuvering and should not be interpreted as a strict platform-level ablation. In addition to laboratory demonstrations, we deployed the robot in a natural lake environment to evaluate its basic free-swimming capability and onboard underwater imaging performance outside the laboratory tank. Using an onboard underwater camera, we recorded the robot during operator-selected open-loop gait commands and captured underwater video from the robot’s perspective, as shown in Figure 5 C and Video S6 . These field demonstrations provide qualitative evidence that the distributed servo-driven undulating fin and flexible body can support stable commanded locomotion and onboard visual recording in an outdoor aquatic environment. They do not constitute autonomous navigation or closed-loop path-following tests. Future versions should integrate onboard state estimation, environmental sensing, and feedback control to evaluate autonomous navigation and inspection performance in more complex natural underwater environments. Discussion Based on the black ghost knifefish, this paper presents Knifefish-inspired robotic fish Naiad, which enhances locomotion performance by undulatory anal fin propulsion and a flexible body. The robot demonstrates multiple locomotion capabilities, including forward/backward swimming, turning, and vertical movement. We conducted extensive robotic experiments to investigate the locomotor performance of the robotic fish under various motion states in free-swimming conditions, and analyzed the influence of different propulsive wave parameters and body curvature β on its performance in straight-line swimming, turning, and vertical ascent. Experimental results show it achieved a straight-line velocity of 17.21 cm/s (0.20 BL/s), a minimum COT of 3.42, a maximum turning rate of 23.67°/s (0.41 rad/s), and a minimum turning radius of 0.26 m (0.31 BL), indicating high energy efficiency and exceptional maneuverability. Furthermore, the robot achieved a vertical ascent speed of 3.80 cm/s. A simplified untethered prototype was successfully tested in an obstacle-filled channel, confirming its ability to navigate complex underwater environments. In addition, field tests were conducted in a natural lake environment to demonstrate the robot’s capability for real-world exploration. This work provides a viable solution for enhancing the agility and adaptability of underwater robots in confined spaces. The outstanding agility, locomotor efficiency, and three-dimensional mobility of Naiad make it highly promising for exploration applications in complex shallow-sea environments. Previous studies on knifefish-inspired locomotion and ribbon-fin propulsion have advanced the field along four related directions: biological observation and theoretical analysis of knifefish locomotion, 20 , 21 , 22 , 23 , 24 CFD and numerical studies of ribbon-fin hydrodynamics, 25 , 26 , 27 , 28 , 29 robotic implementation and locomotion-performance evaluation, 30 , 31 , 32 , 33 , 34 and control-oriented studies of gait regulation and navigation. 35 , 36 , 37 The present study is positioned primarily within the third direction, namely the design and experimental characterization of a knifefish-inspired robotic platform. Compared with earlier robotic studies that mainly examined fin-only undulatory propulsion, often in tethered experimental settings, Naiad integrates a distributed undulatory anal fin with an actively bendable body on a free-swimming platform. This design was motivated by biological observations showing that knifefish maneuvering depends not only on ribbon-fin waves but also on coordinated interactions between fin-wave modulation and body deformation. Our free-swimming experiments indicate that this body-fin coordination improves the robot’s maneuvering capability, particularly by increasing turning rate and reducing turning radius. We further quantified how fin wavelength, frequency, amplitude, and body curvature affect propulsion efficiency and maneuverability. Together, these results provide a more integrated assessment of knifefish-like locomotion and demonstrate that the body-fin coordinated platform can support multiple gait modes, including linear swimming, turning, vertical ascent, and confined-environment navigation. These features define the main advantages of Naiad: body-fin coordinated maneuverability, free-swimming validation of ribbon-fin propulsion, and integrated multi-gait capability within a single robotic platform. In terms of linear swimming, Naiad reached a maximum speed of 0.20 BL/s, which is lower than the peak speeds reported for some free-swimming ribbon-fin robotic platforms, such as the knifefish-inspired vessel developed by Liu and Curet 32 (approximately 0.71 BL/s) and the undulating-fin robot reported by Vercruyssen et al. 34 (0.65 BL/s). This difference is likely related in part to the lower undulation frequency range used in the present experiments (0.42–0.99 Hz), because previous biological and robotic studies have shown that swimming speed generally increases with undulation frequency. 24 , 30 However, Naiad was designed primarily for low-speed maneuvering in confined environments rather than high-speed cruising. The linear-swimming experiments also revealed a reversal in the speed-wavelength relationship between low and high frequencies. To quantify this effect, we performed a two-way ANOVA, which showed that frequency exerted a strong main effect on forward swimming speed, whereas the wavelength main effect was not significant; nevertheless, the significant frequency × wavelength interaction indicates that the influence of wavelength changed across frequency levels. This result is consistent with biological observations that black ghost knifefish primarily increase fin-wave frequency when accelerating at low to moderate swimming speeds, while wavelength changes less markedly. 24 A plausible explanation for the interaction is that increasing wavelength raises the traveling-wave speed, but simultaneously reduces the number of waves distributed along the fin. Previous CFD analyses have shown that these effects may compete: increasing wave speed tends to enhance inertial force production, whereas increasing wavelength reduces the number of waves along a fixed fin, which can decrease the effective wetted area and alter the balance between surge and heave forces. 29 Robotic experiments have also reported diminishing speed gains and greater body pitching at higher fin frequencies. 32 Thus, at low frequencies, the wave-speed benefit of increasing wavelength may dominate, whereas at high frequencies it may be offset by changes in fin-wave distribution, wake interactions, actuator loading, and body oscillation. Because the present study did not directly quantify head or body oscillations or the surrounding flow field, this interpretation should be regarded as a mechanism-based hypothesis rather than direct evidence of energy dissipation. The calculated body-scale Reynolds numbers (Re = 5.66 × 10 4 ∼ 1.45 × 10 5 ) indicate that the present experiments were conducted in an inertia-dominated hydrodynamic regime, and the resulting conclusions are therefore most applicable to fish-scale or robot-scale ribbon-fin swimmers operating within a comparable Reynolds number range. The corresponding Strouhal numbers (St = 0.603–0.837) exceeded the commonly reported St ≈ 0.2–0.4 range associated with efficient undulatory locomotion. 60 , 61 Because S t = f · A U , these elevated values indicate that, for a given undulation frequency and amplitude, the robotic fish did not convert the imposed fin motion into forward swimming speed as effectively as swimmers operating within the canonical efficient Strouhal number range. This may reflect the unoptimized body drag and fin morphology, fin compliance, or limitations of the servo-driven actuation system, although their individual contributions were not isolated. Regarding locomotion efficiency, an exploratory regression analysis revealed a significant nonlinear association between COT and fin-wave speed V wave , as shown in Figure S7 F, with the fitted minimum occurring at approximately V wave ≈ 0.5 m/s. This trend is consistent with previous ribbon-fin studies showing that thrust depends on the relative velocity between wave propagation and swimming and that hydrodynamic efficiency exhibits a broad maximum at an intermediate U / V wave ratio. Notably, the low-COT conditions of Naiad corresponded to U / V wave ≈ 0.28–0.35, close to the hydrodynamically favorable range reported previously. 39 Because COT = P i n ¯ m g v ¯ , this result reflects a favorable balance between input power and swimming speed. However, the underlying mechanisms may involve complex interactions among thrust generation, fin deformation, hydrodynamic losses, and actuator loading. Further flow-field measurements, CFD analysis, and actuator-resolved power measurements are needed to clarify these mechanisms and determine whether optimizing body streamlining, fin morphology, compliance, and actuator bandwidth can reduce St and improve propulsion efficiency. Within the tested operating regime, Naiad achieved a maximum turning rate of 23.67°/s (0.41 rad/s) and a minimum turning radius of 0.31 BL by combining undulatory anal-fin propulsion with prescribed curved-body configurations. During each trial, the body-joint servomotors were held at the joint angles corresponding to the prescribed body bending coefficient β , while the ribbon-fin amplitude, wavelength, and frequency remained fixed. Directly comparable turning data for ribbon-fin robots remain limited. The highly underactuated soft ribbon-fin robot reported by Adibnazari et al. 62 generated banked turns through asymmetric ribbon-fin actuation without an articulated body and exhibited an average instantaneous turning radius of 25.7 m (approximately 26.17 BL), although steady-state turning was not reached because of the limited test volume. This cross-platform comparison provides useful context for the compact turning achieved by Naiad but should not be interpreted as a controlled ablation, because the platforms differ in morphology, actuation architecture, gait definition, and experimental conditions. The present results therefore support a system-level association between increasing prescribed body curvature and faster, more compact turning under fixed ribbon-fin kinematics. They do not isolate the individual contributions of changes in hydrodynamic planform, mass distribution, rotational inertia, added-mass characteristics, and fin-force orientation. These effects are inherently coupled during body reconfiguration and are similarly difficult to separate in biological knifefish turning. 21 Vertical ascent by ribbon-fin propulsion has received relatively limited attention in knifefish-inspired robotic systems, although it represents an intriguing gait observed in biological black ghost knifefish. In this study, the free-swimming Naiad platform allowed us to experimentally examine this gait under practical locomotor conditions and to assess how undulatory parameters influence vertical motion. The vertical ascent speed increased with undulation frequency, which is broadly consistent with the vertical force measurements reported by Curet et al. 25 for a ribbon-fin-driven robotic knifefish. This trend may be explained by the increase in fin-wave speed V wave at the fixed wavelength used here; a higher wave speed can increase the momentum transfer associated with the downward jet and thereby enhance the upward heave force. In contrast, the effect of angular amplitude was non-monotonic: the ascent speed increased from 30° to 40° but decreased at 50°. This result suggests that amplitude does not determine vertical performance in a simply linear manner. Biological observations by Youngerman et al. 20 showed that heave and hover motions in black ghost knifefish involve similar whole-fin amplitudes, although undulation amplitude differs substantially between these two behaviors, implying that undulation amplitude may not be the sole determinant of vertical locomotor performance. Together with our experimental results, this observation suggests that an optimal amplitude range may exist for vertical ascent. These findings extend prior biological and robotic studies by demonstrating counter-propagating ribbon-fin-wave ascent on a free-swimming robotic platform and by providing preliminary evidence for how frequency and amplitude affect this gait. In conclusion, this work provides a comprehensive framework for the development of highly maneuverable knifefish-inspired robots capable of performing complex locomotion tasks in confined underwater environments. By successfully merging fin-driven propulsion with active body flexibility and validating system performance in both laboratory and field settings, we have demonstrated a viable path toward autonomous and adaptive underwater robots. Addressing the identified limitations in sensing, depth adaptability, and communication will further enhance the practicality and scope of such systems in real-world applications. Limitations of the study Despite these advantages, several limitations of the current platform should be acknowledged. First, the straight-line swimming speed of Naiad remains lower than that of some previously reported free-swimming ribbon-fin robotic platforms. This difference may be associated with the relatively low undulation-frequency range, the servo-driven actuation architecture, and the lack of hydrodynamic optimization of the fin-body system. More broadly, Naiad is a reduced-order functional abstraction whose fin-ray number, material properties, body flexibility, and tested wave-parameter ranges differ from those of biological black ghost knifefish. Future refinements should therefore consider actuator bandwidth, fin compliance, body streamlining, wave-parameter optimization, and quantitative biological morphology and kinematics to improve locomotor performance. 63 In addition, the reduced-order turning model contains empirically identified parameters and does not explicitly resolve unsteady flow, added-mass effects, or body-fluid interactions. Its agreement with the experimental data should therefore be interpreted as an in-sample consistency check and a force-moment-level explanation of the observed trends, rather than as an independent hydrodynamic validation. Second, the present study focuses on robotic-platform design and gait-level locomotor characterization rather than trajectory-tracking control. The experiments used prescribed open-loop gait commands, with the fin-wave parameters and body curvature fixed within each condition. Accordingly, the confined-environment and outdoor lake demonstrations validate commanded multi-gait maneuverability and basic free-swimming capability, but do not constitute evaluations of autonomous navigation, closed-loop stability, disturbance rejection, or field success rate. Future studies should integrate onboard state feedback and compare closed-loop controllers using common metrics such as tracking accuracy, energy consumption, and robustness. Third, the reported COT was calculated from the electrical input to the servo-actuation subsystem and excludes the power consumption of onboard control, sensing, communication, and voltage-conversion components. Whole-system power consumption, controlled battery-discharge characteristics, and operational endurance were not quantified. The untethered prototype also has limited sensing and communication capabilities, with perception primarily provided by an onboard camera and communication relying on Bluetooth or tethered links. These constraints currently limit operation in turbid, large-scale, or deep-water environments. Multimodal sensing, 64 acoustic communication, 65 improved sealing, actuator-resolved power monitoring, and more robust onboard control will be required for practical underwater deployment. Method details System architecture The structural design of Naiad draws inspiration from the black ghost knifefish, which has a knife-shaped, laterally compressed body and an elongated ribbon-like anal fin. Based on morphological characteristics and a micro-CT three-dimensional model, we extracted key morphometric parameters of the black ghost knifefish, including body length, body width, body height, and anal-fin dimensions. Key ratios, including the anal-fin-to-body-length ratio (0.818), body-width-to-length ratio (0.073), and anal-fin length-to-width ratio (8.75), were used to guide the design of Naiad. The robot was constructed with a total body length of 845 mm, a body width of 106 mm, and a height of 195 mm. The ribbon fin measures 700 mm in length and 80 mm in width, giving a fin-to-body-length ratio of 0.828, close to the biological template. The body is divided into four modular segments fabricated by resin-based 3D printing. These segments are connected by biaxial servo joints that enable passive and active yaw-plane body bending. The tail gradually tapers in both segment thickness and rigidity, aided by an embedded manganese steel spring plate (0.3 mm thick), to support smooth curvature during motion. Comprehensive technical specifications and design parameters are provided in Table S1 . The outer shell was designed as a hollow cavity with water-injection and drainage ports, enabling coarse and continuous adjustment of the internal water volume and the overall density of the robot. Fine buoyancy trimming was then achieved by adding or removing small counterweights at selected internal and external positions to tune the net buoyancy and the relative positions of the center of gravity and center of buoyancy. Before the vertical ascending experiments, the robot was placed in the water tank with the actuators switched off, and the water volume and counterweights were iteratively adjusted until it remained approximately suspended in water and maintained a stable posture. The buoyancy-adjustment structure is shown in Figure S10 . The Naiad robot emulates the knifefish’s unique locomotion, which relies on undulatory waves traveling along its anal fin. In nature, this ribbon fin allows the fish to perform forward and reverse swimming, vertical hovering, and precise turning within narrow aquatic environments. To mimic this, Naiad’s ribbon fin consists of 11 servo-driven fin rays arranged in a distributed array. The fabrication and assembly process of the flexible ribbon fin used in the knifefish-inspired robot is illustrated in Figure S11 . The main flexible fin membrane was fabricated from Ecoflex 00–30 silicone elastomer. As shown in Figure S11 A, Ecoflex Part A and Part B were mixed at a 1:1 mass ratio and stirred thoroughly until a homogeneous mixture was obtained. The mixture was subsequently degassed under vacuum to remove entrapped air bubbles, thereby reducing internal defects and improving the uniformity of the cured silicone structure. The degassed mixture was then poured into a customized mold and cured on a heated platform at approximately 70 °C, producing a soft ribbon fin with the designed geometry and dimensions. As shown in Figure S11 B, the cured silicone fin membrane was secured to the rigid metal fin rays using bolted connections, and the proximal end of each fin ray was similarly attached to a servo arm using bolts. Repeating this procedure for all fin rays completed the assembly of the integrated flexible ribbon-fin propulsion system. The servo units are configured with a wave propagation controller based on phase differences and amplitude control to reproduce a traveling wave. Seven distinct gaits were programmed: forward swimming, reverse swimming, forward turning (left/right), reverse turning (left/right), and vertical ascent. Forward propulsion is achieved by initiating a posteriorly traveling wave along the fin, while reverse swimming is realized via an anteriorly traveling wave. Turning maneuvers involve coordinated fin undulation and body bending. The vertical ascent gait uses counter-propagating waves from both ends of the ribbon fin, which converge centrally to produce downward jet flow for upward movement. Naiad’s hardware architecture consists of four subsystems: Decision Center, Sensor Layer, Driver Layer, and Power Supply Unit (PSU). A Raspberry Pi 4B serves as the main onboard controller, receiving commands through a wireless keyboard and executing control scripts through a LAN connection. Servo actuation signals are generated by a PCA9685 16-channel PWM driver board, which communicates with the Raspberry Pi through the I2C protocol. Visual feedback is provided by a front-mounted camera, with data transmitted to the host computer. The actuation layer consists of 14 servos, including 3 for the multi-jointed body and 11 for the ribbon fin. Power is supplied by a dedicated 7000 mAh lithium battery and a voltage regulation unit that adjusts the 12 V output to 6.5–6.8 V for servo compatibility. To isolate motion-related energy consumption for COT measurements, the control and actuation power circuits are separated. Power consumption is monitored by a DC power measurement module sampling at 50 Hz, with data transmitted to the host system through USB-TTL. For the untethered version of the biomimetic knifefish robot, the prototype uses a modular architecture consisting of three body segments interconnected by two high-torque servos (25 kg cm). The forward segment contains a transparent compartment that houses the core electronic systems and includes an externally accessible power switch. Undulatory propulsion is achieved through two independently actuated fin sections: the anterior fin module is driven by eight servos (20 kg cm each), and the posterior section is controlled by four additional servos. The electronic architecture consists of four main subsystems: control, actuation, power supply, and communication. The control subsystem uses an Arduino Uno microcontroller coupled with a PCA9685 PWM driver, which generates 50 Hz PWM signals distributed to all servos through the I2C bus. The actuation subsystem comprises 14 servos (two for inter-segment articulation and twelve for fin actuation). Power was supplied by a 7.4 V, 7000 mAh Li-ion battery, corresponding to a nominal stored energy of 51.8 Wh. This rated energy was reported only to describe the onboard power capacity and was not used to estimate operational endurance, because no controlled battery-discharge or whole-system runtime test was performed. Communication is handled by an HC-06 Bluetooth module, enabling wireless command transmission from a host device. To improve waterproofing, all seams were sealed with vulcanized silicone rubber and coated with epoxy resin. After assembly, the prototype demonstrated free swimming and achieved a turning radius of 0.6 BL. Experimental details For the linear locomotion tests of Naiad, a DC power sensor was used to measure the aggregate electrical input power of the servo-actuation subsystem at a sampling rate of 50 Hz ( Figure S1 ). Figure S2 presents the raw and low-pass-filtered time-resolved power signals under four fin-amplitude conditions, which exhibit periodic variations associated with ribbon-fin actuation. The mean input power for each condition was calculated by averaging over multiple complete motion cycles, while Figure S7 B summarizes the cycle-averaged power across the tested wavelength-frequency combinations. The COT was calculated from the mean actuation power and the corresponding swimming speed. Swimming speed was determined from the time required for the robot to traverse a fixed distance ( Figure S3 A), and each condition was tested in triplicate. The corresponding displacement-time data are provided in Figure S4 . The reported COT therefore represents an actuation-level electrical COT and excludes the power consumption of the control, sensing, communication, and voltage-conversion subsystems. Because no controlled battery-discharge test, subsystem-resolved power budget, or whole-system endurance measurement was conducted, the nominal battery capacity was not used to estimate deployment runtime. Turning performance was evaluated using a camera-based motion capture system to dynamically track the robotic fish’s complete turning process. Before each turning trial, the body-joint servomotors were commanded to the target joint angles corresponding to the body bending coefficient β and subsequently held under position control throughout the maneuver. Thus, β represents a prescribed quasi-static body curvature rather than continuous body undulation during turning. The recorded videos were processed using Tracker software, with length calibration applied to extract precise positional data over time. This allowed computation of key kinematic parameters such as linear velocity, acceleration, and angular velocity. The turning trajectory was fitted to a circular curve, yielding a high coefficient of determination (R 2 &gt; 0.9) across all tests ( Figure S8 ), confirming both the robot’s exceptional turning stability and the validity of the circle-fitting method for determining the turning radius. A clear inverse correlation was observed between the body bending coefficient β and the turning radius, underscoring the critical role of coordinated body flexion and undulatory fin motion in enhancing maneuverability. The outdoor lake test was conducted as a preliminary qualitative demonstration of teleoperated open-loop swimming and onboard imaging rather than as a quantitative field-performance experiment. Accordingly, no claims regarding field success rate, endurance, operational stability, or validated real-world deployment were derived from this demonstration; all quantitative performance conclusions were based on the replicated controlled-tank experiments. Quantification and statistical analysis Unless otherwise stated, each robotic locomotion condition was tested in three independent trials ( n = 3), where n represents the number of repeated experimental trials conducted under the same prescribed motion parameters. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to evaluate the effects of undulation wavelength and frequency on swimming performance, with statistical significance defined as p &lt; 0.05. Least-squares regression was used to characterize relationships between locomotion parameters and performance metrics. Turning radii were determined by least-squares circular fitting of the recorded trajectories, and goodness of fit was evaluated using R 2 . The reduced-order turning-model parameters were calibrated using the experimental data as described in the method details . Statistical analyses and data processing were performed using MATLAB and Python.
