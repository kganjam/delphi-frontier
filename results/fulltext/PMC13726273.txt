Background The physical and chemical properties of water are key drivers in the evolution of aquatic life, forcing organisms to adapt to variable ecological conditions [ 1 , 2 ]. In animals, the ancestral metabolic conditions enabling adaptation to seawater underwent modifications with the colonization of new environments, notably the transition from saltwater to freshwater [ 3 ]. Fishes exhibit remarkable physiological plasticity, which facilitates environmental colonization shaping global evolutionary patterns [ 4 ]. Euryhalinity, the ability to tolerate a wide range of salinity levels, is considered an ancestral, transitional trait that enables colonization of new adaptive zones, promoting diversification and eventually leading to the origin of stenohaline taxa (i.e., those that only survive in a narrow range of salinity levels). Today, most fishes are stenohaline, restricted to either freshwater (FW) or saltwater (SW) with narrow salinity tolerances, while true euryhaline species are rare [ 5 , 6 ]. Only 3 – 5% of fish species retain their euryhaline capacity, allowing them to withstand broader salinity ranges and to transition between FW and SW environments [ 7 ]. Despite its adaptive advantages, euryhalinity is rare, likely due to significant physiological and immunological costs [ 8 ]. It is therefore likely that this trait would have been lost, were it not for strong selection pressures [ 9 ]. However, these uncommon euryhaline taxa contribute to evolutionary diversity, as landlocking and FW radiations frequently occur in ancestrally euryhaline lineages [ 6 ]. Euryhalinity can impose substantial energetic demands, although the energetic costs of osmoregulation vary considerably among species and environmental contexts, reaching up to 62% of total energy expenditure under extreme salinity conditions in some fishes [ 10 , 11 ]. For instance, gills alone require approximately 7% of the total energy to sustain the osmoregulatory processes necessary under these conditions [ 10 , 11 ]. Despite its high energy demands, euryhalinity provides a significant advantage specially in the current context of global climate change, where rapid environmental shifts towards salinization are increasingly impacting FW ecosystems [ 12 , 13 ]. Aquatic habitats are typically classified by salinity levels, from freshwater (FW) with &lt; 0.5 Practical Salinity Units (PSU), to brackish water (BW) with 0.5–30 PSU, and seawater/saltwater (SW) with 30–40 PSU [ 14 ]. In natural ecosystems, salinity fluctuations are a key abiotic factor affecting the adaptability and survival of species. Osmoregulation capacity determines an organism's salinity tolerance, as it directly influences biological processes such as growth, survival, lipid metabolism in fish larvae, gonad development, embryo hatchability, feeding and digestion, among others [ 15 , 16 ]. Fishes generally maintain body fluid osmolarity at about one-third of SW [ 17 ]. In SW, they lose water through osmosis and gain Na + and Cl − via diffusion, compensating through SW ingestion, minimal urine excretion, and active salt excretion via the gills. Conversely, FW fish excrete dilute urine, actively absorb salts across the gills, and may also ingest salts through their diet [ 17 – 19 ]. Euryhaline fishes, commonly found in estuaries or as migratory species, endure frequent salinity shifts. Coastal SW salinity fluctuates due to surface runoff, rainfall, floods, and tides, requiring euryhaline fish to exhibit high iono-osmoregulatory plasticity. Their response involves cellular remodeling in osmoregulatory organs, including modulation of transporters, channels, and intercellular structures [ 18 , 20 ]. Under salinity stress, osmosensors trigger molecular cascades that induce cellular remodeling, allowing fish to restore osmotic balance by adjusting or switching between different osmolar environments [ 18 , 20 ]. As research continues to explore independent instances of adaptation to different osmotic environments in fishes, it remains intriguing to determine whether evolution repeatedly employs similar pathways to address the same challenges [ 19 , 21 ]. However, physiological flexibility varies among individuals. For example, in Fundulus heteroclitus , populations exhibit differing abilities to acclimate to extreme osmotic conditions, with intraspecific divergence in osmotic plasticity observed even among geographically close populations [ 22 ]. FW populations demonstrate greater efficiency in compensating for hypoosmotic challenges than BW or coastal populations, despite long-term rearing in SW. Additionally, northern coastal populations tolerate hypoosmotic challenges better than their southern counterparts [ 21 , 23 ]. Salinity acclimation in fish involves high plasticity in key osmoregulatory organs such as the gills, kidney, liver, and gastrointestinal tract (GIT), which maintain homeostasis in FW, BW, and SW environments [ 18 , 24 ]. The gills serve as the primary site for osmotic sensing and compensation, taking up ions in FW and excreting salt in SW [ 18 , 19 ]. The GIT also plays a crucial osmoregulatory role beyond nutrient absorption, especially in SW fishes, which drink SW and rely on intestinal processes, such as bicarbonate secretion and ion precipitation, to reduce chyme osmolality and facilitate water absorption. In fish species exposed to varying salinities, differences in expression of genes related to ion transport, water and solute channels, as well as immune functions, have been identified particularly in mucosal surfaces such as the gills, skin, and GIT [ 24 , 25 ]. A conserved molecular response to osmotic stress has also been identified, involving regulation of the cell cycle, metabolism, protein synthesis, cytoskeletal organization, and immune responses [ 26 , 27 ]. Studies of fish osmoregulatory processes using RNA-seq have increased substantially in recent decades [ 28 – 30 ], with most studies focusing either on model species, such as mummichogs, three-spined sticklebacks, medakas and guppies [ 21 , 31 – 34 ], or on economically important species, such as Nile tilapia, Atlantic salmon, rainbow trout, and Asian, European and spotted seabass [ 9 , 26 , 35 – 41 ]. However, studies focusing on non-model wild species remain scarce, despite their potential to provide insights into salinity adaptation in the current context of global climate change. To address this gap, we investigated transcriptomic variation associated with contrasting osmotic environments in wild populations of Aphanius iberus , the Spanish toothcarp. This small endemic cyprinodontiform is distributed along the eastern Mediterranean coast of the Iberian Peninsula and is currently classified as Endangered in the Libro Rojo de las Especies Españolas [ 42 , 43 ] and in the Spanish Catalogue of Threatened Species (Real Decreto 139/2011, Official State Gazette BOE-A-2011–3582). Aphanius iberus inhabits diverse Mediterranean aquatic habitats, including groundwater springs, coastal lagoons, river mouths and saltmarshes, with salinities ranging from freshwater-like conditions to hypersaline systems such as the Marchamalo salt flats. Apart from studies conducted on Fundulus heteroclitus [ 21 ], transcriptomic research on osmoregulation remains limited within Cyprinodontiformes, a globally distributed and diverse order that includes more than 1,400 species, although some studies have addressed species such as Aphanius dispar [ 44 ], Poecilia reticulata [ 34 ] and Limia perugiae [ 45 ]. Although A. iberus has been the subject of several genetic and ecological studies [ 46 – 52 ], the transcriptomic basis of its response to naturally contrasting osmotic environments remains unexplored. Material &amp; methods Sample collection and processing We selected four populations of Aphanius iberus inhabiting contrasting osmotic environments along the eastern Mediterranean coast of the Iberian Peninsula, covering part of the species’ natural distribution range (Fig. 1 A), and collected four individuals from each locality (Supp. Table 1). At the time of sampling, these populations covered a salinity range from 0.76 PSU in Adra, 4.8 PSU in Cabanes and 7.4 PSU in Santa Pola, to 52 PSU in Marchamalo. The selected populations correspond to four previously identified Operational Conservation Units (OCUs) according to Nester et al. [ 50 , 51 ], but fall within two broader, geographically adjacent genetic lineages described by Estarellas and López-Solano et al. [ 52 ], with two populations assigned to the Levantine lineage and two to the Murcian lineage. This sampling design was therefore intended to capture a broad salinity gradient, ranging from near-freshwater irrigation ponds to hypersaline lagoon-associated systems, while explicitly incorporating the hierarchical genetic structure of A. iberus . Thus, although the selected populations differ genetically and occur in locally distinct hydrological settings, they are embedded within the same broad Mediterranean climatic and biogeographic context, allowing salinity-associated transcriptomic variation to be explored while accounting for population genomic background. Fig. 1 Patterns of differential gene expression across Aphanius iberus populations; A Distribution range of the species with colored dots indicating populations sampled in this study and labels showing salinity measured at sampling in PSU; B Barplot showing number of differentially expressed genes per pairwise population comparison in gills (green, left) and GIT (pink, right); C - D Hierarchically clustered heatmaps of the top 20 DEGs in gills ( C ) and GIT ( D ) from pairwise sequential comparisons of populations in order of salinity level The northernmost population was sampled in the Natural Park of Cabanes i Torreblanca, Castellón province (Séquia del Polo; 40.18500, 0.2063). This habitat consists of historical irrigation and drainage channels (“séquias”) located within a coastal wetland system close to the sea. Salinity at the time of sampling was 4.8 PSU, although this locality can experience substantial fluctuations depending on freshwater input, sea-level influence and groundwater dynamics, with values ranging from moderately brackish conditions to much higher salinities during periods of reduced freshwater input. One additional brackish population was sampled in a coastal saline system characterized by relatively stable intermediate salinity levels. The Santa Pola population (Los Xiprerets; 38.19778, −0.578633), located within the Santa Pola Salt Pans Natural Park, Alicante, inhabits coastal saltmarshes influenced by evaporation, rainfall, freshwater inputs and local water-table dynamics. Salinity at the time of sampling was 7.4 PSU, corresponding to brackish conditions. Although salinity may increase during warm and dry periods and decrease after rainfall or freshwater inflow, this locality represents a comparatively stable brackish environment within the sampled gradient. The Marchamalo population (37.63536, −0.717281), located in Murcia and associated with the Mar Menor lagoon system, represented the hypersaline end of the sampled gradient. Salinity reached 52 PSU at sampling, exceeding typical Mediterranean seawater salinity (~ 38 PSU). Marchamalo was selected because it clearly exceeded seawater-like salinity conditions, whereas brackish populations of A. iberus may periodically approach marine salinity depending on local hydrological dynamics. This population was therefore considered representative of a hypersaline wild habitat, while acknowledging that A. iberus does not inhabit open marine environments but rather sheltered coastal wetlands, saltmarshes and lagoon-associated habitats where salinity can exceed seawater values. The southernmost population was sampled in irrigation ponds in Adra, Almería province (36.7633659, −2.9513865), where individuals were found under very low salinity conditions (0.76 PSU at sampling). Although this value does not correspond to strictly freshwater conditions in a physicochemical sense, available observations indicate that salinity in this locality remains always below 1 PSU. Therefore, Adra was treated as the freshwater-like, low-salinity endpoint of the sampled gradient and is hereafter referred to as the FW group as an operational classification. This classification reflects its relative position within the four-population design rather than implying a fully stable freshwater experimental treatment. Overall, these four populations were selected to represent naturally occurring wild populations distributed along a broad osmotic gradient, from freshwater-like conditions to hypersaline lagoon-associated habitats. They should therefore not be interpreted as controlled salinity treatments, but as natural populations in which salinity is the main environmental contrast considered, while population identity, local hydrology and other site-specific variables are explicitly acknowledged. At the time of sampling, salinity, water temperature, dissolved oxygen, pH and conductivity were recorded in situ at each locality. These field measurements, together with available historical environmental information, were used to characterize the hydrological context of each population. Fish were collected using minnow trap nets and euthanized by immersion in an overdose of buffered tricaine methanesulfonate (MS-222, 0.1%), ensuring rapid loss of consciousness, in accordance with internationally accepted guidelines for the use of fish in research. To reduce potential biases related to sex, age and body size, sex balance was considered during sampling when possible, and only visually identifiable adult individuals were selected for transcriptomic analyses. Adult status was assessed based on body size and external morphology, which is reliable in this short-lived species, whose individuals generally complete their life cycle within one to two years [ 49 ]. All procedures were carried out by qualified personnel under the relevant institutional and regional permits (see Ethics approval and consent to participate section). Specimens were then preserved in RNAlater solution (Invitrogen™) and kept at 4 °C until further processing. The gills and gastrointestinal tract (GIT) of four individuals per population were dissected under a binocular stereoscope and stored at −80 °C at the National Museum of Natural Sciences (Madrid, Spain) for subsequent RNA extraction and cDNA library preparation. RNA extraction, library preparation and sequencing Total RNA was extracted from 32 tissue samples, corresponding to the gills and GIT of four specimens per population (Supp. Table 1). Tissue samples were homogenized in Trizol reagent, and total RNA was subsequently purified using the Invitrogen™ PureLink™ RNA Mini Kit following the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA quantity and quality were assessed with a NanoDrop spectrophotometer. RNA-seq libraries were constructed using the Illumina Stranded mRNA Prep kit, which enriches polyadenylated mRNA through poly-A selection, following the Ligation Reference Guide. Library quality, insert size and concentration were checked with an Agilent TapeStation 4150 and verified with a Qubit High Sensitivity dsDNA Assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific®). Libraries were sent to Novogene Europe (Cambridge, UK) and sequenced using Illumina NovaSeq X Plus technology to generate 150 bp paired-end reads. Sequencing depth per library is reported in Supplementary Table 1, and all libraries were sequenced in a single NovaSeq X Plus run. Sequence processing, read mapping and abundance estimation The quality of the raw reads generated from each of the 32 libraries was evaluated with FastQC v0.12.1 [ 53 ]. Adapter sequences and low-quality bases and reads (phred score &lt; 30) were removed with Trimmomatic v0.39 [ 54 ]. Clean reads were aligned to the reference genome of Aphanius iberus , sequenced and annotated by our research group (GCA_028564705.1 NCBI GenBank; [ 55 ]), using Star Aligner v2.7.9a [ 56 ]. Read abundance was then estimated with StringTie v2.1.7 [ 57 ] following the Simplified Workflow and the prepDE.py script of the StringTie package was used to generate a count matrix with the estimated abundances for each gene. Differential gene expression, gene ontology and pathway analyses All analyses were conducted using Trinity v2.13.2 [ 58 ]. Differential Gene Expression (DGE) analysis was performed through pairwise comparisons using edgeR from trinityrnaseq v2.13.2, executed via the run_DE_analysis.pl script. No single population or salinity condition was used as a universal control or baseline. Instead, each pairwise comparison was interpreted relative to the two populations being compared, and the direction of expression was assigned according to the population showing higher expression within that specific comparison. Library-size differences among samples were accounted for during edgeR normalization prior to differential expression testing. Differentially expressed genes were identified using an FDR-adjusted threshold of ≤ 1e-3 and an absolute log2 fold-change ≥ 2. Heatmaps were created using pheatmap v1.0.12 [ 59 ], while volcano plots were generated with EnhancedVolcano v1.24 [ 60 ] using the same significance and fold-change thresholds for visualization. Principal component analyses (PCA) were also performed separately for gills and GIT using normalized expression values to visualize sample-level clustering across populations, assess the global structure of transcriptomic variation, and evaluate whether variation in sequencing depth influenced sample clustering (Supp. Figure 2). These analyses were conducted in RStudio v2024.12.1.563 [ 61 ]. Gene Ontology (GO) analysis was conducted using InterProScan [ 62 ] and sma3s [ 63 ] annotations from the Aphanius iberus reference genome [ 55 ], which were assigned to each differentially expressed gene. Finally, annotated genes from each comparison were filtered using InterProScan and classified according to the population showing higher expression within each pairwise comparison. These results were used for KEGG (Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes) analysis [ 64 ], performed in ShinyGO v0.80 [ 65 ]. Results Transcriptomic profiles reveal tissue- and population-specific expression differences across contrasting salinity habitats We generated a total of 313.31 GB of data from the 32 sequenced libraries that were used for the DGE analyses. After quality filtering and read cleaning, libraries ranged from 3.15 to 31.43 million high-quality reads (Supp. Table 1). PCA analyses based on normalized expression values showed no anomalous clustering of the lowest-coverage libraries, suggesting that variation in sequencing depth did not drive the main transcriptomic patterns observed across populations. They also revealed clearer population-level separation in the gills than in the GIT, supporting a stronger habitat-associated transcriptomic structure in gill tissue. Within the gill PCA, hypersaline Marchamalo was clearly differentiated along PC2, and the distinct positions of Marchamalo and freshwater-like Adra in the ordination were consistent with the strong DEG signal detected between the two salinity endpoints (Supp. Figure 2; Fig. 2 ; Supp. Table 3). Using an FDR-adjusted threshold of ≤ 1e-3 and an absolute log2 fold-change ≥ 2, we identified a total of 1,428 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in the gills and 504 in the GIT, of which 1,081 and 398, respectively, were functionally annotated (Supp. Table 2). The pairwise comparisons among all samples revealed that the gills exhibited a higher number of differentially expressed genes across populations than the GIT in most cases (Fig. 1 B). The populations that exhibited the greatest amount of differentially expressed genes (DEG) for gills were the hypersaline population of Marchamalo (140 DEGs on average) and the freshwater-like population of Adra (138 DEGs), which correspond to the highest and lowest salinity levels, respectively (Fig. 1 B). Regarding the GIT, the populations that exhibited the greatest number of overexpressed genes were the hypersaline population of Marchamalo (79 DEGs) and the brackish population of Cabanes (61 DEGs) (Fig. 1 B). In the gills, among the most upregulated genes in Adra we identified an SLC12A-domain gene belonging to the cation-chloride cotransporter family , a group that includes NCC-like transporters involved in ion uptake by freshwater teleost gill ionocytes. This pattern is therefore consistent with ionocyte-associated ion transport processes described in teleost gills, although bulk gill RNA-seq does not allow us to determine whether this signal is ionocyte-specific or reflects a broader epithelial response. We also identified an arginase domain-containing gene, linked to nitrogen metabolism and osmotic balance. In contrast, the gill of Marchamalo showed upregulation of a cytochrome c domain gene, consistent with increased energetic demand under hyperosmotic conditions. In the GIT, among the most upregulated genes in Adra we identified an ATPase domain-containing gene, involved in active ion transport under hypoosmotic conditions (Fig. 2 A-D). Functional annotation and pathway analyses highlight osmoregulatory, metabolic, epithelial and stress-related processes associated with salinity responses We conducted Gene Ontology (GO) and KEGG analyses to investigate whether any functions or pathways were overrepresented among the DEGs identified (Fig. 3 and Supp. Table 4). For both analyses, we identified a greater number of overrepresented functions in the gills, with 207 different terms in the category of Molecular Function, 176 terms for Biological Processes, and 40 terms for Cellular Component. For the GIT, 300 different terms were identified for Molecular Function, 354 for Biological Processes and 75 terms for Cellular Component. It is noteworthy to mention again that the comparison of the brackish population of Cabanes vs. the hypersaline population of Marchamalo contributed the most genes to almost every term in every category of the GIT. Most overrepresented GO terms in the category of Biological Processes in both tissues were associated with salinity regulation, including transmembrane transport (GO:0055085) and ion transport (GO:0006811) which were present in both tissues and all populations. Additionally for gills, cation transport (GO:0006812), potassium ion transport (GO:0006813), and ion transmembrane transport (GO:0034220) were observed across all populations. In all populations of the GIT, the term calcium ion transmembrane transport (GO:0070588) was identified. In addition, the following terms were identified in the gills: anion transport (GO:0006820), chloride transport (GO:0006821), and sodium ion transport (GO:0006814) (Supp. Table 5). In the comparison of the BW populations (Cabanes and Santa Pola) with the rest, we identified many overrepresented GO terms associated to immune response (GO:0006955) in both tissues (Supp. Table 6). The majority of the identified genes that contributed to this GO term can be grouped in the following categories: interleukins, immunoglobulins , and MHC complex in the gills; and proteins of the membrane attack complex, cell signaling proteins (Helically-extended SH3 domain) , and interleukins for the GIT (Supp. Table 6). Discussion Our results reveal strong tissue-specific transcriptomic patterns across wild Aphanius iberus populations inhabiting contrasting osmotic environments, with the gills showing a higher number of differentially expressed genes than the gastrointestinal tract (GIT) in most population pairwise comparisons (Fig. 1 B). This pattern is consistent with the central role of the gills as a primary epithelial interface for ion exchange, osmotic sensing and environmental interaction. PCA analyses based on normalized expression values further supported this tissue-specific contrast, showing clearer population-level separation in the gills than in the GIT (Supp. Figure 2). Together, these results indicate that gill transcriptomes captured the strongest habitat-associated structure in our dataset (Fig. 1 B-D). In contrast, GIT expression patterns should be interpreted with additional caution. The GIT performs multiple functions beyond osmoregulation, including digestion, nutrient absorption, epithelial renewal and host-microbiome interactions, which may attenuate or obscure salinity-associated transcriptional patterns [ 70 , 74 ]. The weaker clustering observed in the GIT is consistent with this broader physiological complexity and suggests that GIT-associated DEGs may integrate salinity effects together with variation in gut region, luminal content, feeding state, microbiota and epithelial turnover. Despite these tissue-specific differences, both organs showed differentially expressed genes related to ion transport, epithelial remodeling, metabolism, immune regulation and stress-associated processes (Supp. Tables 1, 5), supporting the idea that salinity-associated variation in A. iberus involves coordinated physiological adjustment across multiple osmoregulatory tissues [ 17 , 75 ]. Several cyprinodontiform fishes closely related to A. iberus are capable of remodeling the gill epithelium through reorganization of cytoskeletal structures, water flux channels and other cellular components in response to environmental change [ 9 , 24 , 76 ]. In this context, our detection of genes related to cytoskeletal organization, tight junctions, membrane components and chromatin-associated structural remodeling provides a transcriptomic link to these known epithelial remodeling processes. More broadly, under hyperosmotic conditions, fishes increase intestinal ion uptake to generate the osmotic gradient that drives water absorption, while the resulting excess salt load is subsequently excreted primarily across the gills. Under hypoosmotic conditions, they actively take up ions across the gills to maintain ionic homeostasis [ 9 , 17 , 18 , 20 ]. Our results are consistent with these physiological patterns, showing DEGs involved in osmoregulatory processes and salinity-associated physiological adjustment across different functional categories (Table 1 and Supp. Table 7). Across all pairwise comparisons, the strongest transcriptomic differentiation was observed in contrasts involving the hypersaline Marchamalo population, particularly against the freshwater-like Adra population, which represents the lowest-salinity endpoint of the sampled gradient. These contrasts showed the highest number of DEGs and the broadest representation of GO terms and KEGG pathways (Fig. 1 , Supp. Tables 1–5). In the following sections, we focus on the genes and biological processes most relevant to salinity-associated transcriptomic variation, particularly those related to ion and membrane transport, epithelial remodeling, energy metabolism, immune function and cellular stress responses. Because gills and GIT contribute to osmoregulation through different physiological roles, we explicitly indicate whether each pattern was observed in gills, GIT, or both tissues. Ion transport and solute carrier genes vary among populations inhabiting contrasting osmotic environments Ion transport emerged as one of the clearest functional signatures in our dataset. Many DEGs were associated with ion transport and solute carrier functions, particularly in membrane-related processes that depend strongly on external osmotic conditions [ 24 , 34 , 77 , 78 ]. These genes varied across pairwise population comparisons, with the strongest differences observed between the freshwater-like Adra and hypersaline Marchamalo populations, and fewer differences detected between the two brackish populations. This pattern suggests that expression differences in ion transport and solute carrier genes are associated with the degree of osmotic difference among habitats, although population background and other site-specific factors may also contribute to these patterns. Physiologically, this is consistent with the need for active transport when external salinity deviates strongly from internal osmotic conditions, because fishes must regulate ion and water exchange to maintain osmotic and ionic homeostasis. In line with this, previous studies have shown significant variation in the abundance of transporters and ion channels in the anterior intestine, contributing to different osmoregulatory mechanisms across the gastrointestinal tract (GIT) [ 35 ]. Among ion transporters, ATPases play a fundamental role by using ATP hydrolysis to drive ion movement across membranes. In gill ionocytes, Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase is located in the basolateral membrane, where it transports Na⁺ from the cytoplasm into the interstitial fluid and K⁺ into the cell, thereby generating the electrochemical gradients that drive transepithelial ion transport. ATP1A1-related transcripts were detected in the gills, including a transcript annotated as a Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase alpha-1 subunit that was upregulated in freshwater-like Adra relative to the brackish populations. However, the available annotation did not resolve these transcripts as atp1a1a or atp1a1b , and their expression patterns differed among transcripts and population comparisons. This prevents assignment of the observed signal to a specific ATP1A1 paralog but remains consistent with salinity-associated modulation of Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase components in teleosts [ 9 , 21 , 26 , 31 , 32 , 37 , 66 – 69 ]. Similar modulation of Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase expression has been reported in Arctic charr ( Salvelinus alpinus ) and chum salmon ( Oncorhynchus keta ), although the direction and contribution of specific isoforms depend on tissue, species and salinity regime [ 27 , 68 ]. To evaluate whether the observed expression patterns were consistent with canonical ionocyte physiology, we examined the gill DEG annotation for established freshwater- and seawater-type ionocyte markers. In the gills, among the genes upregulated in freshwater-like Adra, we identified an nhe3 transcript and a Na⁺-Cl⁻ symporter/NCC-like transcript. Their expression direction is consistent with NHE3- and NCC-mediated Na⁺ uptake described in freshwater teleost gill ionocytes [ 79 ]. Conversely, the seawater-type markers cftr and nkcc1 were upregulated in hypersaline Marchamalo relative to brackish Cabanes and Santa Pola. Thus, although bulk-gill RNA-seq cannot assign these expression signals to specific ionocyte subtypes, the directional shifts of the resolved freshwater- and seawater-type markers are consistent with canonical predictions of ionocyte physiology (Supp. Table 2). Solute carriers are central to ion balance under changing osmotic conditions because they mediate the movement of small solutes across biological membranes [ 20 , 24 , 31 ]. In our dataset, several solute carrier families were differentially expressed in the gills, especially in pairwise comparisons involving the freshwater-like Adra population. In addition to the resolved nkcc1 transcript described above, we identified a separate SLC12A-family transcript annotated as a bumetanide-sensitive sodium-(potassium)-chloride cotransporter, a family involved in Na⁺, K⁺ and Cl⁻ transport and osmoregulation in teleosts, including European sea bass ( Dicentrarchus labrax ) [ 80 – 83 ]. However, the reference-genome annotation did not confidently resolve this transcript as either NKCC1 or NKCC2 ; therefore, we refer to it here as an NKCC-like transcript. This assignment should be interpreted cautiously because NKCC1 and NKCC2 paralogs have distinct physiological roles in teleosts. Because the annotation does not resolve this transcript as NKCC1 or NKCC2 , no specific branchial transport function can be confidently assigned to it. Instead, it supports the differential expression of an SLC12A/NKCC-family transporter across population comparisons, consistent with the broader involvement of ion transport pathways in osmotic regulation. Further phylogenetic or locus-specific analyses would be required to confirm its paralog identity and functional role in A. iberus. Additional ion-transport-related candidates included solute carriers and exchangers, ion channels and ABC transporters (e.g., solute carrier family 9 member 1 (slc9a1) and solute carrier family 12 member 9 (slc12a9), Na⁺/K⁺/Ca 2 ⁺-exchange protein 3, sodium channel protein type 4 subunit alpha B, neurotransmitter-gated ion channel, voltage-dependent L-type calcium channel subunit alpha-1C, ATP-binding cassette sub-family D member and ATP-binding cassette sub-family C member 7 ; Supp. Table 2). Among these, the voltage-dependent L-type calcium channel subunit alpha-1C was differentially expressed in comparisons involving brackish Cabanes, brackish Santa Pola and hypersaline Marchamalo, whereas the Na⁺/K⁺/Ca 2 ⁺-exchange protein 3 was detected in the freshwater-like Adra vs hypersaline Marchamalo comparison. These genes are involved in ionic balance, cell volume regulation and salinity sensing [ 24 , 31 , 71 , 77 , 78 , 82 – 84 ], suggesting that A. iberus may rely on multiple complementary transport mechanisms to maintain cellular homeostasis across fluctuating Mediterranean wetland environments. Similar modulation of solute carriers and gill ion-transport components has been reported during salinity transfer in other teleosts [ 9 , 20 , 32 , 33 ]. Tight-junction remodeling provides another mechanism through which gill permeability may vary across osmotic environments. Claudins are functionally diverse proteins: some paralogs strengthen the paracellular barrier, whereas others form ion-selective pores, and their responses to salinity vary among paralogs, tissues and species [ 85 ]. Claudin-3 was consistently upregulated in the gills of hypersaline Marchamalo, indicating salinity-associated remodeling of tight-junction composition. However, without functional characterization of this paralog in A. iberus , its increased expression cannot be interpreted unambiguously as either enhanced barrier formation or increased paracellular permeability. It is therefore best considered evidence of epithelial junction remodeling under hypersaline conditions. In contrast, aquaporins , which are water channel proteins associated with volume regulation and water flux and are often reported as salinity-responsive in freshwater or salinity-transfer contexts, including in European sea bass acclimated to freshwater [ 20 , 77 , 84 , 86 ], did not show differential expression across populations in our dataset (Supp. Table 2). However, this absence of differential expression should be interpreted cautiously, as aquaporin regulation may be isoform-specific, tissue-specific, post-transcriptional or dependent on exposure timing. Thus, our results do not rule out the involvement of aquaporins in water balance in A. iberus , but indicate that other osmoregulatory categories, such as ion transport and epithelial remodeling, were more prominent among the DEGs identified in this study (Supp. Tables 2, 4). At the functional level, GO analysis revealed multiple terms associated with osmoregulation, mainly including ion and transmembrane transport (Fig. 3 , Supp. Table 4). GO terms included ion , cation and anion transport , as well as pathways mediating the movement of potassium, chloride, sodium and calcium ions across membranes (Fig. 3 , Supp. Table 4). These processes are key components of osmotic regulation in fishes, with numerous studies reporting differences between FW and SW populations [ 21 , 31 , 34 , 69 – 71 , 77 , 78 , 87 ]. In our case, we found tissue-specific patterns, with most terms related to ion transport found exclusively in the gills, consistent with their primary role in mediating ion exchange with the external environment, while calcium ion transmembrane transport was exclusive to the GIT, indicating a distinct and complementary role of this tissue in ionic homeostasis. Energy metabolism reflects osmoregulatory adjustment across salinity gradients Numerous DEGs associated with energy metabolism were identified across population comparisons, particularly in the gills, where transcriptional divergence was highest. These included transporters and enzymes participating in energy-intensive processes, as well as genes involved in protein synthesis and key metabolic pathways [ 70 , 76 ]. This pattern is consistent with the energetic demands of osmoregulation, which arise largely from ATP-dependent ion transport and vary among tissues, species and salinity regimes. Recent tissue-based estimates suggest that gastrointestinal osmoregulatory processes account for approximately 2.5% of standard metabolic rate, whereas branchial osmoregulatory costs have been estimated at approximately 2.4–4% of basal metabolic rate in marine or seawater-acclimated teleosts [ 88 ]. Accordingly, KEGG analyses showed significant enrichment of pathways related to energy acquisition and metabolic adjustment, including amino acid, lipid and carbohydrate metabolism , as well as central aerobic pathways such as the citrate cycle (TCA cycle). Similar enrichment of energy-related pathways under seawater conditions has been reported in other teleosts [ 17 , 36 , 39 , 67 , 69 , 70 , 87 ]. In the gills, the Glycine, Serine and Threonine Metabolism pathway was significantly enriched in the comparison between freshwater-like Adra and hypersaline Marchamalo. Serine, derived from glycolytic intermediates, and glycine, synthesized from serine, are central to metabolic flexibility under environmental change, as previously reported [ 9 , 72 , 77 ]. The citrate cycle (TCA cycle) was enriched between freshwater-like Adra and brackish Cabanes, consistent with its fundamental role in aerobic oxidation of carbohydrates and fatty acids and with previous reports of citrate metabolism shifts during salinity acclimation [ 89 ]. The GO term lipid transport was also consistently overrepresented in the gills (Supp. Table 4). In contrast, the GIT showed enrichment of lipid- and carbohydrate-related pathways, including arachidonic acid metabolism (hypersaline Marchamalo vs freshwater-like Adra) and starch and sucrose metabolism (brackish Cabanes vs freshwater-like Adra). The GO term carbohydrate metabolic process was repeatedly overrepresented across comparisons. Additionally, sulfur metabolism was enriched in the comparison between hypersaline Marchamalo and brackish Santa Pola, highlighting modulation of sulfate pathways that participate in both assimilatory and dissimilatory energy processes (Fig. 3 , Supp. Table 4). Our analyses also identified DEGs involved in fatty acid and carbohydrate metabolism, which serve as major energy reservoirs or direct substrates [ 17 , 31 , 33 , 39 , 67 , 69 , 72 ]. Among them, mannose-P-dolichol utilization defect 1 ( mpdu1 ) was upregulated in the GIT of brackish Cabanes and in the gills of brackish Santa Pola. Because MPDU1 participates in the utilization of dolichol-linked mannose and glucose during glycan biosynthesis, this expression pattern is consistent with variation in glycosylation-related processes [ 90 ]. A B30.2/SPRY domain-containing transcript was also upregulated in the GIT of hypersaline Marchamalo. B30.2/SPRY domains are involved in molecular recognition and protein–protein interactions, although the specific functional significance of this transcript in the present dataset remains uncertain [ 91 ] (Supp. Table 2). Collectively, these results suggest that genes involved in energy production and substrate utilization vary across salinity-associated population contrasts in both osmoregulatory tissues, although the clearest habitat-associated patterns were observed in the gills. These differences are therefore interpreted as metabolic adjustments associated with contrasting osmotic environments in wild populations, rather than as controlled salinity-treatment responses [ 10 , 11 ]. Regulatory and signaling pathways suggest transcriptomic plasticity at salinity extremes Signal transduction and transcription-related genes were prominently represented in comparisons involving the freshwater-like Adra and hypersaline Marchamalo populations. These genes are biologically relevant because osmotic challenges require rapid detection of environmental change, intracellular signal propagation and coordinated transcriptional adjustment. As primary osmosensory organs, gills can initiate signaling cascades that modulate cellular metabolism, mitochondrial activity, protein turnover and epithelial remodeling [ 20 , 66 ]. One of the internal responses to osmotic challenge may involve increased translational capacity and protein synthesis, although these processes can become compromised under excessive salinity stress [ 20 , 36 , 87 ]. In our dataset, many of the top DEGs were associated with receptor-mediated signaling, ubiquitination, chromatin remodeling, RNA/DNA processing and transcriptional regulation . These patterns were also supported by GO categories related to signal transduction and transcriptional regulation, including zinc-binding and nucleic acid-interacting domains , which are consistent with transcriptional modulation under osmotic stress [ 20 ]. Moreover, KEGG analyses indicated that amino acid metabolism may contribute to transcriptional and signaling responses under contrasting osmotic conditions, in agreement with previous studies [ 9 , 71 , 77 , 87 ]. Rather than indicating a single uniform salinity response, these patterns suggest broad regulatory adjustment across salinity-associated population contrasts. Regulatory and signaling genes were particularly evident at the salinity extreme populations and could be grouped into pathways related to receptor-mediated signaling, ubiquitin/protein turnover, transcriptional regulation and RNA/DNA processing. In freshwater-like Adra, gill-associated genes included G protein-coupled receptors, HECT E3 ligase catalytic domain-containing genes, ASB-5 and integrase zinc-binding domain-containing genes, whereas GIT-associated genes included transcriptional and post-transcriptional regulators such as Brf1, TBP-binding domain-containing genes, calpain-5, leucine-rich repeat proteins and guanylate kinase domain-containing proteins . In hypersaline Marchamalo, gill-associated candidates included cytochrome c, endonuclease/exonuclease/phosphatase superfamily members and ribonuclease H-like proteins , while GIT-associated domains included sterile alpha motif, butyrophilin-like, integrase catalytic core, MyHC-beta/XRCC4-like, HECT E3 ligase catalytic domain and GTPase IMAP family member 7 . Many of these genes and domains are associated with signaling, immune regulation, protein turnover or RNA/DNA processing pathways [ 20 , 39 , 70 ] (Supp. Table 2). Together, these patterns suggest enhanced regulatory and signaling activity at the salinity endpoints, potentially supporting rapid transcriptional adjustment under contrasting osmotic conditions. KEGG enrichment analyses reinforced this pattern. Amino acid metabolism emerged as an important component of regulatory and metabolic adjustment under contrasting osmotic conditions, consistent with previous studies [ 9 , 71 , 77 , 87 ]. Cysteine and methionine metabolism was enriched in the gills of freshwater-like Adra, whereas tryptophan metabolism and selenocompound metabolism were enriched in GIT comparisons involving freshwater-like Adra vs hypersaline Marchamalo and brackish Cabanes, respectively (Fig. 3 , Supp. Table 4). Additional pathways associated with hypersaline Marchamalo included RNA degradation and DNA replication . Consistently, over half of the identified GO terms were linked to signal transduction and transcriptional regulation, with particularly strong representation in freshwater-like Adra and hypersaline Marchamalo across all three GO categories. In the gills, Molecular Function terms included nucleic acid, ATP, zinc ion and GTP binding , together with activities such as m ethyltransferase and 2-oxoglutarate-dependent dioxygenase . Biological Process terms encompassed DNA integration, proteolysis, transcriptional regulation, lipid transport, GPCR signaling and transposition . In the GIT, protein phosphorylation , cell surface receptor signaling and DNA repair further emphasized regulatory remodeling at the salinity salinity extremes. Together, these results indicate that transcriptional reprogramming and signaling activity were most pronounced in the freshwater-like and hypersaline populations of the sampled gradient (Supp. Table 4). In the two brackish populations, Cabanes and Santa Pola, gene expression patterns reflected intermediate but still evident regulatory activity. These candidates could be grouped into pathways related to ubiquitination and protein turnover, DNA repair and polymerase activity, glycosylation, RNA processing, post-translational modification, vesicular trafficking and receptor-mediated signaling. In the gills, detected domains included HECT E3 ligase catalytic domain, DNA repair- and polymerase-associated domains , and glycosyltransferases such as BGnT-7 . In the GIT, brackish populations showed transcription- and signaling-related factors, including Piwi-like protein 2, TATA box-binding protein-like 2, DnaJ homolog subfamily B member 6, helically extended SH3 domains, Tudor domains and P2X purinoreceptor , alongside proteins involved in RNA processing, such as DDX43 , post-translational modification, including F-box proteins and transglutaminase-like proteins , and vesicular trafficking, including PRA1 . These profiles suggest that brackish populations maintain active regulatory and signaling networks, but with a more moderate transcriptomic signal than the freshwater-like and hypersaline endpoints of the sampled gradient. Immune and apoptosis-related pathways vary across salinity gradients Beyond osmoregulatory and metabolic adjustment, immune modulation also emerged as an important component of salinity-associated transcriptomic variation in A. iberus . Immune-related genes varied across tissues and pairwise population comparisons, with distinct signatures in both brackish populations and the salinity extreme populations. In the gills, immune-related patterns were mainly associated with cytokine signaling and antigen presentation , whereas in the GIT they were linked to cytokine-related regulation , stress proteins and apoptosis-related pathways (Fig. 3 ; Supp. Table 4). These patterns suggest that epithelial interfaces involved in osmoregulation may also undergo immune adjustment under contrasting osmotic conditions. This interpretation is consistent with the role of environmental salinity as a physiological stressor in fishes [ 34 , 66 ], and with previous studies identifying salinity-associated immune responses across osmoregulatory organs [ 34 , 39 , 71 ]. In Mediterranean wetlands, such immune modulation may be particularly relevant because fluctuating osmotic conditions can interact with epithelial stress, pathogen exposure and energetic trade-offs. Cytokine-mediated signaling was one of the clearest immune-related patterns associated with the sampled salinity gradient. Cytokines are key mediators of communication between immune and non-immune cells during early immune activation, and their modulation is consistent with rapid responses to environmental stressors such as fluctuating salinity [ 92 ]. In our dataset, GO analyses identified immune-related terms mainly in the gills, where an interleukin-8-like chemokine domain was upregulated in brackish Cabanes and Santa Pola relative to the freshwater-like and hypersaline populations. The corresponding interleukin-like transcript showed a population-specific pattern: it was upregulated in brackish Cabanes relative to hypersaline Marchamalo but downregulated in freshwater-like Adra relative to both hypersaline Marchamalo and brackish Santa Pola. Other cytokine-associated regulators reinforced the brackish-population signal. ATP receptors and Butyrate response factor 1 (Tristetraprolin), a zinc finger protein involved in immune regulation, cell proliferation and differentiation [ 93 , 94 ], were detected in brackish populations. In the GIT, helically extended SH3 domain-containing proteins and nuclear factor interleukin-3-regulated protein were upregulated in brackish populations relative to hypersaline Marchamalo. Together, these patterns suggest enhanced cytokine-associated signaling in intermediate-salinity habitats, consistent with immune modulation reported in other euryhaline fishes [ 37 , 39 , 69 , 71 , 95 ] (Fig. 3 , Supp. Table 4). Beyond cytokine signaling, genes involved in antigen presentation, humoral immunity, stress responses and cytotoxic activity also varied across pairwise population comparisons. MHC class II genes, which play a central role in adaptive immune activation [ 96 ], were upregulated in freshwater-like Adra and hypersaline Marchamalo relative to brackish Santa Pola, suggesting increased antigen-presentation-related activity at the salinity endpoints. In contrast, immunoglobulin C1-set domain-containing proteins were downregulated in freshwater-like Adra relative to brackish Cabanes and hypersaline Marchamalo, indicating population-specific modulation of humoral immune components. Heat shock 70 kDa protein 12A was upregulated in the GIT of freshwater-like Adra and in the gills of brackish Santa Pola. Because HSPs contribute to protein refolding, cell-cycle control and immune regulation under stress, this pattern is consistent with their documented responsiveness to salinity variation [ 37 , 71 – 73 ], although reduced HSP expression has also been reported under particular stress contexts or combined salinity-temperature challenges [ 32 , 38 ]. Similar upregulation of HSP family members, including ubiquitin-related proteins, has been described during salinity transfer [ 32 , 41 ]. Finally, the membrane attack complex component/perforin (MAC), essential for cytotoxic activity in innate and adaptive immunity, was downregulated in brackish Cabanes relative to freshwater-like Adra, further highlighting that terminal immune effector pathways also varied among populations (Fig. 3 , Supp. Tables 2, 4). Immune modulation may also be linked to apoptosis-related pathways under osmotic stress. Acute salinity shifts can activate immune pathways, whereas prolonged or repeated exposure to osmotic stress may alter immune capacity and increase susceptibility to infection [ 97 ]. In our dataset, both KEGG and GO analyses supported the involvement of apoptosis-related pathways, particularly in the GIT [ 20 , 21 , 37 , 70 , 98 ]. The KEGG Apoptosis pathway was enriched in GIT comparisons between brackish Cabanes and freshwater-like Adra, and several regulatory components associated with apoptotic or inflammatory modulation were also detected. These included apoptosis inhibitor-2 (c-IAP2) and junctophilin in the GIT of brackish Santa Pola, as well as Butyrate response factor 1 (Tristetraprolin), a regulator of tumor necrosis factor production and inflammatory signaling, which was upregulated in the GIT of brackish Cabanes (Supp. Table 4). However, these patterns require particular caution because GIT transcriptomes may also be influenced by gut segment composition, luminal content, microbiota, feeding state, mechanical abrasion and epithelial turnover. Therefore, apoptosis-related expression patterns in this tissue should not be attributed exclusively to salinity but rather interpreted as reflecting the combined effects of osmotic conditions and broader intestinal physiology. Together, these results suggest that immune regulation and apoptosis-related pathways form part of the broader stress physiology of A. iberus across contrasting osmotic habitats. In the GIT, these patterns should be interpreted as the combined outcome of salinity-associated stress, epithelial renewal and intestinal physiological complexity. Structural remodeling in salinity-associated contrasts Genes associated with cytoskeletal organization and structural remodeling, together with the majority of GO terms within the Cellular Component category, were differentially expressed across the salinity gradient, with a particularly marked signal in BW populations. In the GIT of both BW populations, several DEGs were linked to cytoskeletal structure and cell adhesion, suggesting enhanced structural plasticity under fluctuating salinity conditions. In brackish Cabanes, a KASH domain containing protein was detected, a domain typically localized at the outer nuclear membrane and involved in nuclear positioning and force transmission between the nucleoskeleton and the cytoplasmic cytoskeleton. Additionally, the helically-extended SH3 domain was upregulated in both BW populations (Cabanes and Santa Pola). This variant SH3 fold functions as a lipid interaction module capable of binding acidic phospholipids and is associated with integrin complexes, which mediate cell–cell adhesion and mechanotransduction [ 17 , 25 , 70 ]. These patterns suggest that brackish populations may involve increased cytoskeletal and adhesion-related remodeling under fluctuating salinity conditions. Changes in cell volume caused by passive water diffusion necessitate rapid reorganization of cytoskeletal elements to preserve structural integrity and cellular homeostasis. Chromatin-associated structural components were also modulated. Histones, which regulate chromatin condensation and transcriptional accessibility, have previously been implicated in osmotic acclimation [ 23 ]. In our dataset, Histone H1a was upregulated in the gills of freshwater-like Adra, while Histone H4 was upregulated in the gills of brackish Cabanes. Furthermore, the chromatin-target of PRMT1 (C-terminal) protein was upregulated in the GIT of brackish Santa Pola. This protein associates with facultative heterochromatin and participates in transcriptional regulation, suggesting coordinated structural and epigenetic remodeling under salinity stress [ 21 , 23 ] (Supp. Table 2). Within the Cellular Component category, numerous GO terms were associated with structural elements and membrane organization. The most frequent terms across both tissues included integral component of membrane, membrane, and extracellular region. Gill-specific enrichment of plasma membrane and extracellular space further highlights the structural role of this tissue as the primary interface for osmotic exchange, whereas cytoplasm and integral component of plasma membrane were particularly prominent in the GIT (Fig. 3 , Supp. Table 4). Together, these results indicate coordinated remodeling of cytoskeletal, membrane, and chromatin architecture in response to salinity stress. Transcriptional signals associated with repetitive elements across varying salinity regimes Several differentially expressed transcripts were annotated with domains or sequence similarity related to repetitive elements. For example, Tc1 DNA transposon-related annotations, associated with the mobilization of DNA transposons to new genomic loci, were detected among upregulated transcripts in the gills of both the freshwater-like Adra and hypersaline Marchamalo populations [ 99 ]. In addition, a Barney/Tcb1 transposase-like annotation was detected among upregulated transcripts in the GIT of hypersaline Marchamalo, whereas an L1/LINE-related annotation was detected in the gills of brackish Santa Pola. L1 elements are non-LTR retrotransposons belonging to the Long Interspersed Nuclear Elements (LINEs), which transpose through an RNA intermediate [ 100 , 101 ]. These observations suggest that TE-associated or repeat-related annotations occur among differentially expressed transcripts across salinity-associated population contrasts and tissues (Supp. Table 2). Repetitive elements can modulate the expression of genes involved in key physiological processes, including osmoregulation, and may contribute to rapid adaptation in saline or fluctuating environments by acting as sources of regulatory variation and evolutionary change [ 102 , 103 ]. However, the patterns observed here should be interpreted cautiously. In RNA-seq data from a non-model species, TE-related annotations may reflect genuine transcription of repetitive elements, but they may also arise from multi-mapping reads, annotation uncertainty, fragmented gene models or sequence similarity to conserved TE-derived domains. We also detected upregulation of an RRM (RNA recognition motif) domain - containing gene, which may indicate differences in RNA processing or post-transcriptional regulation under contrasting environmental conditions [ 104 ], although this pattern should not be interpreted as evidence of TE activation. Furthermore, a PiggyBac transposable element - derived protein annotation was detected among upregulated transcripts in the GIT of brackish Cabanes and hypersaline Marchamalo. Although PiggyBac-derived proteins are conserved across taxa, their functional roles in stress adaptation remain poorly understood [ 105 , 106 ]. Together, these findings suggest that TE-associated and RNA-processing-related annotations are present among the differentially expressed transcripts detected across salinity-associated population contrasts. However, they should be interpreted as putative components of the broader transcriptomic remodeling observed among wild populations, rather than as direct evidence of salinity-induced activation of repetitive elements or TE-mediated regulatory plasticity. Locus-specific analyses, improved repeat annotation or targeted validation would be required to determine whether these signals reflect authentic TE transcription. Ecological and conservation implications The present study shows how conserved osmoregulatory, metabolic, epithelial and immune-related processes are combined in an endangered Mediterranean cyprinodontiform inhabiting naturally fragmented and highly variable wetlands. Rather than representing isolated molecular responses, these pathways form an integrated transcriptomic signature associated with populations exposed to freshwater-like, brackish and hypersaline conditions. Similar regulatory responses have been described in better-studied euryhaline cyprinodontiforms such as Fundulus heteroclitus , where gill transcriptomes show salinity- and population-dependent regulatory responses during osmotic acclimation [ 21 ]. In this sense, A. iberus provides a complementary non-model system, because its populations persist across a Mediterranean salinity gradient that includes freshwater-like irrigation ponds, brackish coastal wetlands and hypersaline lagoon-associated habitats. The Marchamalo population is particularly informative because it represents the hypersaline endpoint of the sampled gradient, with salinity exceeding typical seawater values. Pairwise comparisons involving Marchamalo showed strong transcriptomic differentiation, especially in the gills, and included genes and functional categories related to ion transport, energy metabolism, epithelial remodeling, signaling, immune modulation and stress-associated processes. These patterns are consistent with the energetic and regulatory demands required to maintain ionic and osmotic homeostasis under hyperosmotic conditions in euryhaline fishes [ 20 , 31 , 69 ], as well as with coordinated cellular responses to osmotic challenges involving membrane transport, cytoskeletal remodeling, signaling, immune regulation, apoptosis-related pathways and metabolic adjustment [ 20 , 69 , 86 ]. However, because the sampling design did not include a population at seawater-like salinity between the brackish and hypersaline endpoints, comparisons involving Marchamalo cannot distinguish general hyperosmotic responses from mechanisms potentially associated with extreme hypersaline environments. Therefore, the expression patterns observed in Marchamalo should be interpreted as transcriptomic signatures associated with life in a hypersaline wild habitat, rather than as definitive evidence of hypersaline-specific adaptation. The comparatively lower transcriptomic differentiation observed between brackish Cabanes and brackish Santa Pola suggests that both populations share a broadly similar transcriptomic signature despite occupying somewhat different brackish habitats. Cabanes is associated with irrigation and drainage channels, where salinity and hydrology may fluctuate locally, whereas Santa Pola corresponds to a more stable coastal saltmarsh-associated wetland. Nevertheless, their reduced differentiation relative to comparisons involving freshwater-like Adra or hypersaline Marchamalo indicates that intermediate-salinity environments may impose less divergent osmotic demands within the sampled gradient. From an ecological perspective, such brackish habitats may function as transitional environments that are comparatively less extreme than the salinity endpoints and may therefore contribute to population persistence. At the same time, the persistence of A. iberus in hypersaline systems, where reduced interspecific competition may offset physiological costs, underscores the ecological flexibility of the species which may be particularly relevant in the context of accelerating anthropogenic salinization and climate-driven hydrological instability in Mediterranean wetlands. Importantly, the DEGs identified here should be interpreted as salinity-associated candidates rather than as direct salinity-responsive loci, because differential expression was assessed among wild populations rather than under controlled experimental conditions. Their relevance lies in the fact that many of these genes and pathways have previously been linked to osmoregulation, ion transport, epithelial function, metabolism, immune modulation and stress responses in fishes. Tissue-specific transcriptional signatures, particularly in the gastrointestinal tract, further suggest that spatial physiological specialization within intestinal tissues may contribute to overall osmoregulatory capacity [ 35 ]. Therefore, the interpretation of these candidates is supported by the agreement between observed expression patterns and known functional roles, while recognizing that population identity, local hydrology and other habitat-specific variables may also influence expression. By generating a transcriptomic resource for Aphanius iberus , this study expands the application of environmental transcriptomics to an endemic species currently listed as Endangered in the Spanish National Catalogue of Threatened Species ( [ 42 ]; Real Decreto 139/2011, BOE-A-2011–3582). It also provides a basis for future comparative analyses of euryhalinity within Cyprinodontiformes, complementing existing genomic and transcriptomic resources developed for species such as Fundulus heteroclitus [ 21 , 22 ]. Future integration of these transcriptomic data with population genomic scans, genomic regions of elevated differentiation and selection analyses would be valuable to assess whether salinity-associated expression differences are linked to differentiated genomic backgrounds or primarily reflect environmentally induced plasticity. Common-garden experiments, reciprocal acclimation, broader tissue sampling and targeted validation will also be necessary to determine whether the candidate genes identified here represent direct salinity-responsive mechanisms. Conclusions This study provides the first transcriptomic characterization of salinity-associated expression patterns in the endangered Spanish toothcarp, Aphanius iberus . Our results reveal strong tissue-specific transcriptomic variation across wild populations inhabiting contrasting osmotic environments, with the gills showing the clearest population-level differentiation and a higher number of DEGs related to ion transport, epithelial remodeling and energetic metabolism. The gastrointestinal tract also showed salinity-associated expression patterns, particularly involving metabolic, immune and apoptosis-related pathways, although these results should be interpreted with additional caution because of the broader physiological complexity of this tissue. The strongest transcriptomic differentiation was observed in contrasts involving the freshwater-like Adra population and the hypersaline Marchamalo population, representing the lowest- and highest-salinity endpoints of the sampled gradient. These patterns suggest that A. iberus modulates conserved osmoregulatory, metabolic, epithelial and immune-related pathways across naturally contrasting habitats. However, because the study is based on wild populations, the detected genes should be interpreted as putative salinity-associated candidates rather than as experimentally validated salinity-responsive loci. Nonetheless, many of the DEGs identified here have been previously implicated in salinity acclimation and osmoregulatory responses in other teleost species, supporting the biological relevance of the observed transcriptomic patterns. By generating a transcriptomic resource for a non-model endangered cyprinodontiform, this study expands the application of environmental transcriptomics to Mediterranean conservation genomics. Future work combining common-garden or acclimation experiments, broader tissue sampling including kidney and liver, targeted validation, and population genomic analyses will be necessary to distinguish environmentally induced plasticity from constitutive population-level divergence and to clarify the molecular mechanisms associated with salinity tolerance in A. iberus .
