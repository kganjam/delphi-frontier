“For every type of animal there is a most convenient size”—J. B. S. Haldane ( 1 ) How and why body size evolution is regulated remains a central question in biology. Organismal size is one of the most fundamental species traits, correlated with numerous biological processes from metabolism to reproduction ( 2 ). For instance, larger bodied species often inhabit wider geographic ranges, exhibit longer lifespans, display slower developmental rates, and possess higher fecundity levels compared to their smaller counterparts ( 2 – 4 ). While these adaptive responses to selective pressures may explain the underlying “why” behind size evolution, the “how” is equally critical. Body size in animals is regulated by a combination of environmental, hormonal, and genetic factors ( 5 ). Highly conserved regulatory pathways, such as the Hippo signaling pathway ( 6 ), insulin signaling pathway ( 7 ), and the TGF-beta signaling pathway ( 8 ) are crucial regulators of body size and growth. However, much of our understanding of these processes comes from model species such as nematodes, fruit flies, and mice ( 9 – 11 ). While these systems provide insights into the underlying genetic mechanisms controlling body size, they are limited in scope and do not enable broader comparative analyses across the Tree of Life. Miniaturization, or the extreme reduction of adult body size, represents a special case of size evolution and is widespread across many diverse lineages. Miniaturized taxa are commonly characterized by having greatly reduced or simplified morphologies which can range from underdeveloped features to the complete loss of certain structures ( 12 – 14 ). Various explanations for why miniaturization evolved have been proposed, such as smaller species exhibiting higher speciation rates or occupying novel niches ( 12 , 15 ), yet the mechanistic underpinnings remain poorly understood. Given the major morphological reorganizations associated with miniaturization, it is likely that underlying genomic factors are also affected. Many studies examining the genomic basis of miniaturization are confined to insects and other invertebrates, where extreme size reduction is especially common ( 16 ). For example, among wasps, evidence suggests that genes involved in cell and organ size control play a key role in size regulation of miniature species ( 17 ). Similarly, studies of tardigrades indicate that miniaturization is linked to reduced cell numbers within organs ( 18 ). However, considerably less is known about the molecular processes driving the evolution of extreme body size reduction in vertebrates, although some studies have documented genome reduction in certain miniaturized fishes and salamanders ( 19 – 21 ). Among vertebrates, miniaturization has been documented in mammals ( 22 ), birds ( 23 ), amphibians ( 13 ), and reptiles ( 24 ), but it is especially prominent in fishes ( 25 – 27 ). Some of the smallest species of fishes belong to the hyperdiverse (~2,000 species) family Gobiidae (gobies). While most gobies are generally small [&lt;100 mm ( 28 )], repeated and independent trends toward miniaturization have evolved in several genera, including pygmygobies ( Trimma spp.), dwarfgobies ( Eviota spp.), and certain Coryphopterus species, all of which rarely exceed 25 to 30 mm in length ( 29 – 32 ). These repeated reductions in body size make gobies a compelling model for investigating the genetic mechanisms underlying vertebrate miniaturization. Here, we examine patterns of gene expression to gain functional insights into how body size is regulated at the molecular level and to explore the convergent evolution of miniaturization in gobies. We estimate a genome-wide phylogeny, reconstruct ancestral body size, and assemble de novo transcriptomes for six goby species, representing a large and a miniature pair of closely related species in three separate clades. We employ a comparative transcriptomic approach to detect differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between large and miniature species. Since body size is a polygenic trait controlled by multiple genes, common pathways may regulate size and miniaturization specifically. We aim to identify candidate genes involved in these processes and determine whether convergent functional pathways regulate size over deep time. Through a comparative transcriptomic analysis, this study seeks to examine gene expression over a macroevolutionary scale to elucidate the mechanisms of size control in miniaturized vertebrates. Results Repeated Miniaturization Has Evolved Across the Goby Phylogeny. Under a maximum likelihood framework, utilizing a phylogenomic dataset based on 990 exon markers, we estimated a phylogenetic tree for 162 species of gobioid fishes ( Fig. 1 and SI Appendix , Fig. S1 ). Our approach combined newly generated genomic sequences with previously published sequences. Our phylogeny is largely congruent with the most recently published extensive gobioid phylogeny from McCraney et al. ( 33 ) which is based on 23 loci for 827 taxa. Our topology is consistent with previous findings regarding the placement of the four sampled Gobioidei families, with Oxudercidae as sister to Gobiidae, and Eleotridae and Odontobutidae as outgroups. Sequencing and De Novo Transcriptome Assembly of Six Goby Fishes. RNA-seq was performed using muscle and liver samples from three miniature and three large-bodied gobiid fishes, with three biological replicates per species. Illumina NovaSeq paired-end reads produced a total of 841 million raw paired reads, ranging from 20 to 29 million pairs per sample, with an average of 24 million read pairs ( SI Appendix , Appendix 2 on Dryad). Preliminary analyses mapping raw reads back to the closest available reference genome, Boleophthalmus pectinirostris (great blue spotted mudskipper, family Oxudercidae), revealed extremely low mapping percentages for each tissue sample (0.76 to 11.4%; SI Appendix , Appendix 2 ). Instead, we utilized a de novo approach to assemble the transcriptomes for each of the six goby species in our analysis. Filtering of low-quality reads and adapter sequences removed an average of 3.3% of reads per sample ( SI Appendix , Appendix 2 ). Normalization within species retained, on average, 42 million reads (15.6% of input; SI Appendix , Appendix 2 ). All biological replicates were combined to generate a single reference transcriptome assembly for each of the six species. These species-specific assemblies contained an average of 361,435 genes and 486,134 transcripts per species, with an average contig length of 637 bp and N50 of 1,150 bp ( SI Appendix , Table S1 ). Transcriptome reads were mapped back to their respective species-specific assemblies using Salmon v.1.10.1 ( 34 ), with an average of 95.7% of reads mapping back to their references ( SI Appendix , Appendix 2 ). Each of the six transcriptomes was assessed for completeness using the Eukaryota, Vertebrata, and Actinopterygii Benchmarking Universal Single-Copy Orthologs (BUSCO) libraries. Using the Eukaryota library (255 BUSCO groups), assemblies averaged 93.4% completeness with only 1.53% missing. When compared to the Vertebrata (3,354 BUSCO groups) and Actinopterygii (3,640 BUSCO groups) libraries, average completeness was 74.6 and 71.2%, with 14 and 23% missing, respectively ( SI Appendix , Fig. S2 ). Read representation was also high, with Bowtie2 mapping rates averaging 96.71% ( SI Appendix , Table S1 ). Identification of Differentially Expressed Orthologs Between Large and Miniature Gobies. For each of the six species-specific transcriptomes, a majority of genes (89.9 to 92.2%) were assigned to orthogroups ( SI Appendix , Table S2 ). From these orthogroups, OrthoFinder identified 987 one-to-one orthologs present across all six species. Of the 987 orthologs analyzed, 54 (5.47%) were differentially expressed (log fold change &gt; |2|, adjusted FDR P &lt; 0.05), with 30 genes significantly upregulated in miniature species but downregulated in large-bodied species, and 24 showing the opposite pattern—upregulated in large-bodied species but downregulated in miniatures ( Fig. 2 ). DEGs (either upregulated or downregulated) describe the relative difference in gene expression levels, where “upregulated in miniature taxa” indicates higher gene expression compared to larger-bodied taxa, and vice versa. These terms do not imply active evolutionary changes (e.g., ancestral vs. derived states). When visualized as a heatmap, these 54 orthologous genes clustered to reveal distinct expression patterns by size group ( Fig. 2 A ). Additionally, Principal Component Analysis (PCA) of expression levels of differentially expressed orthologs revealed distinct clustering by body size across PC1 (29% of overall variance; Fig. 2 B ). Functional Profiles Differ Between Large and Miniature Gobies. Functional annotation of differentially expressed orthologs using EggNOG-mapper revealed differences in the functional profiles of genes associated with large and miniature goby species. Among the 54 DEGs, we identified several candidates involved in body size evolution and growth regulation. Genes associated with growth inhibiting processes, such as ING2 (Inhibitor of growth family member 2) and CDKN1B (Cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor 1B), were overexpressed in miniature species and underexpressed in large-bodied species ( Fig. 2 C ). In contrast, growth-regulating genes, including TGFB3 (transforming growth factor beta 3), were upregulated in large-bodied species and downregulated in miniature species ( Fig. 2 C ). Among these growth-related genes, we observed evidence of functional convergence on a macroevolutionary scale. In all three miniature species, the growth inhibitors ING2 and CDKN1B were consistently expressed at significantly higher levels compared to large-bodied species ( Fig. 3 ). In contrast, the growth factor gene TGFB3 showed higher expression in all large-bodied species, except in Clade 2, where expression levels were similar between Eviota atriventris and Gobiodon citrinus ( Fig. 3 ). We also identified significant differences in gene ontology (GO) enriched biological processes and molecular functions between large and miniature goby species ( Fig. 4 ). Of the GO terms associated with the DEGs, we identified 932 terms unique to miniature species and 925 unique to large-bodied species, with 707 terms shared between groups. Overrepresentation analysis revealed that genes involved in biological processes such as regulation of the cell cycle, particularly the G2/M transition, were significantly enriched in miniature gobies ( Fig. 4 A ). These include CDK1 (cyclin-dependent kinase 1), CDKN1B , ING2 , and AURKB (aurora kinase B). Additionally, kinase activity and enzyme inhibitor activity were overrepresented molecular functions in miniature species compared to large species ( Fig. 4 B ). In large-bodied species, enriched biological processes were associated with cellular responses to transforming growth factors, tissue development, and positive regulation of the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) cascade, a cell signaling pathway critical for cell growth, proliferation, and differentiation ( Fig. 4 C ). Genes associated with these enriched GO terms include TGFB3 , AGTR1 (angiotensin II receptor type 1), and MKNK2 (MAPK interacting serine/threonine kinase 2). Enriched molecular functions in large-bodied species included growth factor binding and cytokine binding, both involving genes such as DUSP5 (dual specificity phosphatase 5), TGFB3 , MKNK2 , and AGTR1 . Discussion Goby Body Size Convergence Reflects Distinct Gene Functional Profiles. Using comparative transcriptomics across three goby clades with repeated instances of miniaturization, we identified functional differences in gene expression between miniature and large-bodied species. Biological processes and molecular functions related to cell proliferation and growth emerged as a key mechanism regulating body size. Genes upregulated in miniature species possess a functional profile associated with tighter control over cell cycle regulation and growth inhibition. In contrast, upregulated genes in large-bodied species promote cell growth, proliferation, and differentiation. The most recent common ancestor of these species dates to the Eocene, approximately 49.8 Ma ( 35 ), suggesting that these pathways to size regulation are functionally conserved across macroevolutionary timescales. Given that body size is a highly polygenic trait, multiple pathways likely contribute to size regulation, with control over cell proliferation being one of several mechanisms. This study represents a comparative transcriptomic analysis of size control in nonmodel vertebrates, offering insights into the genetic basis of body size evolution. Genes Inhibiting Cell Proliferation Characterize Miniature Gobies. Differences in cell size, and perhaps more importantly cell number, play a pivotal role in regulating animal body size ( 36 , 37 ). Cell number is tightly controlled through coordinated cell growth, proliferation, and death (apoptosis), but it is less known how these processes are regulated ( 37 ). Among the key regulators are Cyclin-dependent protein kinases (CDKs) and their inhibitors. CDKs are crucial for cell-cycle progression, becoming cyclically activated to trigger cell-cycle phases ( 36 ). For example, CDK1 plays a key role in cell division during the G2/M transition of the cell cycle, which ensures cells have attained a sufficient size before entering mitosis ( 38 , 39 ). CDKs are themselves regulated by inhibitors, such as CDKN1B ( 40 ), which blocks cell division at the G1/S checkpoint and limits overall proliferation, acting as a negative regulator of body size ( 37 , 41 ). Experimental evidence supports this role: CDKN1B gene knockout mice exhibit ~30% increased body size due to elevated proliferation rates and increased cell numbers across organs ( 42 – 44 ). In this study, both CDK1 and CDKN1B were upregulated in miniature species ( Fig. 2 ), and both linked to enriched GO terms, including negative and positive regulation of the cell cycle, and regulation of mitotic cell cycle phase transitions ( Fig. 4 ). Although simultaneous upregulation of both CDKN1B (a cell cycle inhibitor) and CDK1 (a cell cycle progressor) in miniature species may seem counterintuitive, we hypothesize that the overexpression of CDK1 might compensate for the earlier cell cycle arrest imposed by CDKN1B. In this scenario, cells that pass the G1/S checkpoint can proceed to mitosis, maintaining essential proliferation and helping to offset a reduced overall rate of cell division. This compensatory mechanism could reflect a broader strategy in miniature species, allowing continued cell cycle progression despite globally reduced proliferation rates, ultimately contributing to reduced body size. However, further functional studies are needed to confirm this hypothesis. Growth-Promoting Genes Are Underexpressed in Miniature Species. Beyond cell proliferation, genes regulating growth and development play a critical role in size control. TGFB3 , a member of the transforming growth factor-beta superfamily, is a multifunctional cytokine regulating cell development, proliferation, and differentiation ( 45 , 46 ). TGFB3 is essential for cardiovascular development ( 47 ), wound healing ( 48 ), and growth in chickens ( 49 ). Additionally, certain TGF-beta signaling pathways regulate body size and development in the nematode Caenorhabditis elegans ( 50 – 52 ). Mutations leading to loss of TGFB3 activity can lead to syndromes characterized by reduced muscle mass and growth retardation ( 53 ). Here, TGFB3 was consistently upregulated in large-bodied gobies and downregulated in miniature species, implicating it in growth-related processes. In large-bodied gobies, TGFB3 overexpression was associated with enriched biological processes and molecular functions, including growth factor binding, regulation of MAPK cascade, endopeptidase activity, and biomineral tissue development. Endopeptidases, such as matrix metalloproteinases, activate TGF-betas to facilitate tissue remodeling ( 54 ), while the MAPK cascade drives cell proliferation and differentiation ( 55 ). Conversely, the growth inhibitor ING2 was consistently upregulated in all miniature species. As a member of the inhibitor of growth (ING) gene family, ING2 regulates cell proliferation, apoptosis, and mediates TGF-β signaling to promote cell cycle arrest ( 56 , 57 ). Additionally, ING2 is a candidate tumor suppressor: loss of this gene results in rapid cell cycle progression and increased cell division ( 58 ). The overexpression of ING2 in miniature species suggests that inhibition of cell proliferation during the mitotic cell cycle progression is a key mechanism controlling body size. Decreased Cell Proliferation Rates and the Morphological Consequences of Miniaturization. Miniaturization entails structural and functional consequences, including morphological reduction and loss ( 12 ). Among vertebrates, body size reduction often imposes constraints on the skeletal system. Miniaturized frogs, salamanders, and the world’s smallest fish, Paedocypris , exhibit loss of cranial elements and other bones ( 13 , 20 , 59 ). In gobies, morphological simplification is a common feature attributed to body size reduction ( 60 , 61 ). Miniature species often exhibit reduced or absent features, including loss of the cephalic sensory canal pores in Trimma ( 62 ), interopercular ligaments in Eviota and Sueviota ( 32 ), and complete absence of body scales and cephalic pores in Trimmatom nanus, formerly the smallest known vertebrate ( 31 , 63 ). The three miniature taxa in this study show morphological reductions consistent with simplification rather than proportional dwarfism. Coryphopterus personatus is one of the smallest species of Coryphopterus , and known morphological reductions and losses include the absence of a pelvic frenum connecting the pelvic spines and a reduced fifth pelvic ray (relative to the fourth ray) ( 64 ). The pygmygobies ( Trimma spp., including T . hollemani ) are distinguished from other goby genera on the basis of their small body size (&lt;30 mm SL), reduced number of dorsal and anal fin rays, and complete lack of cephalic sensory canals and pores ( 65 ). Last, the dwarfgobies of the genus Eviota (including E . atriventris ) are characterized by various reductions/losses including separate pelvic fins which lack a connecting frenum and the total absence of scales on the head, nape, and pectoral fin base ( 29 ). Overall, a comprehensive review of the anatomical characters which differ between miniaturized gobies and larger-bodied congeners is lacking. The downregulation of genes associated with cell proliferation may contribute to the reduced skeletal elemental and sensory structures in Trimma and Eviota , though experimental studies are needed to confirm these links. Currently, no data exist about cell size and numbers in gobies to directly link how these processes correlate to their miniaturized phenotype. However, given the transcriptomic evidence implicating genes regulating cell proliferation, decreased cell proliferation rates may contribute to the observed morphological reductions in these taxa. For instance, digit reduction in amphibians is directly correlated with body size reduction and paedomorphosis ( 66 ), and has been attributed to slowed cell proliferation during development ( 67 ). Here, the overexpression of cell proliferation inhibitors in miniature gobies ( Fig. 4 ) provides further evidence that reduced proliferation rates contribute to the morphological reductions and losses commonly associated with extreme body size reduction. Conclusions. This study presents a comparative transcriptomic analysis of miniature goby fishes, a clade that includes some of the smallest vertebrates on Earth. We identified distinct gene expression profiles between large-bodied and miniature species, underscoring the complex balance between cell growth and proliferation in regulating body size. Inhibition of cell proliferation emerged as a key mechanism in the evolution of miniaturization. We pinpointed specific genes involved in body size evolution, many previously associated with growth, proliferation, and development, identifying them as promising candidates for future studies of body size control. Despite the polygenic nature of body size, parallel changes in gene expression across both large and miniature gobies ( Fig. 3 ) point to macroevolutionary convergence in body size evolution over deep timescales. Together, these findings offer insights into the genetic basis of miniaturization and highlight the value of studying nonmodel organisms to uncover the fundamental processes governing vertebrate size evolution. Materials and Methods Sampling and Sequencing. For this study, we prioritized representatives from known miniaturized genera (e.g., Coryphopterus , Eviota , and Trimma ) and their closest larger-bodied relatives based on the gobiid phylogeny from McCraney et al. ( 33 ). As these genera lack sufficiently larger-bodied congeners, we chose cross-generic comparisons. For example, for Eviota , the closest large-bodied genus is Gobiodon , whose species are on average two times larger than Eviota species ( 31 ). Tissue availability for RNA-seq was a constraint, as it was not feasible to obtain specimens via fieldwork; thus, we sourced specimens from the aquarium trade, selecting the largest Gobiodon species and the smallest Eviota species available. This process was repeated for the two other miniature groups of interest. By focusing on one-to-one orthologs shared across taxa, we ensured comparability despite phylogenetic distance. While nonsister comparisons may complicate ancestral state reconstruction, detecting conserved gene expression patterns across these clades strengthens the evidence for shared mechanisms of miniaturization. We ultimately sourced three individuals per species from the aquarium trade, representing three goby clades that each contain both miniature and large-bodied forms: Amblyeleotris guttata and Coryphopterus personatus (Clade 1), Gobiodon citrinus and Eviota atriventris (Clade 2), and Valenciennea puellaris and Trimma hollemani (Clade 3; Fig. 1 ). Miniaturization in fishes lacks a consensus size cutoff; Weitzman and Vari ( 27 ) proposed 26 mm standard length (SL) based on South American freshwater fishes. While Eviota atriventris , attaining only 17 mm SL ( 31 ) falls well below this threshold, Trimma hollemani and Coryphopterus personatus slightly exceed it, reaching 28 mm SL and 40 mm TL (estimated 32 mm SL), respectively ( 31 ). However, Goatley and Tornabene ( 68 ) argue this threshold is somewhat arbitrary and additional cutoffs for miniaturization may apply. In coral reef fish communities, ecological shifts occur at 43 mm and 46 mm TL, linked to mortality rates and benthic habitat use ( 69 , 70 ). Because of the coral reef association of our miniature species, we adopted a conservative threshold of &lt;35 mm SL for miniaturization. Fishes were held for 24 to 48 h in aquaria before euthanasia with MS-222, following IACUC guidelines (approval #R19-018) in accordance with the American Veterinary Medical Association guidelines for the euthanasia of animals: 2020 edition, section S6.1.1. To minimize gene expression variation due to developmental differences, we sampled only adult fishes. Although this approach may not detect all patterns that are present throughout ontogeny, we were limited to sampling only adult specimens given the extreme difficulty in obtaining fresh tissue samples from larval or juvenile gobies. These species are not easily bred in captivity and wild caught larvae are notoriously difficult to identify to species or even genus level. However, adult expression data are still useful and informative given that the individuals sampled are not yet at their maximum adult lengths and still growing (with the exception of one Eviota atriventris individual; SI Appendix , Table S3 ). All individuals were sampled between 11 am and 4 pm to reduce circadian rhythm effects. Individuals were sampled in eight batches according to their availability from the aquarium trade ( SI Appendix , Appendix 2 ). We sampled tissues from the liver and skeletal muscle, key for growth and metabolism ( 71 ). Tissues were extracted, flash frozen in liquid nitrogen and then stored at −80 °C. Tissue samples were shipped to Novogene ( https://www.novogene.com/us-en/ ) for RNA extraction and reference-based sequencing. Total mRNA was isolated using the poly-A enrichment method, where poly-T oligo-attached magnetic beads are used to pull down the polyadenylated poly-A tail of mRNA. RNA Integrity Numbers (RIN) were calculated, and only samples with RIN &gt; 4.0 were used. One sample ( Trimma hollemani liver) failed library prep and was excluded, leaving 35 remaining tissue samples. Sequencing was conducted on an Illumina NovaSeq platform using 150 bp paired-end reads. Raw reads were filtered for quality and adapter contamination and deposited in the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) Sequence Read Archive (SRA) under BioProject PRJNA1088833. After quality control, sequences were aligned to the closest available reference genome, Boleophthalmus pectinirostris (NCBI), the great blue-spotted mudskipper (belonging to the sister family Oxudercidae) using HISAT2 v. 2.0.5. Because of the low percentage of mapped reads to the reference genome ( SI Appendix , Appendix 2 ), likely due to phylogenetic distance, we performed de novo assembly with Trinity for downstream analyses (see De Novo Transcriptome Assembly below). Phylogenomic Estimation. We generated genomic data from tissue samples obtained from museum specimens for 56 Gobioidei species. DNA was extracted and shipped to Arbor Biosciences for library preparation and target enrichment. Libraries for all samples were processed at the sequencing facilities at the University of Chicago. Sequencing of pair end 100 bp reads was completed on a HiSeq 4000 with a total of 192 samples multiplexed per lane, including samples for other projects not listed here. Raw reads are deposited to NCBI Sequence Read Archive under BioProject number PRJNA1088833. Target capture probes targeted 1,103 single-copy nuclear exon markers for teleost fishes using the “Backbone 2” bait set ( 72 ). To expand our taxonomic scope, we mined these exon markers from 92 NCBI genomes and transcriptomes, the six species-specific de novo transcriptome assemblies, and eight previously published genomes and transcriptomes, using the Hughes et al. ( 73 ) pipeline. Exons were aligned using MACSE v. 2.03 ( 74 ) after removing nonhomologous fragments with the -trimNonHomologousFragments flag. Alignments were further cleaned for single-taxon insertions caused by assembly errors. We removed edges containing more than 60% gaps and short sequences below 50% of the alignment length using the custom “AlignmentCleanerCodons.py” script ( 73 ). An initial phylogeny was estimated with FastTree v. 2.1.1 ( 75 ) to assess quality. We removed exons with low pairwise identity (&lt;80%), leaving a total of 990 exon markers for phylogenomic inference. The final dataset included 162 Gobioidei species (129 Gobiidae and 33 outgroup taxa from Oxudercidae, Eleotridae, and Odontobutidae). We estimated the final Gobioidei phylogeny using RAxML-NG v. 1.1.0 ( 76 ), using by-codon partitioning and 1,000 bootstrap replicates to assess branch support. Body Size Data. We recorded SL and TL data from FishBase ( 31 ) for all species in the phylogeny. For six species lacking FishBase data ( Egglestonichthys bombylios, Kraemeria bryani, Lophogobius cristulatus, Lythrypnus crocodilus, Sicydium gymnogaster, Rhinogobius lindbergi ), lengths were sourced from published literature. Because TL was more commonly available than SL, we derived a linear relationship between TL and SL based on measurements from aquarium trade specimens ( Sampling and Sequencing ), yielding a strong correlation (R 2 = 0.995). We used the resulting equation (TL = 1.257 *SL – 0.0818 cm) to calculate TL for the missing FishBase species, ensuring all taxa in our dataset have an associated TL for use in downstream analysis ( SI Appendix , Fig. S3 ). To infer body size across the Gobioidei phylogeny, we used a maximum likelihood ancestral character state reconstruction for TL using the “fastAnc” function in the phytools package ( 77 ) in R v. 4.2.3 ( 78 ). De Novo Transcriptome Assembly. Raw read quality was assessed with FastQC v. 0.11.5 ( 79 ). Low-quality reads and adapters were removed with Trimmomatic v. 0.38 ( 80 ) using the following parameters: ILLUMINACLIP:2:30:10, LEADING:5, TRAILING:5, SLIDINGWINDOW:4:15, MINLEN:31. Paired reads were retained and reassessed with FastQC. Species-specific de novo assemblies were generated for each goby species using Trinity v. 2.15.1 ( 81 ), with read normalization (k-mer maximum coverage 200; –normalize_reads) and strand-specific settings (–SS_lib_type RF). Transcripts were indexed using Salmon v. 1.10.1 ( 34 ) and resulting reads were mapped back to their respective assembly and transcript abundances using the “salmon quant” command with “-l A -p 8 --validateMappings –gcBias” options. The percentage of reads that mapped back to the respective species-specific assembly were recorded ( SI Appendix , Appendix 2 ). Transcriptome completeness was evaluated with BUSCO v. 5.2.2 ( 82 ) using the Eukaryota, Vertebrata, and the Actinopterygii (ray-finned fishes) databases as references ( SI Appendix , Fig. S2 ). We used BowTie2 v. 2.3.4.2 ( 83 ) to assess assembly quality in terms of read representation where normalized reads were mapped back onto the transcriptome. High overall alignment rates (&gt;90%) were considered optimal. Assembly summary statistics, including percent GC, N50, and average contig length were calculated using the “TrinityStats.pl” script ( SI Appendix , Table S1 ). Peptide open read frames (ORFs) were predicted using TransDecoder v. 5.7.1 ( 84 ) retaining the longest ORF (&gt;100 amino acids). Redundancy was reduced by clustering highly similar peptide sequences with CD-HIT v. 4.8.1 ( 85 ) at a 0.995 similarity threshold. Orthologs were identified with OrthoFinder v. 2.5.4 ( 86 ) by initially running the “-S blast -M msa -a 10” option using the Basic Local Alignment Search Tool (BLAST) searches separately ( 87 ). Pairwise query-database protein BLAST searches were then run with blastp using the options “outfmt 6 -evalue 0.001” ( 88 ). OrthoFinder summary results, including number and percentage of genes per orthogroup, were recorded ( SI Appendix , Table S2 ). One-to-one orthologs shared across all species were extracted for downstream analyses. Differential Gene Expression Analyses. Gene expression of the one-to-one orthologs was quantified from the Salmon abundances using the “abundance_estimates_to_matrix.pl” script in Trinity, generating a counts matrix for differential expression analysis. Differential expression between large-bodied and miniature species was assessed using the DESeq2 method ( 89 ) in R . This method uses the unnormalized counts matrix as input and detects DEGs (orthologs) by fitting a generalized linear model of raw gene counts, following a negative binomial (Gamma-Poisson) distribution ( 89 ). Results, including log2 fold changes (LFC), Wald test P -values, and Benjamini and Hochberg adjusted P -values of the false discovery rate (FDR) for each differentially expressed one-to-one ortholog were generated using the “results” function ( SI Appendix , Appendix 3 on Dryad). One-to-one orthologs were considered differentially expressed based on the following cutoff: FDR-adjusted P -values &lt; 0.05 and LFC &gt; |2|. To visualize differentially expressed orthologs between large-bodied and miniature species, we applied a variance-stabilizing transformation (VST) to the normalized count data using the “vst” function in the DESeq2 R package and generated a hierarchical clustering heatmap using the “pheatmap” function. We visualized differentially expressed orthologs between large and miniature species as a volcano plot, where the LFC is plotted against log10 normalized P -values to depict the magnitude and significance of differential expression. Functional Annotation and Enrichment. We used EggNOG-mapper v2.1.12 ( 90 ) with DIAMOND-based homology searches ( 91 ) to functionally annotate the differentially expressed orthologs identified with DESeq2 . Functional descriptors, including GO terms and gene names, were recorded ( SI Appendix , Appendix 3 ). GO terms were categorized into two groups: those exclusively found in miniature species and those exclusively found in large-bodied species. To identify overrepresented biological processes and molecular functions among the GO terms for large versus miniature species, we performed enrichment analysis with the “enrichGO” function in the R package clusterProfiler ( 92 ) using the zebrafish ( Danio rerio ) annotation database, a P -value and q-value cutoff of 0.05, and the Benjamini–Hochberg multiple testing correction. We visualized the top 10 enriched GO terms with the ‘dotplot’ function in the enrichplot package. Supplementary Material Appendix 01 (PDF)
