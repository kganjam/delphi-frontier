1. Introduction With the rapid development of industry, a large number of synthetic compounds are released into the environment during their production and use, posing serious threats to both ecosystems and human health. Among them, a class of substances that can interfere with the normal functions of the endocrine system including hormone synthesis, secretion, transport, receptor recognition, and feedback regulation, are defined as environmental endocrine disruptors (EEDs) [ 1 ]. Extensive surveys have revealed the frequent detection of EEDs in environmental media such as soil, water, and sediments across various countries and regions [ 2 ]. The environmental occurrence levels of typical EEDs in surface water, wastewater and sediment are summarized in Table A1 . Human exposure to these compounds is also widespread. Over the past two decades, typical EEDs including pesticides and plasticizers have been repeatedly detected in human samples such as serum, urine, placental tissue, and breast milk [ 3 ]. Given the pivotal role of the endocrine system in maintaining homeostasis and regulating reproduction, development, and behavior, EEDs can disrupt the synthesis and metabolism of endogenous hormones, either activating or suppressing endocrine system functions, subsequently resulting in neurotoxicity, immunotoxicity, and reproductive toxicity across multiple systems. Among the various toxic effects of EEDs, genotoxicity has garnered particular attention, as it is directly linked to mutagenicity, teratogenicity, and even the development of certain cancers [ 4 ]. Genotoxicity refers to the damage caused by chemical substances interacting with DNA or other cellular targets responsible for maintaining the integrity of genetic material, such as the formation of DNA adducts, strand breaks, point mutations, and changes in the structure and number of chromosomes [ 5 ], which are also regarded as direct genotoxic endpoints representing measurable alterations to genetic material itself. By contrast, oxidative stress, altered gene expression, and cell apoptosis are regarded as indirect effects, which can mediate or promote the occurrence and hazard of genotoxicity. In the absence of carcinogenicity data, genotoxicity tests are often employed to predict the potential carcinogenic risks of chemical substances [ 6 ]. Therefore, research on the genotoxicity of EEDs has always been a hot topic in the field of environmental toxicology. For clarity, key relevant toxicological endpoints are distinguished. DNA damage refers to molecular-level lesions of DNA molecules. Genotoxicity describes the capability of a chemical to induce DNA- or genome-level damage. If unrepaired DNA damage becomes stably heritable sequence alterations, it manifests as mutagenicity. Subsequent accumulation of such mutations may eventually contribute to carcinogenicity. By contrast, oxidative stress, DNA-damage-response (DDR) activation and apoptosis represent intermediate cellular stress-response events; they do not equate to genotoxicity or DNA damage, though they can be closely linked to these adverse outcomes. Based on the above background, this review summarizes the experimental findings on the genotoxicity of EEDs over the past 15 years. It systematically categorizes the genotoxic effects induced by typical EEDs in animals or humans and delves into their underlying mechanisms of action. The aim of this review is to provide a comprehensive understanding of EED-induced genotoxicity and a new perspective and useful reference for future in-depth investigations into the genotoxicity mechanisms. 2. Methodology This is a narrative review; thus, no PRISMA flow diagram is required. Databases, search terms, time frame, and study eligibility criteria are described below. To systematically retrieve relevant literature, this study conducted a comprehensive search across the Web of Science, ScienceDirect, and China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI) databases using the following keywords: “genotoxicity”, “environmental endocrine disruptors”, “chromosomal aberration”, “DNA damage”, “gene mutations”. The search time frame was set from 2011 to 2026. The retrieved literature covered a broad range of disciplines, including environmental chemistry, toxicology, food science and technology, environmental science, genetic toxicology, environmental molecular biology, ecotoxicology, cell biology, aquatic biology, and agronomy. The initial database search yielded 335 records. After deduplication, preliminary screening based on titles and abstracts was performed, and 216 records were selected for full-text assessment. Inclusion criteria: Original research articles investigating genotoxic effects, underlying molecular mechanisms, or genotoxicity-related detection methods for EEDs; Studies performed using in vivo animal models or in vitro cell models; Publications with publicly accessible full-text resources. Exclusion criteria: Reviews without original experimental evidence; Papers that only described endocrine-disrupting phenotypes without genotoxicity-related endpoints; Conference abstracts, proceedings, and manuscripts without publicly available full-text. Following full-text evaluation, a total of 111 publications met the objectives of this narrative review and were finally included for analysis. 3. Types of EEDs In this review, substances are classified as environmental endocrine disruptors (EEDs) following the authoritative WHO-UNEP scientific assessment framework. Chemicals are defined as EEDs if they can interfere with hormone synthesis, secretion, transport, receptor recognition, or feedback regulation of the endocrine system. According to the results of a joint scientific assessment by the WHO and UNEP, close to 800 chemicals are known or suspected to interfere with endocrine-related molecular targets [ 7 ]. Based on their primary source pathways, they can be broadly classified into five major categories: industrial sources, agricultural sources, consumer product sources, combustion and by-product sources, and emerging environmental pollutants [ 3 ]. Industrial sources primarily encompass plastic raw materials and plasticizers, as well as flame retardants and insulating materials. Bisphenol compounds serve as the fundamental building blocks for polycarbonate plastics and epoxy resins, among which bisphenol A (BPA) is the most extensively produced and widely applied [ 8 ]. Owing to its confirmed estrogenic activity and toxicity, BPA has been progressively replaced by its structural analogs in recent years, including bisphenol F (BPF), bisphenol S (BPS), bisphenol AF (BPAF), bisphenol B (BPB), bisphenol Z (BPZ), bisphenol AP (BPAP), and others [ 9 , 10 ]. Phthalate esters (PAEs) represent the most commonly used plasticizers and are broadly incorporated into polyvinyl chloride (PVC) products. The major representatives include di(2-ethylhexyl) phthalate (DEHP), dibutyl phthalate (DBP), benzyl butyl phthalate (BBP), dimethyl phthalate (DMP), and diethyl phthalate (DEP) and the next-generation plasticizer diisononyl cyclohexane-1,2-dicarboxylate (DINCH) [ 11 , 12 ]. As for insulating and flame-retardant materials, they mainly consist of polychlorinated biphenyls (PCBs), which were historically used as insulating oils in power capacitors and transformers, alongside polybrominated biphenyls (PBDEs) [ 13 ]. Various chemical inputs used in agricultural production constitute a significant source of EEDs. These can be broadly categorized into four groups. The first one is organochlorine pesticides (OCPs) and typical representatives include dichlorodiphenyltrichloroethane (DDT), atrazine, and hexachlorocyclohexane (HCH) [ 14 ]. Owing to their lipophilicity and toxicity, most of these compounds have been banned [ 15 ]. However, they remain detectable in soil and water due to their persistence. The second one is organophosphorus pesticides that are extensively used as substitutes of OCPs and representative compounds include dimethoate, malathion, and acephate [ 16 ]. The third one is pyrethroid insecticides [ 17 ]. Although classified as highly effective and of low acute toxicity, prolonged exposure may still pose potential genotoxic risks. The last one is herbicides and major representatives include linuron, nitrofen, and prometryn. Owing to their large-scale application, they induce notable genotoxic effects on aquatic and soil organisms [ 18 , 19 ]. The production and use of everyday consumer items also release various EEDs into the environment. Based on their functions and chemical properties, these compounds can be grouped into the following three categories. The first one is degradation products of detergents and surfactants. The main compounds such as nonylphenol and octylphenol are environmental breakdown products of alkylphenol polyethoxylate surfactants commonly used in detergents [ 20 ]. The second one is preservatives and bactericides. These are widely added to cosmetics, skin care products, toothpastes, and food preservation materials and major examples include triclosan (TCS), parabens, and benzalkonium chloride [ 21 ]. The third one is pharmaceuticals and personal care products (PPCPs), which include natural or synthetic hormones like 17β-estradiol and 17α-methyltestosterone as well as commonly used drugs such as carbamazepine and diclofenac [ 22 ]. Carbamazepine is included here based on reported potential endocrine-perturbing properties from aquatic toxicological assays with Daphnia magna , while its definite endocrine-disrupting potency has not been fully established [ 23 ]. Combustion and by-product sources primarily encompass pollutants generated directly during combustion processes, as well as by-products formed through subsequent oxidation or disinfection reactions. It can be further divided into the following three subcategories. The first one is plastic incineration products. The incineration of chlorine-containing plastic materials releases persistent organic pollutants (POPs), including dioxins and furans [ 2 ]. The second one is drinking water disinfection by-products (DBPs). During chlorination, the reaction between chlorine and organic matter in water generates a variety of DBPs, such as haloacetic acids, halobenzoquinones and chloroform. These compounds commonly possess certain genotoxic properties [ 24 ]. Among them, formaldehyde has been suggested to exert potential endocrine-relevant effects based on human placental trophoblast experimental observations, yet supporting evidence remains limited [ 25 ]. The third one is tire wear oxidation products. The antioxidant N-(1,3-dimethylbutyl)-N′-phenyl-p-phenylenediamine (6PPD) used in tire rubber can be transformed by ozonation into 6PPD-quinone (6PPD-Q). This transformation product has been shown to exhibit potent genotoxicity in aquatic organisms and has thus attracted considerable research attention in recent years [ 26 ]. In addition, in recent years, a number of emerging contaminants of great concern have been identified as novel EEDs with potent endocrine-disrupting and genotoxic properties. These include per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS), novel organophosphate flame retardants (OPFRs), as well as microplastics and nanoplastics (MPs/NPs). Notably, polymer particles of MPs/NPs themselves are not classified as endocrine-disrupting chemicals; their endocrine-disrupting and genotoxic risks mainly stem from incorporated or sorbed EED-relevant additives (e.g., bisphenols, phthalates, PBDEs), leachable constituents, and particle-provoked inflammatory responses [ 27 ]. PFAS are a class of synthetic fluorinated organic compounds, with typical representatives such as perfluorooctanoic acid (PFOA), perfluorooctane sulfonate (PFOS), and their substitutes [ 28 ]. Novel OPFRs, such as tris(1-chloro-2-propyl) phosphate (TCPP), tris(2-chloroethyl) phosphate (TCEP), and tris(1,3-dichloro-2-propyl) phosphate (TDCPP), have been increasingly employed as substitutes for traditional BPDEs in furniture, electronic products, and building materials in recent years [ 29 ]. These compounds are reported to exhibit potential endocrine-disrupting activity, though available evidence remains inconsistent across studies [ 30 ]. Recent studies have confirmed the genotoxic and potential carcinogenic risks of some of these substances, rendering them priority targets for current research in environmental genotoxicity [ 31 , 32 ]. 4. Genotoxicity Testing Methods for EEDs Genotoxicity endpoints are generally classified into three major categories: gene mutations, chromosomal and genomic aberrations, and DNA damage [ 33 , 34 ]. Accordingly, the testing methods adopted vary depending on the specific endpoints of interest. Comet assay, also known as single-cell gel electrophoresis (SCGE), was originally developed by Östling and Johanson in 1984 as a neutral single-cell gel electrophoresis technique, and later refined into the widely adopted alkaline comet assay by Singh et al. in 1988 [ 35 ]. It is a rapid technique for detecting DNA damage at the single-cell level. The principle of the comet assay involves embedding cells in agarose gel, lysing the cells to remove membranes and proteins while retaining supercoiled DNA, and then subjecting the slides to alkaline or neutral conditions to unwind the DNA. Upon electrophoresis, fragmented DNA from damaged cells, due to its smaller molecular weight, migrates from the nucleus toward the anode, forming a “comet-like” tail, whereas undamaged cells, with intact high-molecular-weight DNA, remain as round fluorescent foci [ 36 ]. By measuring parameters such as tail length and tail DNA content, the extent of single- and double-strand breaks can be quantitatively assessed. More severe damage results in longer tails with higher fluorescence intensity [ 37 ]. Based on the pH of the electrophoresis buffer, the assay is divided into the alkaline comet assay and the neutral comet assay [ 35 ]. The former is more sensitive and can detect both single- and double-strand breaks as well as alkali-labile sites, and is therefore the most widely used version and the latter primarily detects double-strand breaks [ 36 ]. Owing to its sensitivity, versatility, and accuracy, it has been widely applied in genotoxicity testing, human biomonitoring, molecular epidemiology, ecotoxicological studies, and fundamental research on DNA damage and repair [ 38 , 39 ]. When testing endocrine-disrupting pollutants, this assay offers high sensitivity toward single- and double-strand DNA breaks in fish erythrocytes and cultured cells. The Ames test is known as the bacterial reverse mutation assay) was established in 1975 and remains a classic in vitro method for detecting mutagenicity using microbial gene mutation [ 40 ]. Its core principle relies on histidine-auxotrophic strains of Salmonella typhimurium , which cannot grow on media lacking histidine. When a test chemical is mutagenic, it induces reverse mutations in the defective strains, restoring them to the wild-type (histidine-prototrophic) phenotype, thereby enabling colony formation on selective medium. The number of colonies directly reflects the mutagenic potential of the test substance. To mimic mammalian metabolic activation, rat liver microsomal fraction (S9) is typically added to detect promutagens that require metabolic conversion [ 41 ]. The Ames test is rapid, simple, and widely employed for initial genotoxicity screening of pesticides, food additives, and environmental pollutants, and is particularly suitable for evaluating the combined mutagenic effects of complex mixtures [ 6 ]. In EED-related toxicological research, this assay is widely applied for high-throughput preliminary mutagenicity screening of candidate endocrine-disrupting chemicals; nevertheless, it cannot recapitulate eukaryotic cellular or in vivo physiological conditions. Error-corrected sequencing is an innovative technology based on next-generation sequencing (NGS) [ 42 ]. Its core principle involves attaching unique molecular identifiers to each original DNA molecule, allowing the identification and correction of random errors introduced during PCR amplification or sequencing by comparing multiple reads derived from the same original molecule. This enables ultra-sensitive detection of low-frequency mutations. In contrast to the traditional Ames test, which relies on phenotypic reversion in auxotrophic bacteria and primarily detects point mutations with limited sensitivity, error-corrected sequencing can directly identify various types of low-frequency mutations across the whole genome. Therefore, it is especially suitable for precise detection of subtle mutations induced by low-dose EED exposure, combined pollution, and transgenerational effects, offering higher resolution and richer information than the Ames test for environmental genotoxicity assessment. This cutting-edge sequencing tool enables detection of low-frequency somatic mutations induced by EED exposure that escape conventional genotoxicity assays. Even so, high experimental cost and complicated bioinformatic workflows limit its application for large-sample-size preliminary screening. The micronucleus test, developed independently by Heddle and Schmid in the 1970s, is a classic tool for genotoxicity screening of chemicals and particularly effective for detecting clastogens and aneugens [ 43 ]. The in vitro micronucleus test is widely used to assess chromosome damage caused by chemicals. The underlying principle is that during mitosis, if a test substance causes chromosome breakage or spindle dysfunction, chromosome fragments or entire lagging chromosomes fail to be incorporated into daughter nuclei at telophase and instead form one or more small, secondary nuclei in the cytoplasm, termed micronuclei [ 43 ]. The micronucleus assay is frequently adopted to evaluate chromosome damage induced by EEDs such as bisphenols and phthalates in cultured cells and experimental fish; nevertheless, it cannot directly identify primary DNA-strand-break lesions. Combined γH2AX and DDR analysis enables simultaneous assessment of DNA double-strand break (DSB) signaling and downstream repair pathway activation [ 44 ]. The principle is that upon DSB formation, histone H2AX is rapidly phosphorylated at Ser-139 (forming γH2AX) at the break sites, serving as a sensitive marker of DSBs. Concurrently, a series of DDR proteins (e.g., ATM, 53BP1, BRCA1) are recruited to the damage sites, initiating downstream repair cascades [ 45 ]. Simultaneously detecting γH2AX foci and the activation/recruitment of DDR proteins can not only identify the presence and quantity of DSBs but also evaluate the activation status of repair pathways, thus distinguishing between “simple damage” and “damage-initiated repair” [ 31 ]. Compared with measuring γH2AX alone, which only reflects DNA damage occurrence without indicating subsequent repair capacity, the combined approach provides a more comprehensive evaluation of the impact of compounds on the entire DNA damage-repair process. Consequently, this combined strategy has been extensively applied in mechanistic studies of EED-induced genotoxicity and is regarded as one of the gold-standard methods for assessing DNA damage and genotoxicity risk [ 14 , 46 ]. For cellular EED toxicology work, this integrated strategy goes beyond simple DNA double-strand break marking and characterizes the full activation status of DNA-damage-response signalling networks. Still, it is largely restricted to in vitro cell systems and difficult to implement directly within intact animal tissues. With the continuous advancement and widespread adoption of high-throughput sequencing and high-resolution mass spectrometry technologies, multi-omics integration analysis techniques rapidly matured and were widely adopted after 2015 [ 47 ]. These systematic analytical approaches utilize platforms such as whole-genome sequencing, transcriptome sequencing, proteome sequencing, and liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) to comprehensively capture changes in the abundance, composition, and modifications of DNA, RNA, proteins, and metabolites across multiple molecular layers. Multi-omics strategies can efficiently screen for specific biomarkers of genotoxicity and uncover cryptic genetic damage and perturbed pathways that traditional endpoint assays (e.g., gene mutation or chromosomal aberration tests) may miss [ 48 ]. These multi-layer profiling techniques help reveal complex molecular signatures triggered by EED insult. A major caveat is the difficulty in discriminating bona fide direct genotoxic outcomes from secondary molecular changes driven by general cellular stress. In short, these genotoxic assays have their respective strengths and drawbacks for EED assessment. Ames test is suitable for preliminary mutagenicity screening. Micronucleus assays reflect chromosome-level damage. The comet assay is sensitive to DNA-strand breaks but requires strict standardization. γ-H2AX-based tools mark double-strand-break signals at the cellular level. Advanced sequencing and multi-omics workflows provide high-resolution molecular profiling, yet they are often cost-intensive or cannot readily separate direct genotoxicity from indirect stress-related responses. Combining several complementary assays is preferred to obtain robust genotoxicity evidence for EEDs. In Vivo and In Vitro Genotoxicity Studies of EEDs 5.1. In Vivo Studies Using Animal Models EEDs can directly induce DNA damage, elicit genotoxic effects, and even increase carcinogenic risk following long-term exposure [ 33 ]. Figure 1 summarizes recent genotoxicity studies of EEDs across various organisms. The specific information about exposure concentrations, exposure conditions, effect endpoints, experimental methods, and key findings can be found in Table S1 . Rats and mice are the most commonly used mammalian models in toxicology [ 47 , 60 ]. Prethroid insecticide imiprothrin exposure can induce DNA damage and chromosomal aberration in rats using the comet assay and chromosomal aberration test [ 17 ]. Liao et al. [ 61 ] employed the comet assay to evaluate DNA damage in normal and OVA-immunized mice exposed to toluene, and found that toluene exposure induced oxidative DNA damage and elicited genotoxicity in several brain regions. Notably, the hippocampus of control mice exhibited higher susceptibility to toluene-induced genotoxicity compared to OVA-immunized mice, suggesting that immune status may influence the susceptibility to chemical-induced neurogenotoxic effects. As an ecologically relevant macroinvertebrate that is abundant in aquatic ecosystems, the midge Chironomus has been adopted as a standard test organism for evaluating toxicity of EEDs in international standardized protocols. Martínez-Paz et al. [ 62 ] used the comet assay to evaluate the genotoxicity of five EEDs including BPA, nonylphenol (NP), pentachlorophenol (PCP), tributyltin (TBT), and triclosan (TCS) on aquatic larvae of Chironomus riparius , and found that all tested compounds induced DNA damage. Notably, in most treatment groups, prolonged exposure did not correspondingly increase the extent of DNA damage, suggesting that the genotoxicity of these compounds in Chironomus is not simply time-dependent. Aquilino et al. [ 5 ] assessed the DNA damage potential of fungicide vinclozolin (Vz) on Chironomus larvae with the comet assay and measured transcriptional changes in DNA repair-related genes (ATM, NLK, XRCC1) and the apoptosis-related gene (DECAY) and confirmed the potential genotoxicity of Vz to freshwater invertebrates. Fish such as zebrafish and rainbow trout are traditional vertebrate animal models. Zebrafish share high genomic similarity with humans, rendering them ideal for genotoxicity assessment [ 63 ]. Du et al. [ 64 ] employed miRNA sequencing, dual-fluorescence protein assays, and dual-luciferase reporter assays to demonstrate that exposure to environmentally relevant low-dose gradients of DEHP significantly reduced zebrafish embryo survival, accompanied by downregulation of miR-146a and upregulation of TRAF6, indicating that DEHP exerts genotoxic effects during early embryonic development, potentially through modulation of inflammation-related signaling pathways. In adult fish models, Rivero-Wendt et al. [ 65 ] evaluated the genotoxicity of the synthetic hormone 17α-methyltestosterone (MT) in adult Nile tilapia ( Oreochromis niloticus ) and the Brazilian native non-target fish ( Astyanax bimaculatus ) using the micronucleus test and comet assay. Oreochromis niloticus is the target aquaculture species for MT-induced sex reversal, while Astyanax bimaculatus serves as a local bioindicator to assess ecological risks posed by hatchery-released contaminants to natural freshwater ecosystems. They found that no significant differences in micronucleus frequency or DNA damage were observed between the two species, while MT significantly increased erythrocyte nuclear abnormalities in the non-target fish ( Astyanax bimaculatus ). Brown trout ( Salmo trutta ) represents a native salmonid bioindicator species widely used for aquatic endocrine-disruptor risk assessment; its nucleated peripheral erythrocytes are well-suited for micronucleus-based genotoxicity monitoring, and chronic-release implant exposure protocols are well-established in this species to mimic long-term environmental exposure. Frenzilli et al. [ 9 ] employed this species as a model and combined the micronucleus test with liquid chromatography-electrospray tandem mass spectrometry to confirm that both BPA and its substitute BPS induce endocrine disruption and genotoxic effects, and demonstrated that BPA and BPS trigger different DNA damage patterns, suggesting distinct mechanisms of action. Collectively, these studies indicate that different fish models offer unique advantages in evaluating EED genotoxicity, and that different species, exposure concentrations, and detection endpoints can all influence the assessment of genotoxicity. The male Japanese quail ( Coturnix japonica ) has high sensitivity to endocrine disruptors and is a valuable avian model in environmental toxicology [ 66 ]. It has been reported that dibutyl phthalate (DBP) dose-dependently induces typical apoptotic features in spermatogenic cells, including nuclear membrane rupture, chromatin condensation, and apoptotic body formation, confirming the genotoxic effects of DBP on avian germ cells [ 67 ]. Amphibian models also offer unique advantages in genotoxicity assessment. Tadpoles, as the larval stage of amphibians, have short growth cycles, obvious morphological changes, and are easy to observe, making them ideal subjects for studying the effects of environmental pollutants on early development. Scaia et al. [ 68 ] used bullfrog ( Lithobates catesbeianus ) tadpoles as a model and employed the micronucleus test, along with binucleated, anucleated, and nuclear bud indicators, to systematically evaluate the genotoxic effects of NP. They found that NP not only induced genetic damage in tadpoles but also caused significant morphological alterations by interfering with oogenesis, inducing nuclear abnormalities, and changing body color. In addition to aquatic and mammalian models, earthworms (e.g., Eisenia fetida and Pheretima guillemi ) have emerged as valuable model organisms in terrestrial ecotoxicology for evaluating the genotoxicity of EEDs and emerging pollutants in recent years. Chen et al. [ 69 ] integrated transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses and found that soil antimony (Sb) exposure significantly disturbed the endocrine system, immune system, and oxidative phosphorylation pathways in earthworms, confirming its multi-target genotoxicity potential. For nanoplastics, although direct genotoxic endpoints were not measured in the available assays, Zhou et al. [ 70 ] found that nanoplastics accumulated in earthworms and induced oxidative stress, leading to immune coelomocyte death; these observed toxic effects may arise from particle-mediated physical impacts as well as associated chemical contaminants. These earthworm-based studies have not only filled knowledge gaps regarding the genotoxic effects of EEDs on terrestrial invertebrates but also provided critical data for assessing risks associated with soil combined pollution. Taken together, in vivo genotoxicity assessments of EEDs rely on a diverse suite of animal models with complementary strengths and inherent limitations. Rodent models (mouse and rat) possess mammalian physiological features relevant to human health outcomes and are well-suited for investigating reproductive and somatic genotoxicity, although they are relatively costly and less applicable for field-relevant ecological monitoring. Fish-based assays constitute robust systems for aquatic ecotoxicological risk assessment, supporting investigations ranging from developmental toxicity to implant-mediated chronic-exposure experiments. Avian models such as Japanese quail are particularly valuable for evaluating germ-cell-related genotoxic outcomes, as demonstrated by documented apoptotic damage to spermatogenic cells following phthalate exposure in this species. Amphibian tadpole models are highly responsive to early-life-stage genotoxic insults from EEDs, as illustrated by nuclear and chromosomal abnormalities observed in tadpole assays, making them suitable for amphibian-focused ecotoxicological risk assessment. Terrestrial earthworm models can readily detect soil-pollutant-triggered genotoxicity in coelomocyte immune cells; nevertheless, these invertebrates lack the sophisticated endocrine and gene-regulatory networks present in mammals. No single animal model can perfectly recapitulate all aspects of human and environmental risk; therefore, combining evidence from multiple model organisms helps obtain a more comprehensive understanding of EED-associated genotoxic hazards. 5.2. In Vitro Studies Using Human Cell Models Animal experiments can provide in vivo results for chemical genotoxicity assessment. However, they remain costly, time-consuming, and often fail to comply with the “3R” principles. More critically, results obtained from animal tests may not accurately extrapolate to humans due to interspecies differences. In contrast, in vitro toxicity assays based on human cells are more directly relevant to human health and effectively circumvent the uncertainty of interspecies extrapolation [ 71 ]. Therefore, the establishment and optimization of human-derived cell models have become a major method in genotoxicity testing. Figure 2 summarizes recent genotoxicity studies of EEDs across various human cells and the specific information can be found in Table S2 . To date, various human cell lines have been developed for genotoxicity evaluation of EEDs. The human hepatoma cell line HepG2 possesses both hepatocyte functions and cancer cell features, making it widely used in genotoxicity experiments [ 13 , 49 ]. Schmidt et al. [ 72 ] employed the Ames test and comet assay to evaluate the mutagenicity and genotoxicity of eight BPA analogs (BPF, BPAF, BPZ, BPS, DMBPA, DMBPS, BP-1, and BP-2) on HepG2 cells at non-cytotoxic concentrations (0.1–10 μmol/L). The results showed that only BPA and BPS induced significant DNA damage after 24 h of exposure, whereas DMBPS, BP-1, and BP-2 caused only transient increases in DNA strand breaks after 4 h, and BPF, BPAF, BPZ, and DMBPA showed no obvious effects. While Kose et al. [ 10 ] used a modified comet assay in human normal prostate epithelial RWPE-1 cells and found that BPF and BPS could induce high levels of DNA damage at non-cytotoxic concentrations and simultaneously inhibit DNA repair pathways, indicating that different cell types exhibit differential sensitivity to bisphenol analogs. The MCF-7 breast cancer cell line, due to its estrogen responsiveness, is a common in vitro model for endocrine disruption studies. The insecticide flumethrin induced genotoxicity in MCF-7 cells even at a low concentration of 0.1 μmol/L, as shown by micronucleus and comet assays [ 73 ]. The TK6 human lymphoblastoid cell line, which grows in suspension, is easy to culture and exhibits good sensitivity and specificity; it is frequently used in genotoxicity screening. Al-Saleh et al. [ 74 ] used the comet and micronucleus assays to evaluate the genotoxic potential of 42 perfumes in TK6 cells, finding that phthalate esters (PAEs) in perfumes significantly contribute to DNA damage. Luo [ 75 ] used human bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (HBMSCs) as a model to study the genotoxicity of three neonicotinoid insecticides such as imidacloprid (IMI), nitenpyram (NIT), and acetamiprid (ACE) and found that all three induced DNA damage at concentrations that did not affect cell viability. Furthermore, primary human cells offer unique advantages in reflecting real human responses [ 76 , 77 ]. Sicińska et al. [ 78 ] used human peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) to investigate the epigenetic toxicity of phthalates (DBP, BBP) and their metabolites (MBP, MBzP). They found that BBP, MBP and MBzP significantly decreased global DNA methylation levels, induced hypermethylation of the promoter regions of tumor suppressor genes TP53 and P16 with consequent downregulation of their expression, and caused hypomethylation of the promoters of proto-oncogenes CCND1 and BCL2 with consequent upregulation. For respiratory exposure pollutants, the human lung adenocarcinoma A549 cell line and normal bronchial epithelial BEAS-2B cells have become core models [ 79 ]. Multiple studies have shown that emerging EEDs such as chlorinated by-products of UV filters [ 80 ], tire oxidation product 6PPD-Q [ 81 ], and haloacetic acids [ 82 ] can induce oxidative DNA damage and micronucleus formation via ROS elevation, exhibiting genotoxicity. 5.3. In Vitro Studies Using Animal-Derived Cell Models The use of animal-derived cells for in vitro genotoxicity studies represents another important approach due to their well-defined genetic backgrounds, stable proliferation, and sensitivity to oxidative stress and genetic damage. In addition, they can provide cellular and molecular mechanistic insights to complement in vivo findings. Currently, the animal cell models commonly employed in genotoxicity studies of EEDs are primarily derived from fish and mammals. The ZFL (ZF-L) cell line, derived from zebrafish liver, is a classic in vitro model for studying hepatic metabolism, xenobiotic biotransformation, endocrine disruption, and genotoxicity in fish [ 83 ]. Studies have demonstrated that titanium dioxide nanoparticles (TiO 2 NPs) can induce excessive ROS production in ZFL cells, leading to DNA strand breaks, chromosomal damage, and micronuclei formation [ 84 ]. In addition, primary thymic cells from the European seabass (Dicentrarchus labrax) and erythrocytes from Oreochromis niloticus serve as a fish-derived immune-related in vitro model that is highly sensitive to environmentally relevant concentrations of E2 and 17α-ethinylestradiol (EE2) [ 85 , 86 ]. Following exposure, cell proliferation is markedly inhibited, the mTOR energy checkpoint pathway is downregulated, and cell cycle progression and genomic stability are disrupted, ultimately leading to T-cell developmental arrest and immune-related genetic damage. Mammalian cell models are also recognized as a standard in vitro system for genotoxicity assessment. Xin et al. [ 87 ] used Chinese hamster ovary (CHO) cells to systematically evaluate the cytotoxicity, genotoxicity, and mutagenicity of BPA. Zhu et al. [ 88 ] employed Mouse embryonic fibroblast (MEF) cells to investigate the combined genotoxic effects of hexavalent chromium (Cr(VI)) and 1800 MHz radiofrequency electromagnetic radiation and found a synergistic induction of DNA damage. Compared with the above-mentioned commercially available mammalian and fish cell lines, cell models derived from amphibians and terrestrial invertebrates remain relatively limited in EED genotoxicity studies. For amphibians, embryonic cells and spermatogenic cells of Xenopus laevis are classic in vitro models for studying developmental and reproductive toxicity in amphibians [ 89 , 90 ]. For terrestrial invertebrates, earthworm coelomocytes are currently the most frequently used cell model for evaluating the genotoxicity of soil-associated EEDs using comet and micronucleus assays [ 91 ]. 6. Genotoxicity Mechanisms To more clearly investigate the mechanisms of genotoxicity induced by EEDs, we summarized the molecular initiating events (MIEs), key events (KEs), and adverse outcomes (AOs) of EEDs-triggered genotoxicity based on published literature, as shown in Table 2 . Furthermore, integrating the experimental evidence summarized in Table 2 , we proposed a putative adverse outcome pathway (AOP) framework for EEDs-induced genotoxicity. As shown in Figure 3 , EEDs can induce genotoxicity via multiple pathways and the core mechanisms can be summarized as ROS-mediated direct DNA damage, interference with DDR pathways, and disruption of epigenetic regulation. In addition, these pathways interact with each other and may amplify progressively, ultimately leading to accumulation of DNA damage, chromosomal aberrations, and genomic instability. Therefore, synergistic effects of combined exposure may also be an important characteristic of genotoxicity. However, this conceptual framework has not undergone formal weight-of-evidence evaluation. Further systematic experimental validation is still required to confirm the causal linkages among MIEs, KEs, and AOs. 6.1. ROS-Mediated Direct DNA Damage Oxidative stress is the core upstream mechanism by which EEDs trigger direct DNA damage [ 98 ]. EEDs can disrupt mitochondrial respiratory chain function and inhibit antioxidant enzyme systems, leading to excessive accumulation of intracellular ROS and a state of oxidative stress [ 99 ]. Excessive ROS directly attack DNA molecules, inducing various forms of oxidative DNA damage, including base oxidation, single-strand breaks, and double-strand breaks, which constitute the initiating events of genotoxicity [ 70 ]. Numerous studies have confirmed that many typical EEDs initiate genotoxic effects via this pathway [ 100 ]. For example, exposure of zebrafish embryos to various exogenous aquatic environmental pollutants can lead to mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative DNA damage, and pretreatment with the ROS scavenger N-acetylcysteine significantly alleviates the damage, confirming the critical mediating role of ROS [ 101 , 102 , 103 ]. Organophosphate flame retardants such as TCEP, TCPP, and TDCPP also increase ROS levels in human hepatoma HepG2 cells, accompanied by evident DNA strand breaks [ 29 ]. Phthalates such as DBP and DEHP can induce a significant accumulation of ROS in germ cells, which in turn leads to oxidation of sperm nucleobases and DNA strand breaks, thereby compromising sperm genomic stability [ 11 ]. Mohafrash et al. [ 17 ] found that pyrethroid insecticide-induced DNA damage may stem from enhanced lipid peroxidation and oxidative stress induced by excessive production of free radicals (particularly ROS), leading to redox imbalance. Zhou et al. [ 70 ] found that nanoplastics accumulated in earthworms and triggered ROS burst, inhibited antioxidant enzyme activity, and disrupted the lysosome-autophagy pathway, ultimately resulting in the death of coelomocyte immune cells; these toxic outcomes may stem from particle-derived physical effects as well as associated chemical contaminants. 6.2. Interference with DNA Damage Response Pathways DDR is the core defense mechanism for maintaining genomic stability. Upon occurrence of different types of DNA damage, cells initiate highly specific repair processes. EEDs can act at multiple key nodes of DDR pathways, hindering proper damage recognition, signal transduction, and repair execution, thereby exacerbating genotoxicity and compromising genomic stability [ 104 ]. First, EEDs can interfere with early damage recognition and signal activation. They may inhibit the recruitment of the MRN complex, reduce the phosphorylation efficiency of ATM and ATR kinases, and decrease the formation of γH2AX foci, leading to ineffective sensing and transduction of DNA double-strand break signals [ 105 , 106 ]. Studies have shown that TDCPP and TCEP significantly upregulate ATM and γH2AX protein expression, which is both a marker of DNA double-strand breaks and a reflection of compensatory cellular responses to damage signals and interference with this signaling will directly affect the selection and initiation of downstream repair pathways [ 31 , 32 ]. Second, EEDs can disrupt cell cycle checkpoint regulation. They can downregulate core checkpoint proteins such as p53 and p21, leading to dysfunction of G1/S and G2/M checkpoints [ 107 ]. Notably, DNA repair mechanisms are closely related to the cell cycle. Homologous recombination (HR) mainly operates in the S/G2 phase, whereas non-homologous end joining (NHEJ) can function throughout the entire cell cycle [ 14 ]. Disruption of checkpoint function directly weakens the time-window effect of repair mechanisms. Third, EEDs differentially inhibit repair pathways for different types of DNA damage. For DNA DSBs, DNA damage repair primarily relies on HR and NHEJ pathways [ 108 ]. Studies have found that many EEDs upregulate the mRNA expression of HR and NHEJ pathway genes at low concentrations, which appears to be a compensatory protective response [ 31 , 32 ]. However, at high concentrations or under prolonged exposure, some EEDs instead suppress key gene expression, shifting repair from high-fidelity HR to error-prone NHEJ, significantly increasing the risk of gene mutations and chromosomal translocations [ 107 ]. For single-strand breaks (SSBs) and base damage, cells activate three major pathways in coordination: base excision repair (BER), nucleotide excision repair (NER), and mismatch repair (MMR) [ 109 ]. EEDs with different chemical structures exhibit markedly different effects on these pathways. For example, the chlorinated organophosphate TDCPP tends to upregulate APEX1 and FEN1 genes (involved in BER for oxidative base modifications) but significantly downregulates MMR pathway genes (MSH2, MLH1) and NER pathway genes (DDB1, DDB2, ERCC1) [ 31 ]. The alkyl organophosphate TNBP activates most SSB repair pathways at lower concentrations but inhibits them at higher concentrations [ 32 ]. In addition, EEDs can also cross-regulate DDR through their endocrine-disrupting activities. Through hormone-mimicking, they can indirectly influence the transcriptional regulation of DDR-related genes, linking endocrine disruption with genotoxicity and forming a complex damage amplification loop [ 45 ]. For example, BPA exerts dual effects as both an estrogen agonist and an androgen antagonist [ 110 ]. By binding to estrogen receptors, it excessively promotes cell proliferation, inducing DNA replication stress and double-strand breaks. Simultaneously, it competitively inhibits the androgen receptor pathway, downregulating the transcription of core DDR genes such as ATM, BRCA1, and p53, thereby weakening DNA damage repair and cell cycle monitoring functions. TBT induced DNA strand breaks in fourth-instar Chironomus larvae at environmentally relevant concentrations and activated hormone nuclear receptor and early response gene expression [ 111 ]. In our previous study, we found that OPFRs can trigger GPER, activate PI3K/Akt and ERK signaling, change the expression of target proteins and genes associated with SSB and SDB damage-repair pathways, ultimately inducing DNA damage in GT1-7 cells [ 31 , 32 ]. Luo et al. [ 75 ] also revealed that three neonicotinoid insecticides induced DNA damage likely involving the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway and its crosstalk with other pathways. Hormonal disruption exacerbates DNA damage, while defects in repair pathways lead to the continued accumulation of genomic damage. 6.3. Disruption of Epigenetic Regulation Unlike direct alterations of DNA sequence, epigenetic toxicity exerts indirect but long-lasting genetic hazards by modulating gene expression states. The relevant mechanisms mainly involve changes in DNA methylation, histone modifications, and non-coding RNA expression, which ultimately lead to aberrant silencing or activation of genes related to genomic stability [ 112 ]. EEDs can interfere with the activity and targeting of DNA methyltransferases, causing bidirectional disruption of global methylation levels. On one hand, they lead to hypermethylation of promoter regions of tumor suppressor genes and consequent transcriptional silencing, weakening the ability of cells to monitor proliferation [ 113 , 114 ]. On the other hand, they induce hypomethylation of promoters of proto-oncogenes or pro-proliferative genes (e.g., CCND1, BCL2) and aberrant activation, driving abnormal cell proliferation. Phthalates such as BBP, MBP, and MBzP significantly decreased global DNA methylation levels, induced hypermethylation of the promoter regions of tumor suppressor genes TP53 and P16 with consequent downregulation of their expression, and caused hypomethylation of the promoters of proto-oncogenes CCND1 and BCL2 with consequent upregulation [ 77 ]. This bidirectional dysregulation collectively creates a molecular environment conducive to uncontrolled proliferation and genomic instability, amplifying genotoxic insults and potentially increasing carcinogenic risks in human cells. In addition to DNA methylation, EEDs can also affect the activity of histone acetyltransferases, methyltransferases, and other modifying enzymes, changing the post-translational modification status of histones (acetylation, methylation, phosphorylation, etc.), thereby modulating chromatin accessibility in regions containing DNA damage repair genes [ 115 ]. This can indirectly reduce the transcription efficiency of repair proteins and weaken the capacity of cells for damage recognition and repair. Additionally, EEDs can also change the expression profiles of non-coding RNAs such as microRNAs (miRNAs), long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs), and other non-coding RNAs [ 116 , 117 ]. These non-coding RNAs directly target and suppress the translation of mRNAs that encode DNA repair proteins or cell cycle checkpoint proteins. This post-transcriptional regulation further reduces the overall efficacy of the DDR network. Notably, the three epigenetic mechanisms do not act alone but work together. DNA methylation and histone modifications jointly regulate chromatin structure, while non-coding RNAs can target and modulate the expression of methyltransferases and histone-modifying enzymes [ 112 ]. Together, they form a multi-layered regulatory network that amplifies epigenetic toxicity, allowing EED-induced gene expression abnormalities to persist through cell divisions [ 113 ]. Such persistence is a key basis for long-term genotoxicity. In addition, three core mechanisms can interact with each other and amplify progressively [ 118 , 119 ]. Specifically, excessive ROS accumulation overwhelms antioxidant defenses, leading to oxidative damage of lipids, proteins, and nucleic acids. This damage further impairs DDR efficiency, allowing mutations to accumulate unchecked. Concurrently, epigenetic alterations silence critical tumor suppressor genes while activating oncogenic pathways. Ultimately, the synergy among oxidative stress, defective DNA repair, and epigenetic shifts leads to accumulation of DNA damage, chromosomal aberrations, and genomic instability. 6.4. Synergistic Effects of Combined Exposure It is noteworthy that in real environments, EEDs rarely exist as single compounds but rather as complex mixtures of multiple pollutants. Recent studies have demonstrated that combined exposure often produces more potent genotoxicity than single substances [ 120 ]; even these effects were transmitted to the F1 and F3 generations [ 121 ]. Co-exposure of polyamide microplastics and TCPP in thick-shelled mussels at environmentally relevant concentrations synergistically induced oxidative stress via adsorption interactions, suggesting that co-exposure of EEDs poses a potentially more severe genotoxic threat to aquatic ecosystems than exposure to a single compound [ 122 ]. The combined exposure groups of BPA, dibutyl phthalate, and cadmium exhibited significantly higher cytotoxicity, oxidative damage, and DNA damage in HepG2 cells than single exposure groups in a 3 × 3 full-factorial design [ 123 ]. Radiation alone did not induce obvious DNA damage in MEF cells, but combined exposure with Cr(VI) significantly increased comet tail moments and γH2AX foci numbers, accompanied by S-phase cell cycle arrest, indicating a synergistic induction of DNA strand breaks. However, research on combined genotoxicity remains limited. Different types of EEDs can act on various genotoxicity endpoints, such as oxidative stress, DNA repair, cell cycle regulation, and chromosome segregation, and through cross-talk and mutual amplification among pathways [ 39 , 124 ]. The combined toxicity may far exceed the simple arithmetic sum of individual exposures and the synergistic effects of combined exposure are manifested in three main aspects. First, different EEDs simultaneously promote ROS production via multiple routes, pushing intracellular oxidative stress beyond the upper limit of single substances, resulting in more severe oxidative DNA damage [ 125 ]. Second, multiple EEDs jointly target different nodes of DDR pathways, producing multiple inhibitory effects on the repair system, leading to a comprehensive decline in the ability of cells to repair various types of damage [ 118 ]. Third, epigenetic regulation becomes more complex under combined exposure. Different EEDs can synergistically disrupt DNA methylation and histone modification homeostasis, accelerating chromatin structural disorganization and gene expression abnormalities, thereby accelerating the accumulation of DNA damage, chromosomal aberrations, and gene mutations [ 126 ]. 7. Summary and Perspectives This study comprehensively reviews the research advances of the genotoxic effects and underlying molecular mechanisms of both classical and emerging EEDs using in vivo animal and in vitro cell models over the past fifteen years. The integrated literature analysis indicates that bisphenols, phthalates, organophosphate flame retardants, pyrethroid insecticides, and emerging pollutants can induce genotoxic effects including DNA damage, chromosomal aberrations, and micronucleus formation. The core mechanisms primarily involve genotoxic insults driven by ROS-mediated oxidative stress, interference with DDR pathways, and epigenetic dysregulation. Under realistic multi-pollutant coexposure scenarios, combined exposure can significantly amplify genotoxicity through synergistic effects. Notably, some novel substitutes such as BPS and BPF exhibit even higher genotoxicity than the parent compound in some models, highlighting the urgent need for more rigorous safety evaluations of substitutes. Based on qualitative synthesis of in vitro and in vivo studies compiled in this narrative review together with consensus viewpoints from published expert toxicological reviews, existing genotoxicity evidence varies considerably across different environmental endocrine-disrupting chemicals. Bisphenol analogues and phthalates exhibit comparatively consistent genotoxicity-related observations across diverse in vitro cellular models and several in vivo animal studies reported in the literature [ 11 ]. Negative genotoxic outcomes are occasionally reported in the literature, yet a substantial number of reported genotoxic endpoints are induced under experimental concentrations exceeding realistic environmentally relevant human exposure levels [ 127 ]. Notably, several key genotoxic phenotypes of classical EEDs have been replicated across independent experimental systems, genotoxic datasets for many emerging contaminants remain heterogeneous and lack cross-model validation [ 27 , 28 ]. Although numerous studies have confirmed the significant genotoxic potential of various EEDs, several research gaps and challenges remain that warrant future attention. First, the exposure scenarios in current studies deviate considerably from real environments. Most existing studies employ short-term, high-concentration exposure regimens, whereas actual environmental EED levels are typically low but persist over extended periods. Importantly, genotoxic effects observed at micromolar-range in vitro experimental concentrations should not be directly extrapolated to human health risks under real-world environmental exposure scenarios. Future efforts should prioritize the development of highly sensitive detection techniques and focus on the long-term health impacts of environmentally relevant low-dose exposures, so as to address the uncertainty associated with high-to-low dose extrapolation in current risk assessments. Second, the research perspective needs to shift from single-compound to combined exposure. Most published work has centered on individual EEDs, while real environments typically harbor complex mixtures of multiple pollutants over prolonged timescales. Therefore, combined exposure more closely reflects real environmental exposure scenarios. Future studies should pay greater attention to the synergistic toxic effects of combined EED exposure under realistic coexposure scenarios to accurately assess genotoxicity risk. More importantly, combined exposure of multiple EEDs can break through the low-dose effect thresholds of single substances and induce more significant genetic damage even at extremely low environmental concentrations [ 39 , 128 ]. Therefore, the synergistic effects of combined exposure are a critical and indispensable aspect of EED genotoxicity. Third, mechanistic investigations should be upgraded from fragmented to systematic integration. With the rapid advancement of computational toxicology, high-throughput screening technologies, multi-omics, and CRISPR gene editing, genotoxicity research is progressively moving from single-endpoint detection toward in-depth mechanistic dissection and multidimensional data integration. In recent years, artificial intelligence-based toxicity prediction models have begun to be applied to EED genotoxicity screening, opening new insights for efficient risk assessment of large chemical inventories. Future work should integrate these technological advantages to establish a tripartite research framework of “high-throughput in vitro screening, targeted in vivo validation, and computational modeling and prediction.” In summary, the integration of high-throughput screening, multi-omics, and AI-based modeling should be further leveraged to focus on the genotoxic effects and transgenerational mechanisms under low-dose, long-term, and combined exposure scenarios. These efforts will help refine detection standards and risk assessment frameworks for EEDs, thereby providing a more robust scientific basis for environmental pollution control and the protection of human health.
