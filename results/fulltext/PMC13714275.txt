Introduction Similar to lymphatic vessels (lymphatics) in other organs, cardiac lymphatics maintain tissue homeostasis by transporting extra interstitial fluid, macromolecules, and immune cells 1 . Recent studies have revealed an emerging role for cardiac lymphatic vessels in heart regeneration 2 – 8 . Lymphangiogenesis occurs after myocardial infarction (MI), and ectopic enhancement of lymphangiogenesis was found to improve cardiac function after MI in animal models 2 – 8 . However, the molecular mechanisms of cardiac lymphatic vessel formation and their roles in heart regeneration remain unclear. In fact, even conflicting data regarding their roles have been reported 4 , 9 . The zebrafish has been used as a model to study coronary and cardiac lymphatic vessel development and their functions in heart regeneration 6 – 8 , 10 , 11 . We and others have characterized cardiac lymphatic development in zebrafish 6 – 8 . Zebrafish cardiac lymphatics originate from the facial lymphatic vessels and migrate along the ventral aorta to arrive on the bulbus arteriosus (BA) around 1 month post fertilization (mpf) 6 , 7 . Cardiac lymphatic endothelial progenitors cover the BA with a dense network but do not emerge onto the ventricle until the young-adult stage at 3-4 mpf 6 – 8 . This late development of ventricular lymphatic vessels is likely due to increased heart rate at the juvenile-adult stage transition 7 . During this transition, the zebrafish cardiac lymphatic vessels migrate down from the BA towards the apex along coronary arteries and continue to branch to form the lymphatic network on the ventricular surface 6 . While lymphatics develop along coronary veins in embryonic mice 4 , the arterial association in zebrafish is similar to cardiac lymphatics located in the epicardium of developing human heart 12 , 13 , likely due to the differential expression and abundance of Vegfc 14 . We performed spatial transcriptomic analyses of adult zebrafish hearts at the single-cell level and identified lymphatic endothelial cells (LECs). The expression of genes encoding factors that regulate lymphatic development, including vegfc and adrenomedullin a ( adma ), was found around the areas where cardiac lymphatics form. ADM is a 52-amino acid peptide that was first identified as a vasodilator hormone 15 – 18 . The ADM peptide binds and activates calcitonin receptor-like receptors (CLRs)-receptor activity-modifying protein (RAMP) complexes 15 – 18 . ADM signaling and its decoy receptor ACKR3/CXCR7 plays an important role in cardiac lymphatic development in mice 5 , 19 – 26 while the roles of Adm signaling in zebrafish cardiac lymphatics and heart regeneration remain unknown. We found that lymphatics display different morphology on BA and ventricle in zebrafish; BA lymphatics appear flattened and ventricular lymphatics have more branches. To further characterize the molecular mechanisms of cardiac lymphatic development, we generated adma and ackr3a CRISPR mutant zebrafish. The adma mutant fish showed wider BA with reduced lymphatic coverage, and increased lyve1b+ lymphatics with decreased sprouts and altered LEC heterogeneity on ventricles. On the other hand, mutation of ackr3a , which encodes an Adma decoy receptor, resulted in ventricular lymphatic hypersprouting. Overexpression of ackr3a in the epicardium resulted in decreased ventricular lymphatic sprouts, suggesting a potential role for epicardial Ackr3a in regulating cardiac lymphatic development. Furthermore, the adma; ackr3a double mutant phenotypes suggest genetic interactions between adma and ackr3a . Expression of adma and ackr3a was detected in the epicardium-derived cells and within the wound of regenerating zebrafish hearts after cryoinjury. The clearance of neutrophils was impaired in adma mutants while differentially expressed genes related to permeability and immune responses were identified in ackr3a mutants with faster neutrophil clearance 7 days after heart injury. These results suggest that Adma and Ackr3a play important roles in regulating cardiac lymphatic formation and function during heart development and regeneration. Results Single-cell spatial transcriptomics of adult zebrafish heart Emerging technologies using spatial transcriptomic analyses have been developed to examine gene expression and explore potential mechanisms regulating cell–cell interactions in different tissues and organs. To investigate the spatial organization of different cells in the zebrafish heart at the single-cell level, we performed sequential fluorescence in situ hybridization (seqFISH) on sagittal sections of adult zebrafish hearts. We designed a custom panel consisting of 168 genes encoding well-established markers for cardiomyocytes, endothelial cells, smooth muscle cells, and other major cardiac cell types and candidate signaling molecules (Supplementary Data 1 and 2 ). The selected heart section contained 8,650 cells, which were clustered into 10 cell types (Fig. 1a, b ). Fig. 1 SeqFISH Spatial transcriptomic analysis of adult zebrafish heart. a Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) plot illustrating annotated cell clusters based on transcriptomic profiles. b UMAP plots showing spatial distribution of each cluster. c Spatial feature plot highlighting the marker genes for each cluster. d Dot plot displaying the expression of marker genes across clusters; dot color represents average expression level, and dot size indicates the percentage of expressing cells within each cluster. Cardiomyocyte clusters were identified based on high expression of markers such as myl7 and myh7ba (Fig. 1b–d ). Endothelial cell clusters were similarly defined by strong expression of endothelial markers including cdh5 (Fig. 1b-d ). We further identified a cluster expressing high levels of acta2 , tagln , acana , and myh11a that localized to the bulbus arteriosus (BA), suggesting a smooth muscle cell identity (Figs. 1b, d and S1a ). Another cluster, which expressed high levels of tcf21 , tbx18 , col1a2 , and postnb , was found at the outermost region of the heart surface, which is indicative of an epicardial and epicardium-derived cell identity (Figs. 1b–d , S1a ). We also identified a mural cell/pericyte cluster, characterized by high expression levels of pdgfrb (Fig. 1b–d ). Other cell types, including erythrocytes, macrophages, immune cells, and thrombocytes, were also present in our dataset (Fig. 1b–d ). Together, these results demonstrate that spatial transcriptomic profiling enables high-resolution mapping of diverse cardiac cell populations and their anatomical organization in the adult zebrafish heart. SeqFISH reveals cardiac LECs and other regulatory cells Recently, Li et al. employed Spatio-Temporal Enhanced Resolution Omics-sequencing (stereo-seq) to construct a spatially resolved molecular and cellular atlas of the regenerating zebrafish heart 27 . In their dataset, each spatial bin encompassed approximately 962 μm, capturing an estimated 3–8 cells per spot. Although this resolution was sufficient to identify major cell types, it was limited in its ability to resolve rare or spatially restricted populations such as LECs. Notably, we detected rare populations in zebrafish hearts by seqFISH, such as cardiac LECs (Figs. 1 and 2a ). In our dataset, LECs formed a distinct cluster of 164 cells enriched in regions surrounding the BA and adjacent to adipose-like tissue (Fig. 2a ). LEC markers, including flt4 , lyve1b , mrc1a , mrc1b , cdh6 , stab1 and prox1a , were highly expressed within this cluster, further supporting its annotation as a lymphatic endothelial population 28 (Figs. 1c, d , 2b, c , S1b and S1c ). In addition to Vegfc, other signaling molecules known to regulate cardiac LECs such as Adrenomedullin (Adm) were also assessed in our seqFISH dataset (Fig. 2d ). The expression of both adma and admb genes encoding ligands and their receptor genes calcrla , calcrlb , and ramp2 was examined in zebrafish hearts (Fig. 2d ). We found that ramp2 and calcrlb were broadly expressed in the heart and highly expressed in LECs, and the ligand adma was expressed in cells adjacent to LECs (Fig. 2d–f ). This suggests that Adma-calcrlb signaling may play a conserved role in regulating cardiac lymphatic development in zebrafish similar to that in mice. Interestingly, although both vegfc and adma were expressed adjacent to flt4 + LECs, we observed that adma and vegfc were mostly expressed in different cells, suggesting a potential complex interaction network between various cell populations (Fig. 2f ). Some adma mutant fish started to develop thoracic-abdominal edema at 1mpf. The edema in adma mutant fish developed slowly, and some mutant fish were able to survive with severe edema until adulthood (Fig. 3b ). Such developmental defects in adma mutant fish are consistent with what was reported in Adm-/- mice 19 . Therefore, we analyzed and scored male and female fish separately to consider sex as a biological variable. More female adma mutants (51.7%) developed edema compared to males (18.5%) (Fig. S2e ). No obvious ventricle size difference was observed in adma mutants as identified in mice mutants (Fig. S2f ). However, we observed several cardiac morphology changes in adma mutant BAs and ventricles. The dilation of BA was observed by an increased BA width-to-height ratio by 29.6% in male and 40.4% in female adma mutants compared to wild-type (WT) sibling controls (Figs. 3c, d , S2g and S2h ). The BA-to-ventricle size ratio was also significantly increased by 59.3% in male and 68.1% in female adma mutant (Fig. S2f, S2i and S2j ). Although ventricle size was not significantly reduced, the adma mutant heart ventricles appeared rounder in shape than WT sibling controls with ventricular width-to-height ratio significantly increased by 21.8% in male and 16% in females (Figs. 3e , S2k and S2l ). The increased ventricle width-to-height ratio was due to the reduction of ventricle height (Fig. S2k and S2l ). Interestingly, epicardial cell-derived Adm drives cardiac growth during heart development in mice 30 . These results suggest that Adma plays a conserved role in regulating cardiac morphogenesis in zebrafish. In addition to cardiac morphology changes, we have noticed abnormal fat accumulation on adma mutant heart ventricles (Figs. 3c and S2m ). During development, there is little fat accumulation on the edge of adult ventricles (Fig. S2m ). In some adma mutants, fat accumulation is drastic, and fat droplet size also increased compared to WT hearts (Fig. S2m ). In addition to the increased lipid droplets, the location of fat accumulation appears to extend onto the middle region of the heart ventricle surface as well (Fig. S2m ). We then examined cardiac lymphatic development with flt4:mCitrine reporter (Fig. 3c ). We reported previously that flt4:mCitrine reporter specifically labeled cardiac lymphatic vessels in zebrafish 6 . In WT fish, BA lymphatic vessels typically extend gradually into a flat lymphatic network that covers a large area of the total BA surface (Fig. 3c ’) 6 – 8 . Compared to WT siblings, the BA lymphatics in adma mutants appeared more tubular (Fig. 3c ”). The BA lymphatic coverage and the percentage of flattened BA lymphatics were significantly reduced by 38.7% and 85.11% in male and 29.3% and 52.31% in female in adma mutants (Fig. 3c, f, g ). Interestingly, the defects in BA lymphatics did not noticeably affect the lymphatics emerging onto the ventricle in adma mutants (Fig. 3c and S3a ). To exclude the ectopic lymphatics that might be induced by fat droplets 31 , we only characterized the ventricular lymphatics of adma mutant hearts in the areas without fat accumulation (area excluding orange dashed line in Fig. 3c ). We observed the diameter significantly increased by 145% in females (Fig. 3c, h ). The lymphatic dilation was more severe in females compared to males in adma mutants and reduced the statistical power to detect changes in adma male mutants in Two-way ANOVA. The lymphatic diameter was also significantly increased by 38.3% in males when analyzed independently of sex (Fig. S2n ). Analyzing lymphatic parameters in detail showed that lymphatic sprouts (tips with filopodia) were substantially decreased by 80% in male and 66.2% in female for ventricular lymphatics in adma mutants (Fig. 3c, i ). These results suggest a complex role of Adma in regulating BA and ventricular lymphatic development in a sex dependent manner. Different morphology of lymphatic vessels on BA and ventricle suggest that these LECs might contain different populations. To determine cardiac lymphatic heterogeneity in adma mutants, we first crossed the lyve1:dsRed reporter into adma mutants with the flt4:mCitrine reporter. In contrast to the nearly identical expression patterns of lyve1b:dsRed and flt4:mCitrine reporters on the BA, different expression patterns of lyve1b:dsRed and flt4:mCitrine reporters were observed at the growing branches on WT ventricles (Figs. S3a, S3b , 3j, k ). We found that the lyve1b:dsRed reporter was not expressed in newly formed lymphatic sprouts on the ventricle in WT hearts (white number sign in the Fig. 3j ). Interestingly, increased lyve1b:dsRed reporter expression was consistently observed in all ventricular lymphatics (both large vessels and growing branches) of adma mutants (white asterisk, Fig. 3j ). Furthermore, these sprouts double positive for both lyve1b:dsRed and flt4:mCitrine reporters were observed as early as they emerged on the ventricle of adma mutants (Fig. S3a ). These results suggest that cardiac LECs on the ventricle are heterogeneous with differential lyve1b and flt4 expression patterns in WT, and the loss of Adma might upregulate lyve1b expression, and leading to increased lyve1b and flt4 double positive LECs. To further delineate the heterogeneity of cardiac LECs and compare adma mutants and WT sibling controls, we performed seqFISH focusing on cardiac lymphatics from 6 mpf adult adma mutant and WT sibling control male zebrafish. To enrich ventricular LECs, zebrafish hearts were cross-sectioned instead of sectioned longitudinally as in Fig. 1 . In total, 214,284 ventricular cells were retained for downstream analyses (Fig. S3c ). We identified a cluster enriched for lymphatic marker expression, and further refined this population by subclustering and defined LECs based on high flt4 and cdh6 expression (Fig. S4a ). This yielded 287 LECs in the WT heart and 283 LECs in the adma mutant heart (Fig. S4b ). Cells co-expressing flt4 and lyve1b constituted a larger fraction of the LEC cluster in the adma mutant ventricle compared with WT (Fig. 3l ). In addition, the lyve1b expression also increased in adma LECs compared to WT (Fig. 3m and Supplementary Data 3 ). These findings are consistent with our imaging data using lyve1b:dsRed reporter lines (Figs. 3j , S3a and S3b ). In addition to lyve1b , other lymphatic-related genes, such as cdh6, mrc1a, mrc1b, prox1a, and stab1 were also differentially expressed in mutant LECs (Fig. 3m and Supplementary Data 3 ), suggesting lymphatic gene expression profile changes upon loss of Adma. Ackr3a regulates cardiac lymphatic sprouting ACKR3 has been reported to regulate cardiac lymphatic development by modulating ADM signaling as a decoy receptor 32 . To further investigate the mechanism of Adm-Ackr3 signaling in zebrafish cardiac lymphatic vessel development, we examined expression of ackr3a by RNAscope staining in zebrafish hearts (Figs. 5a and S5a ). The expression of ackr3a was detected in the entire BA, the atrioventricular valves, the epicardium of the ventricles, and atria (Fig. S5a ). Different from the expression pattern in LECs in mice, ackr3a was mainly detected in the epicardial cells adjacent to lyve1b + LECs in zebrafish hearts (Fig. 5a ). These results suggest that Ackr3a may play a non-cell-autonomous role in regulating cardiac lymphatic growth through the modulation of Adm signaling in cardiac LECs in zebrafish. To further elucidate the role of adma in zebrafish cardiac lymphatic development, we generated a loss-of-function mutant of ackr3a using CRISPR/Cas9 (Fig. S5b ) . ackr3a transcript levels in ackr3a mutants were decreased in 5 dpf (days post fertilization) embryos, likely due to the nonsense-mediated decay (Fig. S5c ). No obvious survival defects were found in ackr3a mutants, as live adult WT, ackr3a heterozygous, and ackr3a homozygous mutant fish were recovered from offspring of roughly the expected Mendelian ratios. Furthermore, no obvious defects in lymphatic vessels were observed in ackr3a mutant embryos carrying the flt4:mCitrine reporter at 5 dpf (Fig. S5d ). The cardiac lymphatic vessel phenotypes of ackr3a mutants and WT sibling control hearts with the flt4:mCitrine reporter were analyzed at 5.5 mpf (Fig. 5b ). BA and ventricle size were not affected in ackr3a mutants (Figs. 5b , S5e and S5f ), but significant increases in ventricle width-to-height ratio were observed in both males (12.2%, p = 0.0097) and females (12.7%, p = 0.0065) (Fig. 5c ). The quantification of ventricle height and width suggests the observed change was likely driven by increase of ventricle width in ackr3a mutants (Fig. S5g and S5h ). Although we did not observe significantly increased heart size (Fig. S5e ), a trend of decreased BA-to-ventricle ratio was observed exclusively in males (17.9%. p = 0.053) with no change observed in females (10.0%, p = 0.217) (Fig. S5i ). Interestingly, a heart morphology change as hyperplasia was also observed in Ackr3 (Cxcr7)-/- mice 33 , 34 . This change in cardiac morphology showed a trend opposite to that of adma mutants, suggesting Ackr3a might function as a decoy receptor of Adma in zebrafish. To further characterize the role of Ackr3a in ventricular lymphatic development in zebrafish, we quantified various features of ventricular lymphatic vessels. Ventricular vessel diameters were similar between WT and ackr3a mutants (Fig. S5j ). The number of ventricular lymphatic sprouts with filopodia in ackr3a mutants showed a trend of increase by 69% (p = 0.0672) and 76% (p = 0.0339) in both males and females, respectively (blue rectangles and magenta arrows in Figs. 5d, e ). Additionally, ackr3a mutants showed more filopodia per sprout. This is in contrast with adma mutants, wherein there were almost no lymphatic sprouts with filopodia on ventricles. Together, these results suggest that lymphangiogenesis is activated in ackr3a mutants. Epicardial ackr3a overexpression reduces lymphatic sprouts To further investigate the epicardial function of ackr3a in regulating cardiac lymphatic vessel development, we generated an ackr3a- overexpressing transgenic line ( ubi: loxp-H2B-Cerulean-stop-loxp-ackr3a , ackr3a t OE for short; Fig. 5f ). The overexpression of ackr3a in the epicardium was achieved by inducing Cre ( tcf21:CreERT2 ) recombination in ackr3a t OE double transgenic fish (Fig. 5f ). WT control and ackr3a t OE hearts were collected at 7 mpf and imaged using the flt4:mCitrine reporter (Fig. 5g ). Cardiac morphology was first analyzed in WT and ackr3a t OE fish. Consistent with adma mutants, ventricle height showed a decreasing trend and ventricle width-to-height ratio had a trend of increase in ackr3a tOE fish (Fig. S6d-f ). Similar to ackr3a mutants, ackr3a overexpression did not significantly affect vessel diameter (Fig. S6g ). However, opposite to ackr3a mutants and consistent with adma mutants, ackr3a t OE showed a decreased trend in lymphatic sprouts on the ventricles (Fig. 5h ). In summary, ackr3a overexpression in cells of epicardial lineage had an opposite effect to deleting ackr3a , confirming the role of epicardial Ackr3a in regulating cardiac lymphatic development. ackr3a-adma genetic interaction in cardiac lymphatics The cardiac lymphatic phenotypes of adma mutant and ackr3a mutant fish show opposite trends (Fig. 3c, i and Fig. 5d, e ), consistent with the findings in mice that Ackr3 functions as a decoy receptor for Adm 32 . To further investigate the genetic interaction between adma and ackr3a in zebrafish, we examined heart morphology and cardiac lymphatic development in adma; ackr3a double mutants. The sibling WT, adma or ackr3a single mutants from the same crosses were utilized as controls. No obvious survival defects were observed in adma; ackr3a double mutants. Most cardiac phenotypes in adma; ackr3a double mutants were similar to those in adma single mutant (Fig. 6 ). Increased BA width, BA width-to-height and BA-to-ventricle ratio were observed in the adma; ackr3a double mutants similar to that in adma single mutants (Fig. 6a–d ). The adma; ackr3a double mutants also showed aberrant, tubular BA lymphatics with significantly reduced lymphatic coverage similar to adma mutants (Fig. 6a, e ). However, we observed a more dramatic fat droplet formation on the heart surface in adma; ackr3a double mutants (Fig. S7a ). This unusual fat accumulation on the heart surface closely associated with increased ventricular lymphatic outgrowth in adma;ackr3a double mutant (orange dashed line in Fig. 6a ). Similar to adma single mutants, we only characterized the ventricular lymphatics of adma; ackr3a double mutant hearts in the areas without fat accumulation because the lymphatics associated with the fat might skew the results. adma; ackr3a double mutants also show reduced ventricular lymphatic sprouts (Fig. 6f, g ), whereas ackr3a mutants had increased lymphatic sprouts. Reduced neutrophil clearance in adma mutant post-cryoinjury Despite the severe edema, some adma mutants as well as almost all ackr3a mutants can survive to adulthood, allowing us to assess how they are affected during heart regeneration. The expression patterns of adma and ackr3a expression were first examined by RNAscope using regenerating zebrafish heart at 7 days post-cryoinjury (dpc) (Fig. 7a ). The expression of adma colocalized with a sub-population of Aldh1a2+ epicardium-derived cells in the wound area (yellow arrows in Fig. 7a ). In addition, ackr3a was also found to be expressed in Aldh1a2+ epicardium-derived cells and other cells surrounding the wound area (magenta arrows in Fig. 7a ). These results suggest that Adma and Ackr3a might play a role during zebrafish heart regeneration after cryoinjury. Discussion In this study, we applied spatial transcriptomics to analyze the adult zebrafish heart with a focus on cardiac lymphatic vessels. We characterized the heterogeneity of zebrafish cardiac lymphatics, as revealed by both transgenic reporters in vivo and single-cell spatial transcriptomics at the molecular level. We further show that Adm signaling regulates this heterogeneity. Furthermore, the epicardial expression of Ackr3a suggests a previously unrecognized non-cell-autonomous mechanism in cardiac lymphatic development. In addition, we further clarify the roles of pre-existing lymphatics and neo-lymphangiogenesis in heart regeneration using adma and ackr3a mutant models with opposing developmental ventricular lymphatic phenotypes (Fig. 9 ). Finally, we identified downstream targets of Adma-Ackr3a as candidates for future studies. In this manuscript, we utilized an advanced transcriptomic technology, seqFISH, to study spatial relationships of gene expression in zebrafish hearts, focusing on LECs, a rare population in zebrafish hearts (Figs, 1 , 2 and S1 ). Compared to Stereo-seq, seqFISH does not have resolution and sequencing depth limitations, which allows us to study genes expressed at lower levels and rare cell populations that might be difficult to uncover in the previous Stereo-seq dataset of zebrafish hearts 27 . We were able to identify cardiac LECs in heart ventricles and adjacent niche cells expressing vegfc and adma that regulate lymphatic vessel development (Fig. 2 ). Furthermore, we successfully characterized the LEC heterogeneity in WT and heterogeneity change in adma mutant by seqFish (Fig. 4 ). We demonstrated that seqFISH is a powerful tool to study subpopulations in zebrafish tissue, identify potential regulatory relationships between cells, and analyze the heterogeneity within a cell population. In this study, we demonstrated that combining spatial and scRNAseq analysis would help reveal cell-cell communication in zebrafish. ADM and its decoy receptor ACKR3 have known roles in mouse cardiac lymphatic development 5 , 26 , 32 . We used adma and ackr3a mutants to modulate Adm signaling in zebrafish (Figs. 3 and 5 ). Adm-/- mice started to exhibit thoracic edema and hydrops at E13.5 and embryonic death was observed in all Adm-/- mice 19 . Detailed characterization revealed hypoplastic lymphatic sac and permeable lymphatics in Adm-/- mice 46 . Recessive mutation in ADM receptor Calcrl results in hydrops fetalis in human and lymphatic deletion of ADM receptor Calcrl results in embryonic lethality with interstitial edema and dilated lymphatic formation in mice 47 . Consistent with the findings in human and mice, adma mutants showed chronic edema and impacted cardiac lymphatic growth (Figs. 3 and S2 ). However, we observed different phenotypes on BA lymphatic patterning and ventricular lymphatic sprouts and dilation in adma mutants (Fig. 3c, f–i ). It has been suggested that the lymphatic cell population on BA may be more like lymphatic progenitors which remain on the BA until 3-4 mpf when they migrate down to the ventricle. Further studies on the various roles of Adm signaling in different lymphatics will be of interest in the future. Of note, our adma CRISPR mutants deleted most of the adma open reading frame. We cannot rule out that the prohormone precursor PAMP peptide 26 is also deficient. In zebrafish hearts, we observed higher expression of lyve1b reporter in large lymphatic vessels while the growing LEC sprouts only express flt4 but not lyve1b . Interestingly, a distinct population of Lyve1- positive cells is observed in mouse cardiac lymphatics and these Lyve1 low cells penetrate into the myocardium 48 . Notably, we observed a marked up-regulation of lyve1b:dsRed reporter in all ventricular lymphatics in adma mutants (Figs. 3j , S3a and S3b ). In the meantime, accompanied with upregulation of lyve1b:dsRed reporter, ventricular lymphatics are dilated with reduced sprouts at growing ends (Fig. 3c, h, i ). These flt4 + ; lyve1b + LECs appear as soon as they emerged on the ventricle from BA. Taking this together, one interesting possibility that can be investigated in the future is that the Adma signaling inhibits the formation of larger lymphatic branches, and all ventricular lymphatics become lyve1b+ large vessels in adma mutants. Another possibility is that increased lyve1b expression could be a compensatory response to the lymphatic defects in adma mutants. It was reported recently that Lyve1 is increased in cardiac lymphatics after transaortic constriction (TAC) model of pressure overload-induced cardiac hypertrophy and heart failure 49 . However, the impaired ability to clear immune cells in adma mutants indicates that this compensatory dilation is still insufficient in restoring proper lymphatic function. Our finding is consistent with other studies where dilated cardiac lymphatic vessels were insufficient to promote immune-attraction and lymphatic transportation in post-TAC mice hearts 49 . To further illustrate the molecular mechanism of Adm in regulating cardiac lymphatic development, we perform seqFISH in adma mutant and WT sibling hearts. We observed LEC heterogeneity in zebrafish hearts and a reduction of LEC_4, a LEC subpopulation with higher lymphatic gene expression in adma mutants (Fig. 4c, d ). Interestingly, most of those higher expressed lymphatic genes including lyve1b were upregulated in other LEC subpopulations, which may suggest gene expression changes to compensate for lymphatic defects in adma mutants. It will be interesting to further investigate the cell identity of LEC_4 and other LEC subpopulations in zebrafish hearts and the molecular mechanism of Adm on regulating LEC heterogeneity in the future. Previous studies demonstrated that ACKR3 plays a cell-autonomous role in regulating cardiac lymphatic development in mice 32 , 48 . In contrast to mice, ackr3a is expressed in cells adjacent to LECs in the epicardium and the epicardium-specific overexpression of ackr3a showed a non-cell autonomous role of Ackr3a in regulating cardiac lymphatic in zebrafish (Fig. 5 ). Similar to mice, Ackr3a is likely also to function as a decoy receptor to regulate Adm signaling in cardiac lymphatic development. We generated adma; ackr3a double mutants to investigate the epistatic relationship between adma and ackr3a . Most of the cardiac phenotypes (including BA dilation, BA to ventricle ratio, ventricle width to height ratio), BA lymphatic defects, ventricular lymphatic dilation and reduction of ventricular lymphatic sprouts in adma; ackr3a double mutants are similar to the phenotypes of adma single mutants, (Fig. 6 ). These results indicates that adma is epistatic to ackr3a . In previous reports, LEC-specific KO of ACKR3 did not result in significant defects in cardiac lymphatics under physiological conditions 48 or in ear skin lymphatic postnatally 50 . It is possible that ACKR3 has injury specific or tissue/organ specific functions in different lymphatic beds. Our findings of the epicardial Ackr3 roles in zebrafish cardiac lymphatics suggests another possibility of non-cell autonomous role of Ackr3 in regulating Adm availability. It has been reported that Ackr3 can also function as a decoy receptor for Cxcl12 and Cxcr4 in vitro and in mice 50 , 51 . The most parsimonious interpretation of our data is that adma acts downstream of ackr3a , though it is possible that Ackr3a can also acts through Cxcl12-Cxcr4a or other signaling pathways in zebrafish in cardiac lymphatics or different lymphatic beds. The contribution of cardiac lymphatic vessels to post-injury cardiac repair remains controversial in mammals. It has been demonstrated that pro-lymphangiogenesis VEGF-C treatment promoted resolution of cardiac inflammation and improved cardiac function in post-MI mice 4 , 52 . However, no change in infarct scar size was observed in animals treated with AAV based gene delivery of VEGF-C 52 . Another study blocked neolymphangiogenesis by genetic ablation of Vegfr3 or both Vegf-c and Vegf-d, and indicated no effect on cardiac function after MI 9 , Given the limited regenerative capacity of the mammalian heart and the conflicting findings reported in previous studies, further investigations in highly regenerative animal models will be essential to clarify these mechanisms. In our previous study, we demonstrated the importance of pre-existing cardiac lymphatic vessels for proper immune cell clearance and heart regeneration after cryoinjury in zebrafish 6 . In the current study, we used animal models with opposing pre-existing cardiac lymphatic features to examine immune cell clearance. Our results indicate the essential role of pre-existing cardiac lymphatics rather than newly formed lymphatics after injury may play a role in the early immune cell clearance since neolymphangiogenesis does not occur until 14-21 dpc 6 (Fig. 9 ). We further examined whether enhanced neutrophil clearance associated with hypersprouting pre-existing lymphatics in ackr3a mutants could influence regenerative outcomes. However, we did not observe increased CM proliferation or reduced scarring in ackr3a mutant fish during heart regeneration (Fig. S8h-k ). Similarly, mice with LEC -specific deletion of Ackr3 have improved survival rate and cardiac edema but no effect on scar formation post-MI 48 . With a heart regenerative animal model, our results further support that cardiac lymphatic vessels may mainly facilitate cardiac recovery at the early stage and do not necessarily lead to improved regenerative outcomes. However, one limitation in our study is that the ackr3a mutant is a global knockout model and may have potential developmental alterations in other cardiac structures, which may have influenced heart regeneration. Therefore, our results do not exclude the possibility that other approaches enhancing pre-existing cardiac lymphatic vessels and/or lymphatics post-injury might still promote heart regeneration. We also observed sex-specific difference in cardiac lymphatic vessels of zebrafish, especially when analyzing adma mutants. Notably, we observed more severe ventricular lymphatic dilation in female adma mutants (Fig. 3h ). We suspect that this increased lymphatic dilation is correlated with that more female adma mutants showed edema (Fig. S2e ). This may suggest a complex role of Adm in regulating BA and ventricle lymphatic development in a sex-dependent manner. Furthermore, the lymphatic cell populations might be distinct on the BA and ventricle. We found that wildtype male fish have more lumenized lymphatic vessels on ventricle while more female adma mutants develop severe edema. Interestingly, some of the differentially expressed genes we identified in adma and ackr3a mutants are related to permeability and immune responses. How these genes affect the functions of cardiac lymphatic vessels in heart development and regeneration in a sex-specific manner will be further investigated in the future. Spatial data analysis All spatial transcriptomic analyses were performed using R (v4.4.3) in RStudio with the Seurat package (v5.2.1). Raw output files generated by the GenePS platform, including the cell-by-gene expression matrix and cell coordinate metadata, were processed for each experiment. Cell segmentation was initially performed using SG Navigator software, employing a machine learning–based algorithm to segment nuclei based on DAPI staining. For the baseline atlas dataset, segmented nuclei were expanded by 15 pixels ( ~ 1.5 μm) to approximate whole-cell boundaries. In contrast, for the adma loss-of-function dataset, analyses were performed using nuclear segmentation without boundary expansion. Gene identities were decoded with a maximum allowed false discovery rate (FDR) of 5% and assigned to individual segmented cells. For both the baseline atlas and the adma loss-of-function comparison, decoded cell-by-gene expression matrices were transposed and used to generate custom Seurat objects. Cells with zero detected RNA counts were excluded. Normalization and variance stabilization were performed using the SCTransform workflow to minimize technical variability. Principal component analysis (PCA) was conducted, and significant principal components were selected based on elbow plots and PCA heatmap inspection. Uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) was applied using the top 50 principal components to visualize the global transcriptional landscape. For the adma comparative analysis, all tissue sections—including wild-type and adma mutant samples, as well as sections containing ventricle and bulbus arteriosus (BA)—were jointly clustered. This integrated clustering strategy was employed to ensure sufficient representation of lymphatic endothelial cells (LECs). Cell clustering was performed using the FindNeighbors and FindClusters functions with a resolution of 0.2 (and 0.5 for the baseline atlas). Following clustering and cell type annotation, BA-containing sections were excluded, and downstream analyses were restricted to ventricular regions to specifically compare LEC gene expression between WT and adma mutant hearts. Marker gene identification was conducted using the FindAllMarkers function, retaining genes based on average log2 fold change. Spatial coordinates were imported and formatted as a custom dimensionality reduction (reduction key: SG_) and integrated into the Seurat object. Regions of interest (ROI) were defined based on spatial coordinate boundaries, and cells were subset accordingly. Final visualization of spatial gene expression patterns was performed using FeaturePlot, DotPlot and VlnPlot functions. Quantification Ventricle height, width, BA height, width and total length of ventricular lymphatic vessels were measured using straight or freehand tools in ImageJ. Normalized BA or ventricle height and width were calculated by BA or ventricle height and width dividing fish length. Ventricular or bulbus arteriosus size was measured by outlining the whole ventricle area or bulbus arteriosus using freehand selection in ImageJ. Normalized BA or ventricle size was calculated by BA or ventricle size dividing fish length. Ventricular or BA lymphatic coverage was calculated in percentage by first measuring the thresholding of flt4:mCitrine or lyve1b:dsRed channel in ventricle or BA area in ImageJ and dividing the whole ventricle or BA area. The percentage of flattened BA lymphatics was calculated by measuring the thresholding of flt4:mCitrine flattened BA area in ImageJ, dividing the whole thresholding of flt4:mCitrine BA area in ImageJ. The average diameter of lymphatic vessels was calculated by total lymphatic intensity measured by thresholding dividing the total length of lymphatic vessels. The number of Mpx+ neutrophils and IB4+ macrophages was measured using particles measurement tools in ImageJ. Statistical analysis All statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism version 10.4.2. Two-sided unpaired t-test, One-way ANOVA with Fisher’s LSD post hoc test for multiple comparisons and Two-way ANOVA with Fisher’s LSD post hoc test for multiple comparisons were used in comparing male and female WT and mutant samples. A significant difference was considered at p &lt; 0.05. All statistical figures were conducted by Prism. Bar charts were produced with means and standard deviation (SD). Each fish represents an independent biological replicate. At least 3 independent biological replicates were included in each experimental group.
