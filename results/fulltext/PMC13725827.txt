1. INTRODUCTION In the United States, approximately 72.4% of adults aged 20 years or older were classified as overweight or obese from 2021–2023, including 40.3% with obesity ( 1 , 2 ). Beyond cardiometabolic disease, HFD exposure, obesity, and metabolic dysfunction have each been associated with depression, cognitive impairment, and Alzheimer’s disease (AD)-related outcomes ( 3 – 6 ). Midlife obesity is also considered a potentially modifiable risk factor for later-life AD and has been associated with increased risk and greater amyloid and tau pathology in clinical cohorts ( 7 – 10 ). This review treats HFD exposure, overweight, obesity, and metabolic dysfunction as related but distinct conditions. HFD exposure refers to consumption of a diet containing a high proportion of energy from fat, as defined in each study. Although conceptually distinct, HFD and obesity are both closely associated with metabolic disorders, including dyslipidemia and type 2 diabetes ( 3 , 4 ). Importantly, this burden is not limited to individuals with obesity, as over one-third of these deaths occur among individuals who are overweight but not obese ( 1 ). In addition to these systemic and cardiometabolic consequences, growing evidence implicates obesity and HFD in adverse neuropsychiatric and neurodegenerative outcomes. Obesity and HFD are increasingly implicated in depression and Alzheimer’s disease (AD) ( 5 , 6 ). Within this broader relationship, midlife obesity has emerged as a particularly important modifiable risk factor for AD, increasing risk by roughly 60% ( 7 – 9 ), and associating with greater amyloid and tau pathology in clinical cohorts ( 10 ). Dietary patterns may also shape neuropsychiatric risk through partially overlapping mechanisms. Epidemiological studies show that Western, pro-inflammatory diets elevate depression risk, whereas diets rich in fruits, vegetables, and omega-3 fatty acids are protective ( 11 , 12 ). These associations are particularly relevant given the substantial clinical and biological overlap between depression and AD. Depression and AD frequently co-occur, particularly among older adults, and share inflammatory, metabolic, and neuroendocrine pathways ( 7 , 13 , 14 ). Cognitive changes are also a prominent feature of chronic depression and several neuropsychiatric disorders across the life span. Taken together, these clinical and biological overlaps suggest that the conditions may converge through partially shared mechanisms. Therefore, from a biomechanistic perspective, shared underlying pathways between them are plausible and warrant systematic examination. However, this growing mechanistic overlap has not yet translated into coordinated therapeutic strategies. Integrated interventions targeting obesity-related AD and depression remain limited. This systematic review synthesizes evidence that HFD exposure, obesity, and metabolic dysfunction involve multiple interacting mechanisms spanning inflammatory and glial signaling, metabolic and hormonal dysregulation, and gut–brain axis dysfunction. These processes may converge on impaired neuroplasticity and synaptic function across depression-related and cognitive outcomes. In cognitive impairment and AD-related pathology, additional vascular, mitochondrial, and neurodegenerative processes may further contribute. Importantly, our review identifies neuroinflammation as one critical component of this broader network rather than an assumed unifying mechanism. Building on this framework, we also emphasize key knowledge gaps and translational opportunities that may inform more targeted prevention and treatment strategies. To support this synthesis, we employed an AI-assisted, PRISMA-guided systematic approach, as summarized in Fig. 1 and described in detail in Supplemental Material S1 . Existing conceptual frameworks often emphasize individual axes such as immunometabolic signaling, insulin resistance, or gut–brain dysfunction. The present review instead integrates these mechanisms within an evidence-calibrated framework that distinguishes HFD exposure, obesity, and metabolic dysfunction as separate conditions and depression-related outcomes, cognitive impairment, and AD-related pathology as distinct endpoints. 2. METHODOLOGY 2.1 Review Design and Reporting This review was conducted as a full systematic review, reported in accordance with PRISMA 2020. It incorporated pre-specified eligibility criteria, a documented search and screening strategy, structured data extraction, a risk-of-bias appraisal for each included study, and an explicit synthesis method. A central methodological feature was a large language model (LLM)-assisted workflow that used the literature-review platform Elicit ( www.elicit.com ), which supported the initial literature search, abstract-level screening, and structured data extraction under human-defined criteria. All screening criteria were established and approved in advance by the lead authors, who also verified each AI-derived claim against the original source before including it in the final synthesis. 2.2 Terminology and Scope Throughout this review, we distinguish three related but non-interchangeable constructs. High-fat diet (HFD) denotes the dietary exposure itself and is used specifically when reporting findings from experimental studies in which fat content and diet composition were directly manipulated and measured, predominantly in animal models. Obesity refers to a clinically or epidemiologically assessed adiposity phenotype, commonly defined by measures such as BMI, waist circumference, or body-fat percentage. Because dietary composition is rarely measured with comparable precision in human cohorts and clinical studies, findings from these studies are described in terms of obesity or adiposity when those are the phenotypes directly assessed, rather than inferring HFD exposure. In experimental studies, we retain ‘HFD exposure’ as the default term, but use ‘diet-induced obesity’ or ‘obesity’ when the study explicitly demonstrates diet-induced weight gain or increased adiposity, since metabolic dysfunction may arise before, alongside, or independently of measurable weight gain. Metabolic dysfunction is used as an umbrella term for downstream measured physiological abnormalities, including insulin resistance, dyslipidemia, altered glucose regulation, systemic inflammation, or related metabolic disturbances. It is treated as distinct from both the dietary exposure and the adiposity phenotype itself. Type 2 diabetes frequently co-occurs with obesity, but represents a distinct metabolic condition and should be described separately when study design and reporting permit. Within the obesity phenotype, we further distinguish between metabolically healthy and metabolically unhealthy obesity, because individuals with comparable adiposity may differ substantially in insulin sensitivity, lipid regulation, blood pressure, and inflammatory tone ( 15 ). Only the metabolically unhealthy phenotype carries the metabolic dysfunction defined above, through which most of the mechanisms reviewed here are proposed to act; the two phenotypes should therefore not be assumed to confer equivalent neuropsychiatric or cognitive risk ( 16 ). Where a study characterized its animals or participants metabolically rather than by adiposity alone, we report that characterization; as noted in the Discussion (Conditional Modeling Findings), however, this distinction is drawn in almost none of the studies reviewed here, which is itself a substantive limitation of the current evidence base. We apply comparable precision to cognitive and Alzheimer’s disease (AD)-related outcomes, distinguishing four related but separable constructs. Cognitive impairment refers to measurable deficits in behavioral or functional performance — e.g., on memory, learning, or executive-function tasks — as assessed in mechanistic, largely animal, studies. Cognitive decline is reserved for demonstrated deterioration over time, particularly in longitudinal studies. AD-related pathology refers to molecular or structural changes associated with AD, such as amyloid-β accumulation, tau pathology, and neurodegeneration, which may be present with or without accompanying behavioral cognitive impairment; findings reported purely in these molecular/structural terms are described using “AD-related pathology” rather than “cognitive impairment” or “AD” throughout this review. Clinically diagnosed AD is reserved for human disease meeting established diagnostic criteria, for the conventional naming of specific transgenic or knock-in animal disease models (e.g., “AD mouse models”), and for discussion of wider translational implications in the Introduction and Discussion. Behavioral or molecular changes observed in preclinical studies, therefore, do not, by themselves, indicate clinically diagnosed AD; consistent with this convention, the Results section below describes mechanistic, behavioral, and pathological findings using “cognitive impairment” and “AD-related pathology” as appropriate, rather than “AD.” 2.3 Information Sources and Search Strategy Elicit searched the Semantic Scholar and OpenAlex corpus (approximately 138 million peer-reviewed records, aggregating 500+ publishers and databases including PubMed, Wiley, Springer Nature, Taylor &amp; Francis, SAGE, and BMJ Journals). Two structured research questions were run as separate searches: (RQ1) the mechanistic causal link between high-fat diet (HFD), obesity, and depression; and (RQ2) the neurobiological mechanisms by which HFD contributes to cognitive decline in Alzheimer’s disease. A third supplementary search was run, focused specifically on the role of adipokines, exploring how variations in ghrelin and other adipokines’ signaling pathways contributed to the neurobiological mechanisms underlying depression and depression-like behaviors in preclinical and clinical research. No date restriction was applied; searches captured all indexed records meeting the eligibility criteria below at the time of the search. 2.4 Eligibility Criteria Screening criteria were drafted by Elicit against each research question, then manually reviewed, edited, and approved by the lead author before screening began. For RQ1, eligible studies had to: define HFD exposure operationally (≥30% of calories from fat); measure depression as a primary or secondary outcome using a validated clinical scale (e.g., PHQ-9, BDI, MADRS) or an established animal-model behavioral test (e.g., forced swim, sucrose preference, sociability); use an interventional or observational design of adequate duration (≥7 days for interventional studies); report mechanistic data (biological pathway, biomarker, imaging, inflammatory, or microbiome evidence); and focus primarily on dietary exposure rather than pharmacological or surgical intervention. For RQ2, eligible studies required a clinically diagnosed or validated animal model of Alzheimer’s disease (rather than healthy aging or non-Alzheimer’s dementia); an HFD with≥35% calories from fat; a controlled study design; a cognitive performance outcome measure; and reported neurobiological mechanistic data. 2.5 Study Selection Applying these criteria, Elicit retrieved 499 candidate records for RQ1 and 495 for RQ2, screened at the abstract level. Following screening, 107 papers (RQ1) and 85 papers (RQ2) were retained as eligible. All eligible records were then carried forward into structured data extraction. 2.6 Data Extraction For each eligible paper, Elicit extracted pre-specified data under lead-author-authored prompts covering exposure, study design/model, mechanistic pathways, outcome methods, effect direction and magnitude, causal-evidence indicators, and reported limitations. This yielded 963 data points from the 107 RQ1 papers and 850 from the 85 RQ2 papers. A 15% sample of AI-extracted claims was subsequently checked by the lead author against the original source text as an additional quality-assurance step. This extraction-level quality-assurance sample was separate from source verification of report-derived claims incorporated into the final synthesis, described below. 2.7 Risk-of-Bias Assessment All 287 appraised records (277 systematically extracted plus 10 subsequently sourced papers; Supplemental Materials S2 and S4 ) were evaluated using an adapted Office of Health Assessment and Translation (OHAT) Risk of Bias framework. OHAT was selected over SYRCLE and the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale because the corpus included animal, human, and mixed-evidence designs. The 11 standard OHAT questions were consolidated into seven domains ( Table 1 ) by grouping closely related items, including randomization/allocation/blinding and selective reporting/other bias. This adaptation retained coverage of selection, confounding, performance, attrition, detection, and reporting bias while allowing consistent rating across heterogeneous mechanistic studies. Each domain was rated “++”, “+”, “−”, “−−”, or “NR” (not reported) from full text where available. Full text was obtained from the publisher, an open-access repository or institutional mirror, or a PDF retrieved from library services by the lead author; where no channel provided full text, the paper was conservatively rated from its abstract and flagged accordingly. Domain ratings were combined into an overall tier: Tier I (low RoB) required at least 5 of 7 domains rated “+”/“++”, none rated “−”/“−−”, and at most one “NR”; Tier III (high RoB/indeterminate) was assigned where 2 or more domains were rated “−−” or 5 or more were “NR”; all other rating patterns were classified Tier II (some concerns). Of 287 papers, 163 (56.8%) were rated Tier I, 91 (31.7%) Tier II, and 33 (11.5%) Tier III; by evidence type, 69% of preclinical studies reached Tier I compared with 3% of purely human studies. The lead author (KKM) conducted two independent rounds of manual quality-assurance spot checks, reviewing 15% of ratings against the original full texts before finalization. Full methodology and per-domain rating rules are reported in Supplemental Material S2 ; per-study ratings are reported in Supplemental Material S3 . 2.8 Data Synthesis Because Elicit can process 80 sources per report, eligible sources were ranked against the approved screening criteria and preliminary reports were generated from the 80 highest-ranked sources per query. The lead author verified every report-derived claim against its cited source, independently re-read the source material, and incorporated the final human-authored synthesis. Risk-of-bias tiers were then used to calibrate strength-of-evidence language in the Results and Discussion . 3. RESULTS 3.1 Overview of included studies This results section draws on the deduplicated 277-paper corpus comprising 104 depression-focused, 93 cognitive-impairment-focused, and 80 ghrelin/adipokine-signaling-focused papers that subsequently underwent formal risk-of-bias appraisal; the appraisal corpus ( Supplemental Material S2 ) also includes 10 further papers sourced during drafting/revision to fill specific citation gaps not captured by the original extraction, for a total appraised corpus of 287 papers (see Supplemental Material S2 , Section 3.3 ); a full list of included papers is provided in the supplemental material S4 . Within the thematic extraction datasets, direct human evidence was sparse: only 3 of the 104 depression-focused papers were human studies (two prospective cohort analyses and one longitudinal cohort study, with sample sizes of 2,572 and 3,331 reported in two of the three), and none of the 93 cognitive-impairment-focused papers were human studies; across the full 287-paper risk-of-bias corpus, approximately 10% were purely human and a further 8% mixed-evidence designs (see Summary of Risk of Bias appraisal , below). The three human depression studies consistently implicated inflammatory mediators (interleukin-6, C-reactive protein, tumor necrosis factor) as mediators of the diet-depression association, with one study additionally demonstrating a dose-response relationship between ultra-processed food intake and incident depression after adjustment for multiple confounders. The included studies were heterogeneous in design. Across the depression-focused papers, 90% used animal models, with HFD exposure durations ranging from under 4 weeks to over 16 weeks and fat content typically 40–60% of calories. The cognitive-impairment literature employed a wide range of transgenic and knock-in Alzheimer’s disease mouse models alongside wild-type or non-transgenic controls; diet protocols varied in fat composition (typically 40–60% of calories), cholesterol content, and fat source, with HFD duration ranging from 8 weeks to over 15 months and age at diet onset ranging from weaning to late adulthood. The ghrelin/adipokine-signaling stream comprised a mix of animal, human, and mixed-evidence designs, predominantly testing pharmacological, genetic, or endogenous-biomarker manipulation of the ghrelin, leptin, or adiponectin systems, alongside a smaller subset of direct dietary-intervention studies. 3.2 Summary of Risk of Bias (RoB) appraisal All 277 systematically-extracted papers underlying the three literature streams (depression, cognitive impairment, and ghrelin/adipokine signaling), together with 10 further papers subsequently sourced during drafting/revision to fill specific citation gaps not captured by the original extraction, underwent a formal risk-of-bias appraisal using the OHAT tool (full-text-based for the majority; abstract-based with conservative rating for papers where full text could not be retrieved, flagged accordingly). Full methodology and the post-hoc addition process are reported in Supplemental Material S2 ; per-study ratings are reported in Supplemental Material S3 . Overall, 163 of 287 papers (56.8%) were rated Tier I (low risk of bias), 91 (31.7%) Tier II (some concerns), and 33 (11.5%) Tier III (high risk of bias/indeterminate). Tier I representation varied by stream: depression 75/105 (71.4%), cognitive impairment 61/102 (59.8%), and ghrelin/adipokine signaling 27/80 (33.8%) — the last also carrying the largest share of Tier III ratings (17/80, 21.3%). By evidence type, 69% of preclinical (animal) studies were rated Tier I, compared with 3% of purely human studies and none of the mixed-evidence papers, reflecting the corpus’s overall composition of approximately 82% animal, 10% human, and 8% mixed-evidence designs. At the OHAT domain level, dietary or pharmacological exposure was explicitly characterized in 189 papers (65.9%) and a validated outcome measure was named in 242 (84.3%), supporting confidence in what was measured and how. Confounding control was addressed in 248 papers (86.4%). Randomization and/or blinding, by contrast, were reported in only 153 of 287 papers (53.3%) — the remaining 46.7% could not be assessed on this domain even from the full text, because the papers themselves do not state it — and statistical/causal-inference rigor was self-flagged as a limitation in 53 papers (18.5%). The ghrelin/adipokine stream’s comparatively weaker profile reflects a greater proportion of studies testing pharmacological, genetic, or endogenous biomarker manipulation of the ghrelin/leptin/adiponectin system rather than a direct dietary/HFD arm, along with a larger share of narrative reviews and conference abstract records than the depression or cognitive impairment streams. 3.3 Mechanistic pathways linking high-fat diet to depression Thematic analysis of the extracted mechanistic data (104 papers) identified four recurring pathway categories; most papers reported evidence for more than one, and the totals below reflect all pathways reported across studies rather than mutually exclusive categories ( Table 2 ). Among the 98 depression papers with an extractable causal-evidence statement, temporal precedence of dietary exposure over depressive-like outcomes was documented in 94 (95.9%), reversibility of the outcome following removal of exposure or a targeted intervention in 37 (37.8%), and a dose-response relationship in 43 (43.9%). Neuroinflammatory evidence centered on elevated pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, TNF-α, IL-6) ( 17 – 20 ) [all tier I], ( 21 ) [tier II]; activation of microglia and astrocytes ( 22 – 25 ) (all tier I); and upregulation of NF-κB and TLR4 signaling ( Fig 3 ) ( 26 – 28 ) [all tier I] ( 29 ) [tier II], reported across the hippocampus, prefrontal cortex, nucleus accumbens, and amygdala ( 17 , 18 , 23 ) [all tier I]. Several interventions that suppressed these pathways — IKKβ inhibition ( 18 , 30 ) [all tier I], prebiotic-mediated reduction of TNF-α and IL-1β ( 20 , 31 ) [all tier I], and suppression of TLR4/NF-κB signaling by the GLP-1 agonist liraglutide ( 32 ) — were associated with reversal or prevention of depressive-like behavior, and pharmacological reversal of glial activation with fluorocitrate abolished HFD-induced behavioral changes in one study ( 24 ) [Tier I]. In another, proinflammatory cytokine induction was reported as early as two days after HFD initiation, preceding the onset of depressive-like behavior at five days ( 20 ) [Tier I]. Gut-brain axis findings linked HFD to decreased microbial diversity ( 33 ) [Tier I], reduced beneficial taxa such as Akkermansia and Bacteroidetes and increased pro-inflammatory taxa such as Firmicutes ( 34 , 35 ) [all tier I], alongside loss of tight-junction proteins ( 24 , 35 , 36 ) [all tier I], increased intestinal permeability, and lipopolysaccharide translocation into systemic circulation ( Fig 2A ) ( 36 , 37 ) [Tier I]( 36 ). Antibiotic ( 38 ) [Tier I], probiotic ( 39 ) [Tier I], prebiotic ( 31 ) [Tier I], and fecal-transplant studies provided the most direct causal support — including one study in which fecal transplant from antibiotic-treated donor mice transferred behavioral and metabolic improvements to germ-free recipients ( 38 ) [Tier I] — though findings on the necessity of specific bacterial taxa were mixed, with one study finding no effect of antibiotic-induced microbiota change on anhedonia despite pronounced compositional shifts ( 40 ) [Tier I], while another found antibiotic-mediated depletion prevented HFD-induced anhedonia ( 41 ) [Tier I]. More granular metabolite-level evidence linked HFD-driven increases in acetate-producing taxa ( Bacteroides acidifaciens, B. dorei ) to reduced dysbiosis, reduced intestinal permeability, and improved behavior ( 31 ) [Tier I], reductions in anti-inflammatory bile-acid metabolites to hippocampal microglial activation ( 42 ) [Tier I], and antibiotic-induced microbiota depletion to altered fecal short-chain fatty acids, glucose, and amino acids correlating with circulating leptin and behavioral change ( 41 ) [Tier I]. A further study reported a bidirectional relationship in which psychological distress itself shaped diet and microbiota composition ( 43 ) [tier III], and another found that time-restricted feeding prevented depressive-like behavior by suppressing HFD-induced microbiota changes ( 44 ) [Tier II — single study]. Metabolic and neuroendocrine evidence implicated disrupted leptin/insulin signaling and its relation to anhedonia ( 41 ) [tier I] ( 45 ) [tier II], and HPA-axis hyperactivity with elevated corticosterone ( 46 – 49 ) [mostly tier I, one tier II]. Downstream of these changes, several intracellular signaling cascades were implicated — AMPK/mTOR, cAMP/PKA/CREB (via palmitic-acid-driven FFAR1 upregulation and PDE4A activity), PPARγ, GSK3β, and Wnt/CaMKII ( 50 – 55 ) [mostly tier I, with tier II support for GSK3β] — several of which also reversed depressive-like phenotypes when directly targeted: IGF-1 administration, rapamycin, genetic PDE4A deficiency, and neuronal PPARγ overexpression ( 51 , 52 , 56 ) [all tier I]. Central insulin resistance and desensitized hippocampal leptin signaling were also reported as recurring correlates ( 38 , 57 , 58 ) [all tier I]. Neuroplasticity-related findings ( Fig 4 and 5 ) centered on reduced BDNF ( 30 , 50 , 59 ) (all tier I) ( 60 ) [tier II], CREB ( 61 , 62 ) [Tier I]], and synaptic proteins including PSD-95, synaptophysin, SNAP-25, and neurotrophin-3 ( 28 , 47 ) [all tier I], decreased neurogenesis ( 25 , 35 ) [all tier I], and altered dendritic morphology ( 25 , 63 ) [both tier I], ( 49 ) (tier II), alongside serotonergic disruption via BCAA-tryptophan competition ( 64 ) [Tier I]), noradrenergic ( 65 ) [Tier I], dopaminergic ( 46 , 66 ) [all tier I] ( 67 ) (tier II), ( 68 ) [tier III], and glutamatergic/GABAergic disruption ( 47 , 53 , 69 , 70 ) (all tier I). Onset was reported as early as two weeks of exposure and persisting through nine months in one study ( 50 , 61 ) [all tier I], with partial reversibility following pharmacological (aspirin ( 30 ), metformin ( 64 ), riluzole ( 70 ) [all tier I]), or dietary intervention (omega-3 ( 71 ) [tier I]; ketogenic ( 49 ) [tier II]). Disrupted neuropeptide Y signaling was also reported alongside these monoamine and amino-acid-transmitter changes ( 72 ) [Tier I]. Across these pathways, depressive-like behavior and its molecular correlates were most frequently localized to the hippocampus ( 25 , 35 , 47 , 73 ), the prefrontal cortex ( 28 , 52 , 63 , 74 ), the nucleus accumbens ( 18 , 59 , 70 ), the hypothalamus ( 51 , 58 , 72 ) and the striatum/ventral tegmental area ( 59 , 67 ). 3.4 Conditional modeling findings Beyond these four pathways, the extracted data identified several conditional/moderating factors: co-occurring stress exposure, which variably potentiated depressive-like behavior ( 59 , 75 , 76 ) [tier I] or, in a subset of studies, was itself attenuated by high-fat feeding ( 77 , 78 ) [tier I]; developmental timing, with greater vulnerability following maternal, juvenile, or adolescent exposure than adult-onset exposure ( 49 , 61 , 63 , 79 ) [tier I]; sex, with divergent behavioral and molecular responses between males and females, including opposite-direction effects in some paradigms ( 80 – 83 ) [tier I]; duration of exposure, with effects emerging within days and intensifying with prolonged feeding ( 17 , 20 , 51 ) [all tier I]; genetic background, with strain- and gene-dependent susceptibility ( 21 , 51 , 58 , 84 ) [all tier I]; and dietary fat type, with saturated fat more consistently associated with depressive-like behavior than isocaloric monounsaturated fat ( 18 , 85 ) [tier I]. Direct evidence for fat-type specificity beyond this saturated/unsaturated contrast came from n-3 polyunsaturated fatty acid (PUFA) manipulation studies: genetically increasing n-3 PUFA levels reversed lipopolysaccharide-induced depressive-like behavior and normalized microglial M1/M2 polarization ( 86 ) [tier II], while chronic dietary n-3 PUFA deficiency itself produced a chronic-stress-like phenotype with HPA-axis hyperactivity and prefrontal atrophy, reversible by adrenalectomy or metyrapone ( 48 ) [tier I]. Combined chronic stress and HFD exposure additionally produced antidepressant-treatment-resistant phenotypes in a subset of studies, with fluoxetine rendered ineffective ( 75 ) [tier I], and vicarious social-defeat stress combined with a Western-style diet during adolescence reduced reward sensitivity and antidepressant efficacy in adulthood ( 76 ) [tier I]— together suggesting HFD-related neuroinflammation may itself contribute to treatment resistance in stress-exposed animals. 3.5 Mechanistic pathways linking high-fat diet to cognitive impairment The same thematic approach applied to the 93 cognitive-impairment-focused papers identified four recurring pathway categories, again not mutually exclusive ( Table 3 ). Among the 99 papers with an extractable causal-evidence statement, temporal precedence was documented in 82 (82.8%), reversibility in 37 (37.4%), and a dose-response relationship in 5 (5.1%). Evidence on amyloid-β was inconsistent across studies: several reported HFD-exacerbated amyloid plaque and oligomer burden ( 87 ) [tier I], ( 88 ) [tier I], attributed in part to increased β-secretase (BACE1) activity and decreased insulin-degrading-enzyme expression ( 89 ) [tier I] ( 90 ) [tier II], to reduced transthyretin-mediated amyloid-β clearance ( 90 ) [tier II]— single study]) and to enhanced amyloid-precursor-protein processing via dephosphorylation/SUMOylation ( 91 ) [Tier II — single study], while others found no effect on parenchymal amyloid, plaque burden, or tau pathology despite clear HFD-associated cognitive impairment ( 92 – 94 ) [tier I]; ( 95 ) [tier II], suggesting the association is model-, diet-, and protocol-dependent ( 96 ) [Tier III — single review study]. Tau-pathology evidence more consistently implicated an SGK1-GSK-3β-mediated phosphorylation pathway linked to glucocorticoid and hyperglycemic signaling ( 97 ) [Tier I], with one study reporting HFD-driven tau changes independent of peripheral insulin resistance ( 98 ) [Tier II — single study]. Mitochondrial dysfunction — reduced spare respiratory capacity, altered morphology, and impaired mitophagy ( 99 – 102 ) [all Tier I] — and synaptic/dendritic loss, including reduced synaptophysin ( 102 ), PSD-95 ( 103 ) [Tier I], and downregulated neurite-projection genes ( 104 ) ( Fig 4 and 5C ), were reported more consistently and correlated directly with memory impairment, including in one study where an 8-week HFD produced hallmark neurodegenerative pathology (amyloid and tau accumulation) without overt systemic hypercholesterolemia ( 101 ). Neuroinflammatory changes — microglial activation, cytokine and inflammatory-marker upregulation ( 87 , 88 , 92 ) [all tier I], ( 49 ) [tier II]; and complement-mediated synaptic pruning ( 105 ) [Tier I] — were among the most consistently reported findings, frequently present independent of amyloid or tau burden ( 92 ) [Tier I]. Proposed mediators included T-cell-driven microglial lipid-droplet accumulation impairing plaque consolidation ( 49 ), a C/EBPβ/asparagine-endopeptidase signaling cascade driving both amyloid and tau pathology, attenuated by aspirin in one study ( 95 , 106 ) [all tier II], TREM2 upregulation correlating with plaque burden ( 107 ) [tier I], ( 104 ) [tier III], and astrocyte-derived ApoE/cholesterol dysregulation ( 108 – 110 ) [Tier I], with the ApoE4 isoform linked to amyloid aggregation in human evidence ( 109 ). A direct causal chain has also been reported linking HFD exposure to astrocytic dysfunction: HFD rapidly elevates circulating saturated fatty acids, disrupting the blood–brain barrier and triggering local cytokine production that activates astrocytic TLR–MyD88 and NF-κB/IKKβ signaling within 1–3 days of onset, preceding measurable weight gain, with astrocyte-specific IKKβ/MyD88 knockout studies indicating this inflammatory activation is causally upstream of diet-induced obesity rather than secondary to it ( 111 – 113 ). Metabolic and hormonal evidence centered on impaired brain insulin signaling, including HFD-driven suppression of adiponectin receptor 1 (AdipoR1) and AMPK signaling ( Fig 5B ) ( 103 ) [Tier I] and reversal of amyloid and memory deficits with exogenous insulin ( 114 ) [Tier III — single study] or exercise ( 115 ) [tier I] ( 109 ) [tier II], lending itself to a proposed “type 3 diabetes” classification ( 99 ) [Tier I], alongside HPA-axis/glucocorticoid-driven NLRP3 inflammasome and HMGB1 activation ( 116 ) [Tier I], and FFAR3-mediated glucose dysregulation whose genetic ablation prevented cognitive deficits under HFD in one model ( 117 ) [Tier I]. Oxidative-stress evidence implicated impaired Nrf2 antioxidant signaling via reduced Akt activation ( 93 , 103 ) [all Tier I], NADPH-oxidase upregulation specific to transgenic AD models ( 118 ) [Tier I], and non-heme iron accumulation ( Fig 5A ), with iron chelation reported to reverse cognitive deficits in one study ( 119 ) [Tier II]. Gut-brain and vascular evidence linked HFD to depletion of beneficial gut taxa such as Akkermansia muciniphila , increased permeability, and systemic endotoxemia signaling through TLR4 ( 120 ) [Tier I], and blood-brain-barrier breakdown marked by loss of the tight-junction protein occluding ( 89 ) [Tier I]. Microbiota-transplant studies provided direct causal support, with transplantation of HFD-associated microbiota conferring cognitive deficits to chow-fed recipients in one study ( 120 ) [Tier I], and platelet-activation studies linked HFD to amyloid fibrillization and cerebral vascular permeability ( 121 ) [Tier II]. Blood-brain-barrier permeability changes were reported to precede measurable cognitive impairment by several weeks in one study ( 89 ) [Tier I] and to emerge within two days of HFD initiation in another ( 20 ) [Tier I]. 3.6 Mechanistic pathways linking high-fat diet to ghrelin, leptin, and adiponectin signaling The 80 papers in the ghrelin/adipokine-signaling stream were not amenable to the same four-pathway thematic categorization applied to the depression and cognitive-impairment literatures above, because this stream predominantly tests pharmacological, genetic, or endogenous-biomarker manipulation of the ghrelin, leptin, or adiponectin systems directly, rather than extracting a dietary-exposure-to-outcome pathway from a common HFD paradigm; the mechanistic findings are therefore summarized narratively below. Ghrelin exerted complex, context-dependent effects on depressive-like behavior and cognition. In adult, non-stressed rodents, ghrelin or growth-hormone-secretagogue-receptor (GHSR) agonism reduced depressive-like behavior and enhanced hippocampal neurogenesis via PI3K/Akt and AMPK-autophagy signaling and BDNF/CREB pathways ( 122 , 123 ) [all tier I], while suppressing inflammatory signaling. In juvenile or acutely stressed models, by contrast, studies reported paradoxical, depressogenic effects of exogenous ghrelin, or reduced stress-induced neuroinflammation and depressive-like behavior following GHSR disruption, indicating that the direction of ghrelin’s effect on mood is state- and developmental-stage-dependent rather than uniformly protective or harmful. Leptin dysregulation was consistently linked to atypical depression characterized by hyperphagia and weight gain, with central leptin resistance — rather than absolute circulating leptin level — suspected as the operative mechanism ( 124 ) [Tier II — single study]. Adiponectin showed the most consistent inverse association with depression across studies ( 125 , 126 ) [all tier II], with experimental evidence for antidepressant-like effects via AdipoR1/AdipoR2 agonism in serotonergic and hippocampal circuits ( 127 , 128 ) [all tier I]. In the cognitive-impairment literature, impaired adipokine signaling — particularly reduced adiponectin receptor 1 (AdipoR1) activity — disrupted AMPK signaling ( Fig 3 ), increased oxidative stress and insulin resistance ( Fig 5 ), and accelerated HFD-related cognitive impairment ( 103 ) [Tier I]. Glucocorticoid-driven activation of hippocampal inflammasome pathways (NLRP3, HMGB1) further compromised memory ( 116 ) [Tier I]. Collectively, adipokine pathways are biologically relevant and potentially targetable — particularly ghrelin and leptin signaling in atypical depression, and adiponectin/AdipoR1 signaling across both depression and cognitive impairment — although heterogeneity by sex, age, depression subtype, and adipokine measurement method remains substantial across this literature. 3.7 Shared mechanistic pathways between depression and cognitive impairment Depression-related outcomes, cognitive impairment, and AD-related pathology may share overlapping biological pathways. Across specific preclinical models, even short-term HFD exposure can induce neuroinflammation, microglial activation, synaptic loss, and cognitive impairment—effects that may occur independently of amyloid burden or obesity per se ( 92 , 93 , 105 , 129 ). Chronic neuroinflammation is a recurrent contributor: adipose dysfunction is associated with elevated pro-inflammatory cytokines and microglial activation, which may contribute to both depressive symptoms and AD-related neurodegeneration ( 7 , 130 , 131 ). Insulin resistance provides another mechanistic link. Impaired insulin signaling has been linked to amyloid accumulation and disruption of neurotrophic and synaptic pathways, which are also implicated in mood regulation ( 9 , 130 , 132 ). Gut microbiota dysbiosis further bridges these conditions. Obesity-associated microbial shifts have been associated with increased intestinal permeability, lipopolysaccharide-driven endotoxemia ( Fig 2 ), and alterations in tryptophan–kynurenine metabolism ( 133 ) ( Fig 4A – B ), fostering neuroinflammation, mood dysregulation, and cognitive impairment. Activation of IDO is particularly relevant in this framework: cytokine-induced IDO activity depletes tryptophan needed for serotonin synthesis while increasing neurotoxic kynurenine metabolites ( Fig 4 ), linking immune activation to both depression and neurodegeneration ( 134 ). Adipokine imbalance—especially reduced adiponectin signaling and altered leptin–adiponectin ratios—has also been implicated in shared pathology, with adiponectin pathways emerging as potential therapeutic targets in obesity-related AD ( Fig 5B ) ( 135 ). Additionally, saturated fatty acids and ceramides have been associated with mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress relevant to both mood and cognition ( 133 , 136 ). Consistent with these mechanisms, obesity is recognized by a Lancet commission as a major modifiable dementia risk factor ( 137 ), and diets low in saturated fats and rich in antioxidants and omega-3 fatty acids are associated with lower risk of both depression and cognitive impairment. DISCUSSION 4.1 Mechanistic Pathways Connecting HFD and Depression The reviewed studies identified four closely linked pathways associated with HFD-related depression-like behavior: inflammatory and neuroinflammatory mechanisms; gut-brain axis dysfunction; metabolic, stress-response, and hormonal pathways; and neurotransmitter, brain cell structural changes, and neuroplasticity pathways. Because most mechanistic evidence is preclinical, “depression-like behavior” is used for animal outcomes, whereas “depression” and “depressive symptoms” are reserved for human evidence. 4.1.1 Gut-Brain Axis Dysfunction in Depression Gut-brain axis findings suggest that taxonomic shifts (reduced Akkermansia/Bacteroidetes , increased Firmicutes ) and barrier dysfunction may represent one route from diet to depressive symptoms ( Results ). Intervention studies support a contributory role for microbiota-related pathways, but inconsistent taxon-specific findings and limited metabolite-level evidence caution against premature translation. At the metabolite level, no consistent signature has emerged across HFD models, and the direction and functional consequences of reported changes vary with dietary composition, microbial community, and host phenotype. 4.1.2 Neuroinflammation Mechanisms in Depression Neuroinflammatory findings are consistent with broader neuroimmune models of depression. The temporal ordering of inflammatory signaling prior to behavioral change in several preclinical studies supports an upstream contribution in those models, although translation to human HFD-associated depression remains uncertain. The literature also rarely separates peripheral inflammation from central neuroimmune activation, limiting inference about their temporal and causal relationship. These findings therefore support inflammation as an early and modifiable contributor without establishing it as necessary or sufficient across all models. 4.1.3 Metabolic, Stress and Hormonal Pathways HPA-axis and glucocorticoid-signaling findings place HFD-related depression within a stress-diathesis framework, in which dietary and psychosocial stressors may converge on corticosterone-, BDNF-, and HPA-related pathways. Omega-3-related reversibility further suggests that fatty acid composition, not fat content alone, may modulate this stress-neuroplasticity axis. Non-diet stress and obesity models provide complementary evidence that sustained corticosterone signaling impairs hippocampal function and that reducing corticosterone can normalize related deficits ( 138 , 139 ). Mechanistically, glucocorticoid-receptor activation transrepresses BDNF transcription at promoters I and IV, shifting the TrkB:p75NTR ratio and silencing the functional BDNF response ( 139 ), while chronic corticosterone exposure reduces GluA1 expression and impairs long-term potentiation ( 138 ). Chronic glucocorticoid exposure also reduces hippocampal dendritic spine density and arborization ( 140 ). Recovery and CRHR1-manipulation studies further indicate that these structural and cognitive effects can be reversible. Hippocampal dysfunction may then weaken HPA-axis negative feedback, creating a feed-forward cycle ( 141 ) that may worsen HFD-related disruptions in HPA and BDNF. These signaling pathways may converge on impaired neuronal energy metabolism and structural plasticity rather than represent independent mechanisms. Their context dependence, including variation with dietary fat composition, reinforces the importance of both fat quality and total fat intake. Adipokine pathways provide an additional metabolic link. Central leptin resistance and impaired melanocortin signaling are associated with anhedonia ( 57 , 58 ). Together, these findings support convergence of metabolic, hormonal, and stress-responsive pathways in HFD-related depression. Central receptor responsiveness may therefore be more informative than circulating hormone concentrations alone, which should not be treated as equivalent measures of adipokine function. 4.1.4 Neurotransmitters, Neuronal Signaling to Neuroplasticity These findings support the notion of neuroplasticity impairment as a convergent downstream feature of HFD-related depression, involving BDNF, synaptic proteins, monoaminergic systems, and astrocyte-mediated glutamatergic signaling. Partial reversibility across pharmacological, dietary, and exercise interventions further indicates that these changes are not necessarily fixed. Notably, several of these effects — including BDNF suppression via AMPK/CREB signaling, palmitic-acid-driven FFAR1/PDE4A-mediated synaptic loss, and astrocyte-mediated glutamatergic dysregulation — arise through direct metabolic and energy-sensing routes rather than through inflammatory intermediates, indicating that neuroplasticity impairment in HFD-related depression is not solely an inflammatory epiphenomenon but reflects convergent, partly inflammation-independent mechanisms. Direct evidence for an astrocyte-specific role in HFD-associated depression-like behavior remains limited, so astrocytic effects are best interpreted within the broader cellular network. 4.2 Mechanistic Pathways Connecting HFD and Cognitive Impairment Neuroinflammation was the most consistently reported mechanism in the cognitive-impairment literature and was often present without corresponding amyloid or tau changes ( Results ), indicating that HFD-associated cognitive impairment may involve partly amyloid/tau-independent processes. As in the depression literature, peripheral and central inflammatory contributions were rarely separated. TREM2 upregulation tracked with amyloid burden ( 92 ); elevated inflammatory markers ( 49 , 87 , 88 , 142 ) — illustrated in Fig 2 and 5A — were similarly reported independent of amyloid-β (Aβ) and tau burden ( 92 ), together implying that neuroinflammation constitutes a mechanistically distinct route to cognitive impairment rather than solely a downstream consequence of classical amyloid/tau pathology. These findings support immune mechanisms as contributors to cognitive impairment while also underscoring the uncertain translational efficacy of anti-inflammatory intervention. Astrocytes may play an important modulatory role in this process. Astrocytes are major CNS sources of apolipoprotein E (ApoE) and cholesterol ( 108 , 143 – 149 ). HFD-associated saturated-fat and inflammatory signaling may engage astrocytic TLR–MyD88/NF-κB/IKKβ pathways before measurable weight gain, suggesting an early modulatory role without establishing a universal causal sequence. Altered astrocytic ApoE and lipid handling modulate microglial inflammatory responses ( 108 ) ( Fig 5B ) and amyloid aggregation ( Fig 5C ), with the ApoE4 isoform conferring heightened AD risk ( 148 ). Astrocyte involvement in memory-related ensembles provides an additional rationale for studying these cells in cognitive impairment ( 150 ). HFD-associated glial responses may reflect state-dependent microglial and astrocytic phenotypes rather than uniform activation. Myelin abnormalities reported in some models further suggest possible contributions from the oligodendrocyte lineage to circuit vulnerability, although direct mechanistic evidence remains limited. 4.2.1 Gut–Brain Axis, Barrier, and Vascular Dysfunction Gut-brain and cerebrovascular findings implicate blood-brain-barrier dysfunction as a potential link between HFD and cognitive impairment. Microbiota-transfer experiments further support a contributory role for gut-derived signals ( Results ). Vascular dysfunction, including platelet activation, further promotes cerebral amyloid angiopathy, neuroinflammation, and tau pathology via hyperphosphorylation ( 26 ). More broadly, blood-brain-barrier breakdown, reduced cerebral blood flow, and impaired neurovascular coupling may contribute independently of amyloid and tau pathology, although dedicated vascular-imaging studies were sparse. Together, intestinal and cerebrovascular dysfunction may reduce neural resilience but are unlikely to produce cognitive impairment independently. 4.2.2 Metabolic and Hormonal Pathways This theme encompasses disrupted hormonal signaling and oxidative stress as key links between HFD and cognitive impairment. Brain insulin-signaling findings are consistent with broader links between type 2 diabetes and dementia risk and provide a rationale for testing metabolically targeted therapies in HFD/obesity-related cognitive impairment. Oxidative-stress and iron-accumulation findings were less directly tied to dietary fat than insulin signaling. Mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress also recur across obesity, depression, and cognitive impairment ( 151 , 152 ). Autophagy/mitophagy findings suggest another cross-cutting mechanism, but neither was a primary outcome in the extracted datasets. Endoplasmic reticulum stress was not directly examined. When present, type 2 diabetes may further increase neural vulnerability through chronic hyperglycemia and insulin resistance, which can exacerbate vascular, oxidative, and synaptic dysfunction beyond the effects of adiposity alone. 4.2.3 Neuronal Morphology, Synaptic Dysfunction and Neurodegeneration Pathways Inconsistent amyloid/tau findings suggest that HFD-associated cognitive impairment can occur through partly amyloid/tau-independent mechanisms ( Results ). The SGK1-GSK-3β pathway provides one plausible link between metabolic dysfunction and tau pathology. Cross-study discrepancies likely reflect differences in model, diet composition, and exposure duration, reinforcing the need for standardized HFD protocols. These findings warrant further testing of tau-related pathways without assuming they are the dominant route from diet to cognitive impairment. The consistency of mitochondrial and synaptic findings across models — emerging early and preceding overt metabolic disease ( Results ) — supports a growing view in the neurodegeneration field that bioenergetic failure may be an initiating rather than downstream event in Alzheimer’s-type pathology. This has direct implications for intervention timing: therapies targeting mitochondrial function or mitophagy may need to be administered well before clinical symptom onset to be effective, consistent with the broader shift toward preclinical and prodromal intervention trials in Alzheimer’s disease research. However, synaptic dysfunction is not specific to AD and may occur without increased Aβ or tau burden. Ghrelin effects were strongly context dependent, varying by developmental stage and stress state ( Results ), which cautions against uniformly protective or therapeutic interpretations. Adiponectin showed a more consistent inverse association with depression, but translational relevance remains to be established. Adipokine-related mechanisms in cognitive impairment largely converge on insulin/AMPK and glucocorticoid-inflammasome pathways rather than forming a separate route. Heterogeneity by sex ( 127 , 153 ), age ( 136 ), depression subtype ( 154 ), and adipokine measurement ( 155 ) remains substantial, limiting translational inference. 4.3 Conditional Modeling Findings Moderating factors support a stress-diathesis rather than simple dose-response model, with developmental timing, genetic background, and concurrent stress modifying HFD-related vulnerability. Earlier-life exposures appear particularly consequential, although their mechanisms remain incompletely resolved. Developmental-timing patterns parallel broader stress-neuroscience findings ( 156 , 157 ), suggesting possible overlap with epigenetic and HPA-axis mechanisms. In aged animals, HFD effects were generally reported as additive to baseline age-related change ( 158 ); future studies should distinguish these effects directly. These variables should be interpreted as modifiers of HFD responsiveness rather than evidence of obesity or metabolic dysfunction unless excess adiposity or specific metabolic abnormalities were directly demonstrated. Sex-dependent behavioral and molecular divergence indicates that sex can modify the operative HFD-related mechanism and should therefore be incorporated into adequately powered, pre-specified analyses. Parallel stress literature suggests that hormonal and epigenetic regulation may contribute to these sex-dependent responses ( 159 ). Pooling male and female data without testing sex-dependent effects may therefore obscure mechanistic differences. Because most reviewed studies operationalized HFD exposure rather than a defined metabolic phenotype, the field cannot yet determine the relative contributions of adiposity, specific metabolic dysfunction, and dietary fat composition. Future studies should decouple fat quality from weight gain and directly compare metabolically distinct obesity phenotypes. Early dietary effects should therefore be distinguished from later consequences of obesity or metabolic dysfunction, and total fat content alone is insufficient to characterize exposure. 4.4 Key Evidence Patterns Temporal precedence and reversibility strengthen mechanistic inference within the preclinical models reviewed, but do not establish a uniform causal relationship in humans. Limited dose-response evidence, particularly for cognitive impairment ( Results ), further argues against assuming a simple linear relationship. Across studies linking HFD to depression and cognitive impairment, the most consistent evidence implicates metabolic, inflammatory, gut–brain axis disruption and impaired neuroplasticity, each supported by convergent interventional data. A clear pattern of association emerges, though the evidence base remains largely preclinical. HFD effects are multifactorial, with inflammatory, metabolic, gut-brain, and neuroplasticity changes frequently co-occurring. Neuroinflammation often functions as a hub linking peripheral dysfunction to central synaptic changes and has also been implicated in maternal-HFD-related vulnerability ( 68 ) and in related literature ( 134 , 160 , 161 ). However, metabolic, gut-brain, and neuroplasticity pathways also contribute independently of inflammation. Mendelian randomization findings further support an inflammation-independent association between BMI and specific depressive symptom clusters ( 162 ). Intervention studies provide additional mechanistic support across metabolic targeting (11β-HSD1 inhibition ( 163 ), IGF-1 supplementation ( 56 )), enhancement of neuroplasticity (ketogenic diet ( 49 ), omega-3–mediated BDNF-synaptic marker restoration ( 71 )), inflammatory suppression (GLP-1 agonists ( 32 , 161 ), exercise inhibiting TLR4/NF-κB signaling ( 101 )), and gut–brain modulation (short-chain fatty acids ( 37 , 50 ), prebiotics ( 31 ), time-restricted feeding ( 44 )). Together, these interventions support multiple targetable pathways. Across these interventions, restoration of BDNF signaling and synaptic structure supports neuroplasticity as a convergent downstream endpoint. Here, neuroinflammation is treated as a recurrent point of convergence rather than a universal hub, and neuroplasticity as one important downstream convergence domain rather than a single obligatory endpoint. Null and inconsistent findings were also present, including cognitive impairment without amyloid/tau changes, microbiota alterations without corresponding anhedonia, and heterogeneous adipokine-behavior relationships ( Results ). Because publication and indexing practices likely underrepresent null findings, their true prevalence cannot be inferred from this corpus. 4.5 Shared and Distinct Mechanistic Patterns between Depression and Cognitive Impairment Depression-related and cognitive outcomes share neuroinflammatory, insulin/metabolic, gut-brain, adipokine, and neuroplasticity-related mechanisms, but these pathways are not uniformly shared ( Table 4 ). Amyloid/tau processes are more specific to cognitive impairment and AD-related pathology, whereas monoaminergic and reward-circuit mechanisms are more prominent in depression. This distinction allows the conditional model to accommodate convergent biology without collapsing the outcomes into a single cascade. Evidence therefore supports convergent mechanisms linking HFD exposure to depression-related outcomes and cognitive impairment through interacting pathways rather than a single cause. In specific preclinical models, HFD exposure can rapidly induce peripheral inflammation that precedes behavioral changes, while gut–brain alterations may provide additional microbiome-related immune, metabolic, and neural routes that influence brain function ( Fig 2 ). Concurrently, insulin and adipokine resistance (metabolic dysfunction) and systemic inflammation may converge at the blood–brain barrier (BBB) ( Fig 3 and 5B ), while elevated BCAAs and related metabolic changes may further alter central signaling relevant to neuroplasticity ( Fig 4A – C ). Inflammatory modulation of neurotransmitter systems—particularly serotonin via altered synthesis and competition for brain uptake of precursors by other inflammatory metabolites (e.g., BCAAs)—may further influence BDNF signaling and neurogenesis, potentially limiting adaptive capacity ( Fig 4C ). These interactions are integrated in the Conditional Integrative Model ( Fig 6 ), described below. 4.6 The Conditional Integrative Model: A Molecular Stress-Diathesis Framework The Conditional Integrative Model proposes that neuroinflammatory, metabolic/adipokine, microbiome, and neurotransmitter processes are moderated by stress, genetics, age, sex, developmental timing, and dietary composition. It is conditional because these processes are variably engaged rather than obligate, and integrative because they converge on impaired neuroplasticity and, for cognitive outcomes, AD-related pathology. The model is framed as a molecular extension of stress-diathesis: dietary/metabolic exposures interact with genetic, epigenetic, developmental, and metabolic vulnerabilities rather than acting alone. This formulation anchors the largely preclinical mechanistic evidence to a familiar clinical framework. Related reviews likewise identify BDNF and NF-κB as convergence nodes ( 43 , 124 , 134 , 164 , 165 ). Some changes arise before overt obesity develops, whereas subsequent excess adiposity and metabolic dysfunction may amplify peripheral inflammation, impaired insulin and adipokine signaling, oxidative stress, and neural vulnerability. The relative contribution of each pathway is expected to vary across experimental contexts, with no requirement that all intermediate mechanisms or downstream processes be engaged in a given phenotype. Accordingly, depression-related outcomes, cognitive impairment, and AD-related pathology may share mechanisms without representing a single biological phenotype or obligatory endpoint. We distinguish three levels of mechanistic support: experimentally supported relationships demonstrated by temporal ordering, intervention, or genetic manipulation; convergent evidence supported across studies without direct causal testing; and hypothetical cross-domain connections lacking tests of temporal sequence, necessity, or sufficiency. This claim-level framework complements, rather than duplicates, the study-level RoB tiers. Claims supported predominantly by Tier I studies are weighted more strongly within each support level. No single convergence node has been established as a dominant upstream mechanism across models. However, causal hierarchies remain unclear (e.g., does inflammation come first and trigger metabolic dysfunction, or do they both occur in parallel ab initio ?), evidence is largely preclinical and sex-biased, and translational consistency is limited. Longitudinal and comparative human studies are needed to validate and refine this integrative model. Indeed, direct human evidence within the papers underlying this review was limited to three cohort studies in the depression literature and none in the cognitive-impairment literature (see Results , Overview of included studies), underscoring that the integrative model proposed here is substantially inferential and preclinically derived rather than directly demonstrated in human populations. 4.7 Comparison with Alternative Conceptual Frameworks and the Molecular Basis of the Conditional Integrative (Stress-Diathesis) Model Several existing frameworks emphasize individual components of this relationship. The metabolic/insulin-resistance hypothesis centers on impaired central insulin signaling and neuronal glucose utilization ( 166 , 167 ). Gut-brain frameworks prioritize microbiota-brain communication ( 168 ). Allostatic-load models emphasize chronic physiological wear ( 169 , 170 ). An immunometabolic model integrates inflammatory and neuroendocrine dysregulation with atypical depressive symptoms ( 171 ). Each foregrounds a particular axis — metabolic, microbial, allostatic, or immunometabolic. The Conditional Integrative Model instead treats these axes as interacting contributors that converge on neuroplasticity-related outcomes. Within this formulation, neuroplasticity is treated as one major convergence domain rather than the universal terminal pathway of every mechanism. The stress-diathesis framework provides a precedent for this conditional architecture. FKBP5 variants can interact with early-life adversity through allele-specific epigenetic regulation of glucocorticoid signaling ( 156 , 172 ). HPA-axis dysregulation links such vulnerability to environmental stress and has been associated with hippocampal and prefrontal changes ( 173 ), while chronic stress impairs dendritic structure, neurogenesis, and long-term potentiation ( 174 ). This provides a diet-independent molecular precedent for the proposal that HFD-related metabolic and neuroinflammatory effects operate within a moderated vulnerability framework. In this model, convergent loss of synaptic and structural plasticity provides a biological substrate for reduced adaptive capacity. HFD-relevant epigenetic findings provide additional support, including altered hippocampal DNA methylation at genes involved in memory and cognition ( 175 ), with age-dependent methylation differences also reported ( 158 ). BDNF-related epigenetic changes have been observed after Western-diet exposure ( 176 ), maternal HFD ( 177 ), and in human depression studies ( 178 ). Developmental timing also alters epigenetic regulation of reward-circuit genes, with different patterns after maternal versus adult HFD exposure ( 179 , 180 ). Histone and non-coding RNA mechanisms have also been reported ( 181 ). Reversibility has been demonstrated with DNA methyltransferase inhibition ( 176 ) and SIRT1 activation ( 175 ). 4.8 Human Translation and Research Priorities Human observational evidence supports associations among diet quality, obesity, depression, cognitive impairment, and dementia risk, but these relationships do not establish that mechanisms identified in preclinical HFD models operate equivalently in humans. Associations between obesity and cognitive outcomes are not uniform and may vary with age at adiposity assessment; late-life weight loss can further complicate interpretation through reverse causation. Education, socioeconomic status, physical activity, sleep, comorbid type 2 diabetes or cardiovascular disease, and overall dietary pattern may confound or modify these relationships. Recent evidence supports dietary interventions for reducing depressive symptoms in obesity. A 2024 meta-analysis of 25 studies showed moderate improvements with calorie restriction (SMD = −0.47) ( 182 ). Subsequent meta-analyses confirmed smaller but significant benefits of hypocaloric diets (SMD −0.27 to −0.23), though certainty remains low ( 183 , 184 ). Evidence for low-fat diets is weak, with negligible effects on depression (SMD −0.03) ( 184 , 185 ). In contrast, the Mediterranean diet has shown antidepressant effects, including in the SMILES trial and comparative studies ( 186 , 187 ). Ongoing trials, including MEDIMIND ( 188 ) and MoodFOOD ( 189 ), will clarify efficacy in clinical populations. Overall, interventions emphasizing caloric reduction and diet quality—particularly Mediterranean-style diets—appear most clinically relevant for managing depression in obesity. Table 5 summarizes this evidence qualitatively across dietary intervention types for both depression and cognitive/dementia outcomes. Complementary multi-omic approaches, including metabolomics, proteomics, and single-cell transcriptomics, may help resolve biological heterogeneity, identify pathway-specific signatures, and define subgroups with differential vulnerability or treatment responsiveness. Lifestyle interventions combining diet and exercise can reduce depressive symptoms independently of the magnitude of weight loss ( 192 ). For cognitive outcomes, diet, exercise, and bariatric-surgery interventions have been associated with improvements across multiple domains ( 193 ). Bariatric surgery produced relatively rapid cognitive gains but more variable psychiatric effects ( 194 ). Overall, weight-loss interventions appear to improve, but not fully restore, obesity-associated brain changes ( 195 ). GLP-1 receptor agonists are among the most promising pharmacological approaches. In a randomized trial, liraglutide improved memory despite comparable weight loss and glycemic control between groups ( 196 ), while broader evidence suggests potential mood and quality-of-life benefits ( 194 ). Proposed mechanisms include neuroplasticity, dopaminergic, and anti-inflammatory effects ( 197 , 198 ); larger cohort studies have not supported early suicidality concerns ( 199 ), although long-term neuropsychiatric data remain limited. Anti-inflammatory therapies have shown less consistent clinical benefit, indicating that neuroinflammation is unlikely to be a sufficient stand-alone target. Across intervention classes, the strongest candidate targets include insulin resistance, neuroinflammation, gut-brain signaling, and adipokine normalization. Major gaps include an incomplete understanding of the molecular links between HFD exposure, depression-related outcomes, and AD-related pathology, particularly in astrocyte–microglial lipoprotein signaling. Studies also lack standardization in model design, diet composition, duration, and sex and age representation. Human translation remains limited because direct cohort evidence is sparse. Additional gaps involve fat-type specificity, ApoE interactions, and vascular/BBB mechanisms. Most experimental obesity models do not reproduce the full metabolic, socioeconomic, dietary, and psychosocial complexity of human obesity, while available human associations remain vulnerable to residual lifestyle and socioeconomic confounding. The predominantly preclinical evidence should therefore be interpreted as mechanistic hypotheses requiring human confirmation. Future work should prioritize prospective cohorts integrating metabolic, neuroimaging, and molecular measures, as well as approaches capable of resolving peripheral-versus-central inflammation and glial heterogeneity. No individual biomarker or biomarker panel is currently sufficiently validated to identify individuals at greatest risk for obesity-associated depression or cognitive impairment. Multimodal profiles integrating metabolic, inflammatory, neuroimaging, cognitive, and clinical measures may therefore better identify biologically vulnerable subgroups. PANoptosis and related cell-death mechanisms ( 200 , 201 ) and autophagy/ferroptosis modulation ( 202 ) remain outside the HFD-specific evidence base and therefore require direct testing. Future priorities should emphasize prospective longitudinal human studies and continued evaluation of insulin-resistance, neuroinflammatory, gut-brain, adipokine, and GLP-1-related targets. 5. CONCLUSION This review synthesizes current evidence on overlapping but partly distinct mechanisms linking HFD exposure, obesity, and metabolic dysfunction to depression-related outcomes, cognitive impairment, and AD-related pathology. It also proposes a conceptual model situating these interlinked, largely preclinical pathways — the Conditional Integrative Model — within a molecular stress-diathesis framework. On the evidence reviewed here, these pathways are better described as a conditional network of overlapping but partly distinct mechanisms, moderated by developmental timing, sex, genetic background, and concurrent stress, than as a single causal cascade. Mechanistic inference is strongest within specific preclinical models, and the model is therefore offered as one plausible account of their convergence rather than an established, fully unified mechanism. Clarifying these relationships through longitudinal human studies may help identify individuals most vulnerable to diet- and metabolism-associated neural dysfunction. Such work may inform more targeted prevention and intervention strategies without assuming that preclinical mechanisms operate equivalently in clinically diagnosed depression or AD.
