1 Introduction Information about the morphological design, physiological functions, and genetic toolkit for development is encoded in nucleic acids, whose primary structure is the result of complex evolutionary processes spanning billions of years. Instructions for building organisms might be modified from one generation to the next by changes in coding and regulatory DNA (Carroll et al. 2005 ). Time could be comprehended as a double agent, effectively creating but also erasing clues about phylogenetic relationships among extant organisms with every new mutation. Highly divergent genes saturated with mutations can cause difficulties in inferring phylogenies (Felsenstein 2004 ). In this case, body structure, especially at the cellular level, might be the last piece of information left for unraveling evolutionary history. Although the nature of cellular organization is conserved across the tree of life, detecting homologies and genealogical relationships remains a challenging task, as we shall document in the present review. The ciliate class Phyllopharyngea is characterized by highly diverse morphologies and body architectures (Lynn 2008 ). Although phyllopharyngeans represent a morphologically, morphogenetically, and ecologically heterogeneous group of eukaryotic microbes, their monophyly is robustly supported by DNA data (Gao et al. 2012 ; Chen et al. 2016 ; Wang et al. 2017 ; Pan et al. 2019 ; Fahrni et al. 2022 ) and two highly specific cellular features: the ultrastructure of the microtubular appendages of basal bodies (kinetosomes) and the very distinctive microtubules (the so‐called phyllae ) of the cytopharynx (Small and Lynn 1981 ). Besides these two common ultrastructural traits, components of the oral apparatus, developmental sequences, and life histories are highly diverse and characteristic of each of the four recognized subclasses: Cyrtophoria, Chonotrichia, Suctoria, and Rhynchodia (Lynn 2008 ; Figure 1 ). Among these four subclasses, only representatives of Cyrtophoria resemble other “typical” ciliates. Cyrtophorians are usually free‐living and free‐swimming microorganisms, reported globally from a broad spectrum of habitats such as mosses, rivers, lakes, and seas (e.g., Wang 1977 ; Foissner 1979 , 1988 ; Foissner et al. 1991 , 2002 ; Petz and Foissner 1997 ; Lynn 2008 ; Fan et al. 2014 ; Xu et al. 2016 ; Pan et al. 2017 ; Hu et al. 2019 ; Qu et al. 2022 ; Choi et al. 2024 ). Their somatic ciliature is composed of monokinetids (i.e., unpaired kinetosomes with fibrillar associates), restricted to the ventral body side and divided into two ciliary fields: right and left. The oral region is typically surrounded by one preoral and two circumoral kineties composed of dikinetids (i.e., paired kinetosomes with fibrillar associates) (Deroux and Dragesco 1968 ; Deroux 1970 , 1976a , 1976b , 1977 ; Figure 1D ). Characteristic features distinguishing cyrtophorians from all other phyllopharyngeans include a simple oral ciliature and a well‐developed cytopharyngeal basket (of the cyrthos type), which serves for ingestion of algae, preferably diatoms, bacteria, and animal tissues (Deroux 1974 ; Foissner et al. 1991 ; Sniezek et al. 1995 ; Lynn 2008 ; Jin et al. 2021 ). A profound reorganization of the cyrtophorian bauplan led to the genesis of the subclass Chonotrichia. Small subunit rRNA gene phylogenies and phylogenomic analyses corroborated the morphology‐based hypothesis that this group could be closely related to cyrtophorians (Jankowski 1973 ). In fact, chonotrichs are nested deep within one cyrtophorian clade, the family Dysteriidae (Lynn 2016 ; Pan et al. 2019 ; Fahrni et al. 2022 ). The subclass status is, therefore, misleading and has been maintained mostly due to historical reasons and the very distinctive body architecture of chonotrichs. The body of their trophic stage is typically divided into four parts (Figure 1C ): (1) an apically located collar carrying somatic ciliature, (2) a neck‐like constriction containing the cytopharynx, complex of the contractile vacuole, and components of the cytoproct, (3) a main vase‐ or calyx‐like cell body with nuclei and food vacuoles, and (4) a holdfast apparatus (Jankowski 1973 ; Fahrni 1982 ). Chonotrichs live in close association with a wide range of mostly marine crustaceans (Jankowski 1973 ; Fernandez‐Leborans 2001 ). The trophic stage is permanently attached to the host, which completely precludes its dispersion to new hosts. The emergence of a free‐swimming “larval” stage in the life cycle provided a solution to this dispersal constraint. The larval somatic ciliature resembles the right and left ciliary fields typical of cyrtophorians (Jankowski 1973 ; Fahrni 1984 ; Lynn 2008 ). The presence of these ciliary fields, along with the heteromerous macronucleus (which is partitioned into karyomeres with strikingly different DNA and RNA contents, and hence, with differential staining capacities), is considered a morphological synapomorphy of chonotrichs and cyrtophorians (Lynn 2008 ). The free‐swimming “larval” stage evolved independently also in the subclass Suctoria. This mobile form is typically the only developmental stage possessing ciliated basal bodies (Matthes et al. 1988 ; Dovgal 2002 ; Lynn 2008 ). After settling on a new substrate or host, metamorphosis into the predatory adult stage occurs. Suctorians lack the typical oral apparatus composed of a single cytostome and cytopharynx in both the larval and adult stages (Lynn 2008 ). Instead, they have numerous sucking tentacles designed for prey capture (Tucker 1972 ; Curry and Butler 1976 ; Lynn and Foissner 1994 ; Figure 1A ). The absence of ciliature in the trophic stage, multiple independent cell mouths (polystomy) localized at the tip of tentacles, various bizarre shapes, and altered stomatogenesis make suctorians one of the most morphologically derived groups of ciliates (Matthes et al. 1988 ; Foissner et al. 1995 ; Dovgal 2002 ; Ma et al. 2021 ). Lastly, the subclass Rhynchodia comprises small‐sized predators and ectoparasites possessing only one sucking tube in the anterior region of the cell (Lom and Kozloff 1968 ; Raabe 1970 ; Lynn 2008 ; Figure 1B ). The phylogenetic position of rhynchodians within the class Phyllopharyngea is unstable (Gao et al. 2012 ). Traits such as the homomerous macronucleus (with no differentiation into zones containing differing DNA and RNA contents, and hence, with uniform staining capacity, except for heterochromatin granules and nucleoli), the absence of oral ciliature, and the presence of extrusomes in the sucking tube suggest a connection between rhynchodians and suctorians (Grell and Meister 1982 ; Lynn 2008 , 2016 ). On the other hand, the ventrally located somatic ciliature of some members of the order Hypocomatida (Raabe 1970 ; Deroux 1976b ; Lynn 2008 ) and the merotelokinetal stomatogenesis (Foissner 1996 ) point to a closer relationship with cyrtophorians. Feeding strategy is considered the most crucial diversification force behind morphological disparity, especially in oral structures, among the four phyllopharyngean subclasses (Lynn 2008 ). The role of intrinsic factors such as chromosome fragmentation (Mochizuki 2010 ; Yan et al. 2019 ), gene expression profiles, and developmental timing in the morphological evolution of phyllopharyngeans remains largely unexplored. In this review, we discuss the potential impact of changes in developmental timing in terms of heterochronic processes, which have been mostly studied in plants and animals but only rarely in microbes (Hadži 1952 ; Williams and Clamp 2007 ; Arthur 2011 ; Tomescu and Rothwell 2022 ). We shall address the question of whether heterochrony has the potential to elucidate the observed extensive body changes in the trophic stages of chonotrichs and suctorians relative to cyrtophorians and rhynchodians. Since the deep phylogenetic relationships of phyllopharyngeans remain poorly understood using molecular approaches, we aim to restore the kinships among the four subclasses using structural and ontogenetic bodies of evidence. 2 Morphological Evolution of Oral Structures The most recent common ancestor (MRCA) of ciliates possessed a cell mouth (cytostome), that is, a two‐dimensional orifice through which food passes into the cell (Lynn 2008 ). During their more than one‐billion‐year‐long evolutionary history, the cytostome was independently lost multiple times in various unrelated groups of ciliates, such as kentrophorids (Raikov 1971 ; Fenchel and Finlay 1989 ; Foissner 1995 ), astomes (de Puytorac 1954 , 1969 , 1972 ), haptophryids (Corliss et al. 1965 ; Rataj and Vďačný 2018 ; Rataj et al. 2022 ), clausilocolids (Lom 1959 ; Zhang and Vd'ačný 2022 ), and some other hymenostomes (Poljanskij 1925 ; Kozloff 1954 ; Frankel 1961 ; Orias and Pollock 1975 ; Basmussen and Orias 1975 ). Much more rarely, the number of cell mouths has increased, for example, in pycnothrichids (Foissner et al. 2020 ) and suctorians (Matthes et al. 1988 ; Dovgal 2002 ). Oral structures of ciliates exhibit paramount diversity, depending on the feeding mode, presence or absence of an oral (buccal) cavity, surrounding ciliature, and the architecture of the cytopharynx (Lynn 2008 ). Despite the huge morphological disparity, the cell mouth is localized at a fixed position on the cell surface in monostomous ciliates and polystomous pycnothrichids (Foissner et al. 2020 ). Independent cell mouths, however, are situated at the tip of sucking tentacles, far away from the cell body in suctorians. Along with the absence of ciliary rows in the trophic stage, this was the reason why suctorians were not classified within the phylum Ciliophora in the past (Dovgal 2002 ). The fact that stomatogenesis (formation of new oral structures) is typically connected with oral or somatic ciliature raises numerous questions about the evolution of polystomy and tentacles in suctorians. Did the ancestor of suctorians lose its primary cytostome? Did the suctorian oral apparatus form by multiplication of the ancestral phyllopharyngean oral apparatus, or was it formed de novo from precursors of the original phyllopharyngean oral structures, followed by their multiplication? Could addressing these questions about the origin of polystomy and tentacles help us resolve the puzzling phylogenetic relationships at the base of the class Phyllopharyngea? Besides the issues associated with polystomy and the transformation of oral structures toward a predatory lifestyle in suctorians and rhynchodians, we also review the consequences of the raptorial feeding mode on the loss of primary oral ciliature and the need for evolution of secondary oral ciliature in algivorous, bacterivorous, and histophagous phyllopharyngeans. Finally, the secondary loss of this secondary oral ciliature in chonotrichs will be discussed in the context of the transition to a suspension feeding mode. 2.1 Are the Rhynchodian Sucking Tube and Suctorian Tentacle Homologous Structures? Bardele and Kurth ( 2001 ) suggested that the ancestor of the class Phyllopharyngea lost its primary oral ciliature along with the primary cytopharynx. To compensate for this loss, tentacle‐like structures emerged in suctorians (Figure 2A–C ), and a sucking tube evolved in rhynchodians (Figure 2D–F ). It was speculated that the rhynchodian sucking tube could represent a transition between the tentacles of polystomous suctorians and the cytopharyngeal basket of monostomous cyrtophorians and chonotrichs. This poses the question of whether the rhynchodian sucking tube is homologous to the suctorian tentacles. Due to the scarcity of sequences from the subclass Rhynchodia, its phylogenetic position and kinship to the subclass Suctoria are not robustly resolved in 18S rRNA gene phylogenies (Gao et al. 2012 ; Chen et al. 2016 ; Fahrni et al. 2022 ). Common features of both subclasses include: (1) the internal morphology (architecture) of oral tubes (Lom and Kozloff 1968 ; Hitchen and Butler 1973 ; Figure 2B,E ); (2) the homomerous macronucleus (Lynn and Foissner 1994 ; Figure 1A,B ); (3) the presence of extrusomes (Lynn and Foissner 1994 ; Figure 2C,F ); and (4) the oral apparatus protruding from the body surface (Raabe 1970 ; Dovgal 2002 ; Pan et al. 2012 ; Figure 2A,D ). Despite these shared morphological characteristics, the homology of the rhynchodian tube and suctorian tentacles, as well as the sister‐group relationship of rhynchodiands and suctorians, remains questionable. Arguments against the homology include the considerably different morphogenesis of the rhynchodian sucking tubes and the suctorian tentacles (Lynn and Foissner 1994 ). While the single sucking tube arises in the opisthe in association with ciliated somatic ciliary rows during binary fission of rhynchodians (Bower and Meyer 1993 ), tentacles originate in cooperation with unciliated subcortical kinetosomes at any time in the trophont stage of suctorians (Hitchen and Butler 1973 ; Figure 2C ). Because tentacles do not arise during binary fission but conspicuously later, after the metamorphosis of the swarmer, an independent origin of suctorian and rhynchodian oral structures is suggested herein. The presence of the sucking tube and tentacles thus does not necessarily indicate a close relationship between rhynchodians and suctorians, and these oral structures may be independent adaptations to a predatory and/or parasitic lifestyle. Our speculation is corroborated by the different types of extrusomes used by suctorians and rhynchodians to kill prey, and by the different localization of extrusomes (Lom and Kozloff 1968 ; Bardele 1976 ; Hausmann 1978 ; Grell and Meister 1982 ; Lynn 2008 ). Thus, suctorians possess haptocysts docked at the tip of tentacles (Dovgal 2002 ; Lynn 2008 ; Figure 2C ), while rhynchodians exhibit haptotrichocysts, which are situated deep in the phagoplasm of the sucking tube and have never been found to protrude into the tip of the tube (Lom and Kozloff 1968 ; Figure 2F ). On the other hand, rhynchodians and cyrtophorians share merotelokinetal stomatogenesis, which, in addition, involves complete or partial reorganization of the parental cytostomal area (Bower and Meyer 1993 ; Foissner 1996 ; Bardele and Kurth 2001 ; Figure 3A–F ). Both features are, however, most likely plesiomorphies of phyllopharyngeans (Foissner 1996 ). The same applies to the homomerous macronucleus shared by rhynchodians and suctorians. Plesiomorphies as such do not have the power to support hypotheses about phylogenetic kinships, as only synapomorphies represent the signal in creating monophyletic groups (for a review, see Vďačný 2017 ). According to molecular phylogenies, there are just three main phyllopharyngean lineages: suctorians (designated as S), rhynchodians (designated as R), and the cyrtophorian–chonotrich clade (designated as C 2 ). Only three evolutionary scenarios are possible for three groups: (S, (R, C 2 )), ((S, R), C 2 ), and ((S, C 2 ), R). The latter scenario has never been depicted in 18S rRNA gene phylogenies (Gao et al. 2012 ; Chen et al. 2016 ; Fahrni et al. 2022 ). If the second scenario were valid, one would expect the presence of a sucking tube/tentacle in the MRCA of suctorians and rhynchodians, or even at the base of the class Phyllopharyngea. However, sucking tubes and tentacles are most likely analogous structures (see above) that were not a property of the MRCA of phyllopharyngeans, casting doubt on the monophyletic origin of suctorians and rhynchodians. Consequently, the first scenario remains the most plausible. Despite this, the origin and evolution of tentacles are still not clear. Either ancestral oral phyllopharyngean structures were multiplied in the MRCA of suctorians, which is corroborated by the presence of phyllae in tentacles (Lynn and Foissner 1994 ), or the primary oral apparatus was completely lost and suctorian oral structures were created de novo from precursors of the phyllopharyngean oral apparatus, as suggested by the very peculiar morphogenesis of tentacles in the adult stage (Hitchen and Butler 1973 ). 2.2 Secondary Loss of the Secondary Oral Ciliature The specialization of a certain body part for prey capture or food acquisition is a challenging evolutionary task not only for macroorganisms but also for unicellular eukaryotic microorganisms. Various heterotrophic phylogenetic lineages have found unique ways of body remodeling to achieve this goal. It is speculated that the progenitor of ciliates made the feeding process more efficient by evolving a complex oral ciliature. Eisler's ( 1992 ) hypothesis about the evolution of the ciliate kinetome, an assembly of all kinetids covering the ciliate body, predicts not the somatic but the oral ciliature to be regarded as ancestral. The first step in the evolution of the kinetome was the formation of a paroral membrane, a compound ciliary organelle lying along the right side of the oral area, which historically but falsely has been termed “membrane.” This archaic ciliature, inherited from a flagellate‐like ancestor, was responsible for food intake, locomotion, and stomatogenesis. In the second step, a first somatic kinety (i.e., single structurally and functionally integrated file or row of kinetids) was formed from the right row of kinetosomes of the paroral membrane as a result of longitudinal splitting of the paroral membrane and sequential migration of the forming kinety to the right. Subsequent multiplication of ciliary rows resulted in functional differentiation into somatic (responsible for motion) and oral (responsible for nutrition) ciliature. In the third step, derived somatic ciliary rows gave rise to adoral organelles (short ciliary rows composed of polykinetids located on the left side of the oral region), which, along with the paroral membrane, provided an oral function (Eisler 1992 ). From this point of view, ciliate lineages that lack distinctive oral ciliary structures are not considered archaic but highly derived groups that underwent a reduction of the primary oral structures. A huge variety of modifications of the ancestral ciliate oral apparatus, which was thus composed of the paroral membrane and accompanying adoral organelles, has evolved across the ciliate tree of life (Foissner 1996 ; Lynn 2008 ). For instance, the paroral membrane was transformed into the circumoral kinety in the classes Litostomatea and Prostomatea independently (Bardele 1999 ; Vďačný et al. 2010 ), or the number of adoral organelles increased significantly in parallel across different ciliate classes (Lynn 2008 ). The reduction or complete absence of oral dikinetids is a prominent feature of three out of the four phyllopharyngean subclasses (Lynn 2008 ). Only representatives of the subclass Cyrtophoria show a simplified oral ciliature that is typically composed of a preoral kinety and two circumoral kineties (Figure 2G,I ). Both the increase and the decrease in the complexity of oral structures can be found within cyrtophorians (Qu et al. 2022 ). Research on the cyrtophorian Chlamydodon mnemosyne revealed critical differences in the ultrastructure of the stomatogenetic kinetids, indicating that already the MRCA of the class Phyllopharyngea had lost the primary oral ciliature (Bardele and Kurth 2001 ). It is important to stress that the reduction or complete absence of oral ciliature is not a rare phenomenon in ciliates, as it has occurred at least five times independently (see above). For instance, astomes lost all oral structures (cytostome, cytopharynx, and oral ciliature) in tandem with the transition to an endosymbiotic lifestyle (de Puytorac 1954 , 1969 , 1972 ). Some lineages of predatory haptorians (e.g., enchelyids) have evolved oralized monokinetids from somatic ciliature to compensate for the loss of oral dikinetids (Foissner and Foissner 1985 , 1988 ). An analogous process, that is, the complete loss of the primary oral ciliature and its subsequent replacement with a secondary one, was proposed for the subclass Cyrtophoria. The cyrtophorian secondary oral ciliature had to be derived from somatic kineties (i.e., rows of somatic kinetids), as the primary oral ciliature had been completely lost in the ancestor of phyllopharyngeans (Bardele and Kurth 2001 ; Figure 3A–F ). The MRCA of the class Phyllopharyngea was, with a high probability, like haptorians, an active, rapacious predator. Therefore, we speculate that the reduction or absence of the primary oral ciliature might correlate with the predatory lifestyle (Vďačný et al. 2010 , 2011 , 2012 ). The subsequent oralization of somatic ciliary rows was connected with the transition to algivory, bacterivory, and histophagy in cyrtophorians. Another question is why we do not find oral dikinetids within the subclass Chonotrichia when it is nested deep within the cyrtophorian clade. Remnants of the cyrtophorian secondary oral ciliature (two short rows of circumoral dikinetids) have so far been detected in just one chonotrich species, Chilodochona quennerstedti (Grain and Batisse 1974 ). Since the genus Chilodochona branches off first within the chonotrich clade (Lynn 2016 ; Fahrni et al. 2022 ), and oral kineties have not been observed in any other chonotrich (Lynn 2008 ), we suggest a secondary loss of the secondary cyrtophorian oral ciliature in the vast majority of chonotrichs. We suppose that the shift from the free‐living algivorous lifestyle of cyrtophorians to the sessile filtration mode caused massive morphological changes in the body organization of chonotrichs. The dorsal body side of chonotrichs was dramatically expanded and serves for attachment, causing the ventro‐dorsal axis to become the main cell axis. Somatic kineties of the ventral side of the trophont are restricted to the apically located cavity or helical collar (Jankowski 1973 ; Fahrni 1982 ). The chonotrich somatic ciliature is thus feeding‐oriented (pseudo‐oral) in the trophic stage (Lynn 2008 ) and is no longer used for active motion. The huge morphological rearrangement of the chonotrich body plan, along with the ciliary pattern, mirrors the shift to the suspension‐feeding mode, which was enabled by the functional oralization of the entire somatic ciliature. Chonotrichs are the only ciliates that utilize somatic instead of oral ciliature in the filtration of food particles (Jankowski 1973 ; Lynn 2008 ). As a consequence, chonotrichs are sessile, without any possibility of changing their position after attachment to their animal hosts in the trophic stage. In cyrtophorians, the secondary oral ciliature, which was derived from the somatic ciliature, became structurally and functionally oral. Although the functional transformation is complete in chonotrichs, the ultrastructural transformation is still undone, and, therefore, the feeding ciliary structures of chonotrichs are called pseudo‐oral (Lynn 2008 ). 3 Heterochrony in Morphological Evolution Heterochronic processes encompass evolutionary novelties that arise from shifts in the relative timing of developmental events. The definition and understanding of heterochrony, along with its consequences on morphological and molecular traits, have changed several times since Haeckel ( 1866 ) used this term for the first time 159 years ago. According to the primary definition, established by de Beer ( 1930 ) and later revisited by Gould ( 1977 ), heterochrony involves changes in ontogenetic sequences through time. Heterochronic changes result in contraction/extension or a shift in the timing or rate of morphological development. In practical terms, we recognize three global courses of heterochrony: (1) paedomorphosis, when for a given age, the descendant has a shape typical of the ancestor at a more juvenile age; (2) peramorphosis, when the descendant's ontogeny continues to a more mature stage (this could be a hypothetical extrapolation beyond the terminal morphology of the ancestor); and (3) dwarfism or gigantism, when the descendant retains ancestral shape but differs in size. Reduction in morphological development (paedomorphosis) may result from neoteny, progenesis, and post‐displacement. Increased morphological development during ontogeny (peramorphosis) may arise from acceleration, hypermorphosis, or pre‐displacement (de Beer 1930 ; Gould 1977 ; Alberch et al. 1979 ; McNamara 1986 ; Raff and Wray 1989 ; Webster and Zelditch 2005 ). Numerous examples of morphology‐defined heterochronies have been documented for metazoans (e.g., Wake and Brame 1969 ; Alberch et al. 1979 ; McNamara 1982 ; Reilly 1987 ; Hanken et al. 1997 ; Smirnov 1999 ; Arthur 2011 ; Bhullar et al. 2012 ; Esquerré et al. 2017 ; Camacho et al. 2019 ; Świś 2023 ). Heterochronic shifts playing a key role in the origin and diversification of leaves, roots, flowers, and fruits within different clades of plants have been evidenced as well (e.g., Geuten and Coenen 2013 ; Kostyun et al. 2017 ; Buendía‐Monreal and Gillmor 2018 ; Reich et al. 2020 ). These clues for heterochrony across metazoan and plant clades outline an option as to whether the heterochronic processes influence the morphological evolution of other eukaryotic organisms, including the microbial world. 3.1 Heterochrony in Single‐Celled Organisms Since the heterochronic shift originates from changes in the developmental sequence, a parallel for ontogeny needs to be found in unicellular organisms. The only difference between the ontogeny of protists and metazoans is one of scale (Hadži 1952 ; Kociolek and Williams 1987 ). If we define ontogeny as the development of an organism through its life cycle (Blackmore 1986 ), then observed changes at the tissue–organ levels in multicellular organisms could be found as analogous to those at the molecular–organellar levels in protists (Kociolek and Williams 1987 ). Among unicellular eukaryotes, ciliates represent a model group with continuous structural and spatial changes during their life cycle, showing ontogenetic transformations that are analogous to those in “higher” organisms. The first evidence for heterochrony in the context of cell size in relation to maturity (represented by the ability to undergo binary fission) was observed in the transgenic yeast Schizosaccharomyces pombe (Nurse 1985 ; Russell and Nurse 1986 , 1987a , 1987b ). Paedomorphic and peramorphic phenotypes were achieved through mutations in the temporal control system, whose products initiate mitosis (Raff and Wray 1989 ). These changes, however, do not correspond to the plasticity of regulatory mechanisms or to developmental shifts in the ontogenetic sequence of the given species. Nevertheless, these experiments on yeasts reveal molecular systems that can be potentially affected by heterochrony. Williams and Clamp ( 2007 ) presented potent evidence for a heterochronic shift within the phylum Ciliophora. Trophonts of the genera Opisthonecta and Astylozoon are actively swimming, which is in sharp contrast to other adult peritrichs of the order Sessilida, which possess a stalk used to attach the trophont to the substratum. They assumed that the trophic stage of these two genera became mobile as a result of a neotenic process. Consequently, the paedomorphic trophonts of Opisthonecta and Astylozoon should correspond to a juvenile stage of telotroch, a migratory free‐swimming larval form in the life cycle of sessile peritrichs (Williams and Clamp 2007 ; Lynn 2008 ). A motile larval stage, called a swarmer, emerged independently in the class Phyllopharyngea. 3.2 Heterochrony in Phyllopharyngeans The larval stage (swarmer) evolved twice in the evolutionary history of the class Phyllopharyngea, namely in the MRCAs of suctorians and chonotrichs. The independent origin of suctorian and chonotrich swarmers stems not only from molecular phylogenetic analyses but also from their conspicuous morphological differences (Figure 4A,B ). While suctorian swarmers possess few somatic ciliary rows, often arranged to form paratenes (transverse ciliary rings), and do not display any cell mouth (Figure 4A ), the larval stage of chonotrichs exhibits a fully developed cytostome and ciliary rows arranged in two fields (Fahrni 1984 ; Dovgal 2002 ; Lynn 2008 ; Figure 4B ). The process of peramorphosis must have operated independently in the morphological evolution of suctorians and chonotrichs, causing the juvenile phase of the life cycle to be elongated convergently. Peramorphosis might have enabled a transition from a mobile to a sessile lifestyle in the adult stage. Our hypothesis about the peramorphic nature of suctorian and chonotrich adult morphologies is supported by the following facts: (1) the larval stage, which represents the immature phase, is incapable of conjugation (sexual reproduction) or binary fission (asexual reproduction); (2) both reproduction modes are observed only in the trophic (mature/adult) stage; (3) the suctorian swarmer does not possess a cell mouth and hence is incapable of trophic feeding (although the chonotrich swarmer has a cell mouth, it is assumed that food intake is restricted to the adult stage, as the swarmer's ciliature is used for active motion); (4) trophic stages of both subclasses are strikingly different from those of other phyllopharyngeans (Figures 1A–D and 4C–E ); and (5) some suctorians show conspicuous size enlargement in the adult stage, as expected for peramorphosis (especially hypermorphosis), where the elongated juvenile phase leads to the attainment of a larger maximum size, meaning the descendant adult should be larger than the ancestral adult (McNamara 1986 ). Besides homology of the right and left ciliary fields, the presence of attachment organelles, represented by the cyrtophorian podite and the chonotrich holdfast apparatus, further supports the shared evolutionary history of both subclasses. One of the main reasons for considering that heterochrony has influenced these two phyllopharyngean subclasses is the fact that suctorian and chonotrich migratory larval stages resemble the adult stages of rhynchondians and cyrtophorians more than their trophic sessile forms do (Jankowski 1973 ; Fahrni 1984 ; Lynn 2008 , 2016 ). The two main ciliary fields of the chonotrich swarmer are generally considered homologous to the right and left somatic ciliary fields of cyrtophorians (Figure 4B–E ). On the other hand, the consequences of morphology‐defined heterochronies in suctorians might seem puzzling. If the juvenile stage recapitulates the morphology of the ancestor, then one could assume that the MRCA of suctorians did not possess the cell mouth. Although this seems to be a paradoxical implication, it does not contradict the aforementioned discussion about the de novo formation of the oral apparatus in adult suctorians (see above). 3.3 Changes in Timing of Ontogenetic Sequence in Suctorians and Chonotrichs Besides Gould's ( 1977 ) and Alberch et al.'s ( 1979 ) definitions of heterochrony, the attention of the biological community has been reoriented to the developmental and molecular aspects of this phenomenon. Regarding the ontogenetic sequence, which occurs in highly regulated spatial and temporal contexts, heterochrony can be perceived as a shift in the relative timing of developmental events during ontogeny (Smith 2001 , 2002 , 2003 ). In addition, individual events must be conserved between the ancestor and the descendant (related species), and only their relationship to ontogenetic time is modified (Alberch and Blanco 1996 ; Webster and Zelditch 2005 ). Two cases can be distinguished: (1) timing modification that involves a change in the rank order of developmental events (if the order of events is be A–B–C in the ancestral sequence, then the signal for heterochrony in the descendant would be A–C–B); and (2) temporal displacement of the ontogenetic event without a change in the order of events (in this case, the events A–B–C retain the same rank in both ancestor and descendant, but the absolute ontogenetic time differs between them; e.g., event C in the descendant may occur sooner/later or last shorter/longer time compared to the ancestor) (Smith 2001 ; Webster and Zelditch 2005 ). The clonal life cycle of ciliates includes three main steps, inevitable for successful fission, that occur in the following order: stomatogenesis (formation of new oral structures), karyokinesis (division of the nucleus), and cytokinesis (division of the cytoplasm, i.e., cell division per se ) (Foissner 1996 ). After the successful completion of all three processes, the daughter cell has a virtually identical morphology to the mother cell. This basic rank order of developmental steps is common to the majority of ciliates, including representatives of the subclasses Cyrtophoria and Rhynchodia (Figures 5A and 6A ). In principle, the correct completion of early ontogenetic events is essential for initiating subsequent ones. However, any developmental sequence does not necessarily reflect causal consequences (Alberch 1985 ). Raff and Wray ( 1989 ) stated that the nature of causal relationships in a developmental system determines the relative ease of dissociation of events, and hence the range of possible heterochronies. Karyokinesis preceding cytokinesis is the only causal relationship in the ciliate developmental sequence. The absence of the cytopharyngeal basket and differentiated oral kineties can be interpreted as a neotenic shift in the ontogeny of chonotrichs. The rank order of individual developmental events in the clonal life cycle of suctorians and chonotrichs differs from the “basic” ontogenetic sequence of rhynchodians and cyrtophorians (Figures 5B and 6B ). Daughter cells of suctorians and chonotrichs bud from their mother cells, causing juveniles to differ strikingly from adults. The metamorphosis of swarmers generated by budding is, therefore, an inevitable prerequisite for their maturation. The transformation of free‐swimming swarmers into sessile adults represents a newly inserted step in the ontogeny of suctorians and chonotrichs (Figures 5B and 6B ). Both sexual and asexual reproduction, as well as the ability for active food intake, are possible only when the metamorphosis from larval to trophic stage is completed. The ontogenetic divergence of chonotrichs lies in the timing of stomatogenesis. The onset of this process is normally associated with the early stages of binary fission in ciliates (Foissner 1996 ), but its completion is postponed in chonotrichs, taking place after metamorphosis. Since the processes of karyokinesis and cytokinesis proceed in a standard mode (Jankowski 1973 ; Fahrni 1984 ; Foissner 1996 ), the emerged larval stage exhibits a cytostome but lacks the cytopharyngeal basket and differentiated oral dikinetids (Figure 6B ). These two traits are characteristic of their closest relatives within the subclass Cyrtophoria and are formed prior to karyo‐ and cytokinesis (Lom and Corliss 1971 ; Hofmann 1988 ). The function of the free‐swimming swarmer is to search for an appropriate place to settle, where the subsequent metamorphosis to the adult stage occurs. At this point, the suspended stomatogenesis resumes, and somatic ciliary rows of the swarmer reorganize to form the pseudo‐oral ciliature of the trophic stage. The interrupted process of stomatogenesis can be interpreted as a neotenic shift in the ontogeny of chonotrichs. The ontogenetic sequence of suctorians significantly differs from that of chonotrichs in the initiation of new oral structure formation. In suctorians, stomatogenetic processes are not associated with early morphogenetic stages but begin after the onset of metamorphosis (Figure 5B ), a newly inserted step following karyo‐ and cytokinesis. Because stomatogenesis occurs after cytokinesis, the released swarmer does not possess a cytostome. On the other hand, stomatogenesis in chonotrichs is initiated before karyo‐ and cytokinesis, and hence their swarmer carries a cytostome, as mentioned above. Since the order of ontogenetic events is disrupted, we assume that the highly modified body architecture of suctorians originated via a heterochronic shift as well. 3.4 Adaptive Scenario for Heterochronical Change in Suctorians and Chonotrichs Although suctorians and chonotrichs are morphologically highly dissimilar, adults of both groups show motionless feeding behavior. As mentioned above, they also share a dispersal larval stage associated with an extra ontogenetic step (metamorphosis), leading to dramatic remodeling of the gross body morphology of the adult trophic stage. We speculate that the modification in the rank order of developmental events and relative elongation of the ontogenetic timeline in suctorians and chonotrichs might have been correlated with shifts in their feeding strategies. Suctorians are typically ambush predators that attach to the substratum by a non‐contractile stalk or directly by the body, waiting to encounter motile prey. They use tentacles to capture and devour the prey (Matthes et al. 1988 ; Dovgal 2002 ; Lynn 2008 ). On the other hand, chonotrichs are suspension feeders fastened to the mouthparts and body appendages of a variety of crustaceans by a non‐contractile stalk or holdfast apparatus (Jankowski 1973 ; Fahrni 1984 ). As filtrators, chonotrichs engage the entire body (somatic) ciliature in creating feeding currents to bring tissue debris, cuticle pieces, bacteria, and other protists to the cytostome (Lynn 2008 ). New feeding modes were probably responses to specific selective pressures that favored sessile life forms over free‐swimming ones. There is an apparent trade‐off in the value of motility, as swimming increases encounters with food but also with predators (Jakobsen et al. 2006 ). Due to the contrasting feeding strategies of suctorians and chonotrichs, different selective pressures were at play. Suctorians lead a predatory lifestyle, and trophic stages typically carry numerous cell mouths, each at the tip of a tentacle (Dovgal 2002 ), to increase the probability of capturing prey, while chonotrichs are filtrators with a single cell mouth at the bottom of an atrial or helically organized cavity (Jankowski 1973 ). Despite these differences, both groups share interrupted and postponed stomatogenesis, which we interpret as a neotenic shift (i.e., paedomorphosis). As a consequence of this heterochronical change, the dispersal (larval) stages of suctorians and chonotrichs do not possess a cytopharyngeal basket and hence are not capable of feeding. We consider the lack of functional oral structures in their swarmers to be the common paedomorphic sign of suctorians and chonotrichs. On the other hand, the elongated juvenile phase enabled further extrapolation beyond the terminal morphology of their ancestors (i.e., peramorphosis), allowing a transition from a mobile to a sessile lifestyle in the adult stage. Moreover, the later onset and subsequent acceleration of the stomatogenetic process facilitated the evolution of tentacles and polystomy in suctorians. The peramorphic transformation of body shape in chonotrichs, along with pseudo‐oralization of their somatic ciliature, are also innovative traits, especially with respect to their ancestral cyrtophorians. To summarize, the suctorian and chonotrich adult morphologies are a result of mosaic or modular heterochrony, in which various traits are affected by different types of heterochrony (Bastir and Rosas 2009 ; Koyabu et al. 2014 ). The timing of stomatogenesis shows the highest plasticity among the developmental steps of phyllopharyngeans, and the heterochronical shift in the onset of stomatogenesis led to the emergence of novel oral (feeding) structures, that is, tentacles in suctorians and an atrial or helically organized cavity lined by the pseudo‐oralized right and left ciliary fields in chonotrichs. The reorganization of body parts in the predatory suctorians and filtratory chonotrichs could have been realized through a combination of multiple paedomorphic and peramorphic processes, which are only rarely considered to underlie the adult morphologies of unicellular organisms. 4 Conclusions 1. Distinctions in the number of cell mouths, extrusome types, and especially in the course of stomatogenesis suggest that the rhynchodian sucking tube and suctorian tentacles are analogous structures, convergently evolved for food acquisition. Rhynchodians might be more closely related to cyrtophorians and chonotrichs based on the presence of only one cell mouth, somatic ciliature throughout the entire life cycle, and merotelokinetal stomatogenesis. However, the homomerous macronucleus of rhynchodians is suggestive of their deep segregation from the cyrtophorian–chonotrich clade, whose distinctive trait is the heteromerous macronucleus. 2. The MRCA of phyllopharyngeans most likely lost its primary oral ciliature in association with the ancestral predatory lifestyle. The progenitor of algivorous/bacterivorous cyrtophorians, therefore, had to generate new oral ciliary structures from somatic ciliature to compensate for this loss. The transition to a suspension‐feeding mode in chonotrichs, along with a dramatic reorganization of their body architecture, resulted in a secondary loss of the secondary oral ciliature. Assuming that the secondary oral ciliature was inefficient for suspension feeding, chonotrichs had to engage the entire somatic ciliature for this purpose and remodel the ventral body side to an atrial cavity or a helical collar lined by somatic ciliary rows. Although this ciliature has an oral function (after metamorphosis), its ultrastructural nature remained somatic. Hence, the feeding ciliary structures should be termed pseudo‐oral. 3. Larval stages (swarmers) emerged independently in suctorians and chonotrichs. They probably resulted from heterochronies triggered by the adoption of new feeding strategies and the loss of active motion in trophic stages. Peramorphosis played a key role in the evolution of polystomy in suctorians and the dramatic remodeling of body architecture in both suctorians and chonotrichs. The newly inserted step, metamorphosis, in their life cycles caused relative elongation of their developmental sequences compared to those of cyrtophorians and rhynchodians. However, suctorians and chonotrichs share multiple paedomorphic signs, such as the absence of the cytopharyngeal basket and the loss of the secondary oral ciliature. Thus, paedomorphosis and peramorphosis acted on different body structures in both groups, resulting in adult morphologies shaped by mosaic heterochrony. 4. Attention should be focused on the molecular aspects underlying the relative elongation of developmental sequences and the phenomenon of mosaic heterochrony in suctorians and chonotrichs. In particular, changes in regulatory DNA, as well as activators and repressors in signaling developmental pathways, need to be identified and characterized across all four phyllopharyngean subclasses. We pointed out that the timing of stomatogenesis shows the highest plasticity among developmental steps. The comparatively simple and unicellular body organization of phyllopharyngeans could provide a key source of information on the origins of heterochrony and its impact on the evolution of body plans in early eukaryotes. Investigating the genetic toolkit for phyllopharyngean development will, however, face multiple obstacles, including the uncultivability of these microorganisms, the tight association of chonotrichs and at least some suctorians and rhynchodians with their animal hosts, and the synchronization of the chonotrich life cycle with molting of their crustacean hosts.
