INTRODUCTION Cachexia is a complex syndrome characterized by ongoing loss of skeletal muscle mass (with or without loss of fat mass) that cannot be entirely reversed by conventional nutritional supplementation [ 1 ]. Etymologically, the term “cachexia” is derived from the Greek kakós “bad, poor” and héxis “condition”. Cachexia can be caused by several underlying illnesses, including kidney disease, heart failure, neurological disease, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), acquired immune deficiency syndrome (AIDS), and especially cancer. These conditions induce systemic inflammation and metabolic dysregulation, leading to predominant cachectic features including reduced food intake (anorexia), decreased muscle mass, and progressive functional impairment of the entire organism. Cancer cachexia occurs in 25%–70% of cancer patients and leads to at least 22% of cancer-related deaths [ 2 ]. The clinical significance of cancer cachexia is profound since it not only compromises the quality and length of life in cancer patients but also reduces their tolerance to anticancer therapy, restricting treatment options. Despite the devastating role that cachexia plays in the outcomes of cancer patients, there are still few practical measures to counteract this lethal wasting syndrome. Pancreatic cancer has the poorest prognosis among all malignant tumors, with the lowest 5-year survival rate of merely 13% [ 3 ]. Moreover, the incidence of this catastrophic disease is growing at a rate of 0.5%–1.0% per year, and it has been projected to become the second-leading cause of cancer-related deaths by 2030. Several factors contribute to the grim prognosis of pancreatic cancer, including rapid progression, early metastasis, postoperative recurrence, and resistance to chemotherapy, as well as the highest prevalence of cachexia among malignant tumors of up to 63% [ 4 , 5 ]. Notably, cachexia can be present months or even years before the diagnosis of pancreatic cancer [ 6 , 7 ], and has a disastrous impact on the patient's survival [ 8 ]. The importance of cachexia in pancreatic cancer has attracted myriad studies to unveil the mechanisms of its development, to bring new insights for potential therapeutic interventions. Although pancreatic cancer cachexia is primarily characterized by loss of skeletal muscle and adipose tissue, there is growing evidence that it is essentially a systemic cancer-driven phenomenon, both affecting and being influenced by multiple organs, including the heart, liver, nervous system, digestive system, etc. [ [9] , [10] , [11] , [12] , [13] ]. The systemic alterations and multi-organ interactions in the settings of pancreatic cancer cachexia are garnering increasing attention. Although previous studies revealed the role of some cachexia-inducing factors and inter-organ interactions in pancreatic cancer cachexia, such as those between skeletal muscle and adipose tissue, the evidence is too scattered to allow a comprehensive elucidation of the pathophysiological processes underlying this complex syndrome. In addition, the boom in new research on this topic necessitates a review that comprehensively summarizes key findings in recent years. Here, we briefly introduce the definitions, classifications, and clinical presentations of pancreatic cancer cachexia. We then focus on the current knowledge of cachexia-inducing factors and the mechanisms of multi-organ interactions underlying pancreatic cancer cachexia. Finally, we also summarize the emerging animal models of this syndrome and discuss potential treatment strategies for pancreatic cancer cachexia to provide suggestions for experimental research and clinical practice. DEFINITION, DIAGNOSIS, AND CLASSIFICATION OF CANCER CACHEXIA Our understanding of cancer cachexia has evolved. In 2008, an international panel of experts defined cachexia as “a syndrome with complex metabolic abnormalities caused by an underlying disease and characterized by loss of skeletal muscle mass with or without loss of fat mass” [ 14 ]. In addition, cachexia was recognized as affecting both adults and children, resulting in weight loss and stunted growth, respectively. This condition is notably distinct from other causes of weight and muscle loss, such as starvation, aging, depression, malabsorption, and hyperthyroidism. A clinical diagnostic panel of cachexia was presented, including weight loss in the presence of an underlying illness, such as cancer, plus three of the following criteria: decreased muscle strength, fatigue, anorexia, low fat-free mass index, and biochemical abnormalities of certain serum markers, including C-reactive protein (CRP), interleukin-6 (IL-6), hemoglobin, and albumin. In 2011, a consensus based on the Delphi process defined cachexia as “a multifactorial syndrome defined by an ongoing loss of skeletal muscle mass—with or without loss of fat mass—that cannot be fully reversed by conventional nutritional support and leads to progressive functional impairment.” Pathophysiologically, cachexia results from a negative protein and energy balance driven by reduced food intake and metabolic dysregulation [ 1 ]. The experts also presented consensus statements for diagnosis based on three criteria, including body weight loss, body mass index (BMI), and sarcopenia. Finally, the European Society for Medical Oncology (ESMO) published the Clinical Practice Guidelines for Cancer Cachexia in Adult Patients in 2021 [ 15 ]. This guideline recommended defining cachexia as “a disease-related subtype of malnutrition identified by malnutrition screening tests, accompanied with at least one phenotypical criterion (weight loss, low BMI, or low muscle mass) and increased systemic inflammation” ( Box 1 ). Alt-text: Box 1 In addition to a clear definition and accurate diagnosis of cancer cachexia, clinicians also need a staging system to guide clinical practice. Fearon et al. defined cachexia as a continuum with three stages: pre-cachexia, cachexia, and refractory cachexia [ 16 ]. The severity of cachexia increases over time, but can also be partially alleviated through adequate interventions. In the stage of pre-cachexia, a slight involuntary weight loss (≤ 5%) was present, along with early clinical and metabolic signs including anorexia and metabolic changes. In pancreatic cancer patients, signs of pre-cachexia could be detected even 18 months ahead of diagnosis [ 7 , 17 , 18 ]. Patients who experienced a body weight loss of &gt; 5% within 6 months or weight loss of &gt; 2% along with either BMI &lt; 20 kg/m 2 or sarcopenia, but have not reached the standard of refractory cachexia, were classified into the stage of cachexia. These patients often displayed a reduction in food intake and increased systemic inflammation. Refractory cachexia was defined as an end-of-life phase, characterized by advanced cancer that was refractory to anticancer therapy and systemic abnormal activation of catabolism. These patients had a low-performance status (the World Health Organization [WHO] score of 3 or 4) and an expected survival of less than three months ( Figure 1 ) [ 1 ]. The cachexia score (CASCO) is a powerful tool developed to stage cachexia patients [ 19 ]. This score incorporated five main factors: body weight and lean body mass loss; anorexia; inflammatory, metabolic dysregulation, and immunosuppression; physical performance; and quality of life (QOL). All measurements could be carried out with relevant questionnaires to be completed by patients or biochemical and physical tests. The clinical validity of CASCO, and a simplified version called MiniCASCO (MCASCO), was evaluated in a study that prospectively enrolled 186 cancer patients and 95 controls [ 20 ]. Based on the results of clinical trials, CASCO and MCASCO are valid and easy-to-use tools for cachexia classification. However, these studies are single-center clinical trials with relatively small sample sizes, and multicenter studies with larger sample sizes are needed. As one of the most malignant tumor types, pancreatic cancer has the highest prevalence and earliest onset of cachexia [ 21 ]. Resembling other tumor types, cachectic pancreatic cancer patients suffer from both physical and neuropsychiatric symptoms such as anorexia, fatigue, and depression. However, pancreatic cancer patients have unique presentations due to the important role of the pancreas in the digestive and endocrine systems. Pancreatic exocrine insufficiency (PEI) results in maldigestion with concomitant abdominal discomfort, bloating, belching, diarrhea, and steatorrhea. The impairment of pancreatic islets leads to compromised endocrine function characterized by reduced glucose tolerance and even diabetes mellitus. The average degree of weight loss of pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) patients is among the highest across all tumor types [ 22 ]. Furthermore, distinct wasting phenotypes can be observed even in patients with the same diagnosis, staging, and treatment. Studies showed that most patients simultaneously experienced muscle and fat wasting (MFW) during FOLFIRINOX therapy or neoadjuvant treatment [ 23 , 24 ]. The rest of the patients showed either fat-only wasting (FW) or no-wasting (NW) presentations. Patients with the NW presentation showed higher response rates and better prognoses than the MFW and FW groups. Interestingly, no muscle-only wasting presentation was observed among these patients, suggesting that adipose tissue was more sensitive to pancreatic cancer cachexia. Another study found that pancreatic head tumors were associated with a higher prevalence and degree of weight loss than distal tumors [ 25 ]. These results indicated that cancer cachexia was heterogeneous across patients and influenced by the location of the primary tumor. MECHANISMS OF PANCREATIC CANCER CACHEXIA The Pivotal Role of Cachexia-inducing Factors The systemic nature of cancer cachexia makes it elusive. Therefore, alterations in molecular characteristics within the blood are often regarded as a crucial entry point for studying systemic diseases. Accordingly, there are multiple lines of evidence that most of the manifestations of cancer cachexia are closely linked to specific molecular features. Here, we summarize the roles and mechanisms of these cachexia-inducing factors to provide insights for interventions such as targeted therapy ( Figure 2 and Table 1 ). important culprit of cancer cachexia. Elevated CRP levels were found to be significantly associated with pancreatic cancer cachexia and poor prognosis [ 119 ]. However, the specific inflammatory factors that induce the initiation of cachexia remain elusive, because cancer cachexia is accompanied by the upregulation of a plethora of inflammatory factors. These factors can be produced by cancer cells, immune and stromal cells in the tumor microenvironment (TME), and involved organs, or released by tissues undergoing wasting. The initial factors derived from tumor cells may induce the systemic inflammatory response, resulting in a cascading release of inflammatory factors through either downstream regulation or crosstalk between pathways. As systemic inflammation is mostly evaluated in the period of an overt cancer cachexia presentation, the longitudinal trajectories of these factors frequently remain unclear [ 16 ]. Tumor necrosis factor family (TNF-α) TNF-α is one of the first factors identified to be associated with cachexia. Early in 1986, Beutler and Cerami isolated “cachectin”, which induced wasting in rabbits [ 120 ]. TNF-α was found to be predominantly produced by activated macrophages, but tumor cells are also a significant contributor to its secretion in pancreatic cancer patients. As a pro-inflammatory cytokine, TNF-α was found to be strongly associated with enhanced lipolysis, protein degradation, and anorexia [ 121 ]. These effects were largely attributed to the pivotal regulatory role of TNF-α in multiple signaling pathways. In vitro , it blocked adipocyte differentiation and adipogenesis via the Wnt signaling pathway [ 27 ], and stimulated lipolysis by activating the MAPK/ERK pathway, while also elevating intracellular cAMP [ 122 ] and JNK [ 123 ]. When treated with TNF-α, cultured muscle cells showed activation of the NF-κB pathway and downregulation of PI3K/Akt signaling, which led to protein loss and apoptosis of myotubes [ 29 , 30 ]. In vivo studies further demonstrated that TNF-α could stimulate the expression of ubiquitin and the E3 ubiquitin ligase atrogin1/MAFbx via p38 MAPK and Akt/FoxO4 [ 28 , 124 ]. This increased the ubiquitination and degradation of rat skeletal muscle proteins, while genetic knockout of TNF receptors was sufficient to block protein breakdown [ 125 ]. In addition, TNF-α decreased the expression of MyoD, which was found to be essential for skeletal muscle differentiation and satellite cell proliferation following muscle injury [ 126 ]. Intraperitoneal injection of recombinant human soluble TNF receptor in rats ameliorated anorexia, indicating negative modulation of appetite by TNF-α [ 127 ]. Corresponding to the multiple lines of experimental evidence that emphasized the vital role of TNF-α in cancer cachexia, serum TNF-α levels were shown to be negatively correlated with nutritional status and cachectic outcomes in pancreatic cancer patients [ 128 ]. TNF-like weak inducer of apoptosis (TWEAK, also known as TNFSF12) is a structural homolog of TNF-α, and together with its receptor Fn14, it was similarly found to be associated with sarcopenia, leading to its recognition as a new cachexia-inducing factor [ 129 , 130 ]. TWEAK was found to induce skeletal muscle wasting by increasing the transcription of E3 ligase: atrogin-1 and MuRF1, which subsequently led to degradation of myosin heavy chain (MyHC) via simultaneous inhibition of PI3K/Akt and activation of nuclear factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells (NF-κB) [ 32 , 33 ]. Interleukins Pancreatic cancer cells secrete interleukins, including IL-1, IL-6, and IL-8. Among them, IL-6 was deemed to have particular importance due to its prominent association with cachexia. It can be induced by TNF-α and IL-1, acting synergistically to induce systemic inflammation. IL-6 was shown to be significantly associated with pancreatic cancer occurrence, progression, prognosis, and cachexia in clinical studies [ 131 ]. Polymorphism of the IL-6-634G allele was associated with higher susceptibility to cachexia and decreased survival in Chinese pancreatic cancer patients [ 132 ]. Unlike TNF-α, which is involved in cachexia via multiple pathways, IL-6 mainly activates JAK, which phosphorylates STAT3 to execute its function in host metabolism and energy balance [ 35 ]. In different murine models, IL-6 was proven to induce wasting and retarded the growth of skeletal muscle through STAT3 activation [ 36 , 37 , 133 ] and expression of atrogin-I [ 38 ]. Enhanced IL-6 also led to lipolysis and thermogenesis [ 39 , 40 , 134 ]. In addition, IL-6 was found to regulate leptin production in adipocytes [ 135 ], which was closely correlated to ingestive behavior. Moreover, IL-6 was found to cause myocardial failure [ 41 ], hepatic acute-phase protein response (APPR) [ 42 ], and anorexia [ 35 ]. Blocking IL-6 binding to its receptor or genetic knockout of JAK2 [ 136 ] was sufficient to abrogate the cachectic phenotypes in mice [ 137 ]. Likewise, tumor-derived leukemia inhibitory factor (LIF), another important member of the IL-6 superfamily, was also identified as one of the drivers of cachexia in mouse models [ 43 ]. Apart from IL-6, interleukins such as IL-1β, IL-8, and IL-10 also play a role in systemic inflammation and pancreatic cancer cachexia. For instance, Scheede-Bergdahl et al. showed that in advanced cancer patients, IL-1β was a better predictor of cachectic conditions than IL-6 [ 138 ]. Despite IL-1 being able to directly cause loss of skeletal muscle and fat similar to IL-6, evidence also suggests that IL-1 primarily induces neurological alterations, e.g., activation of the anorexic and inhibition of the orexigenic pathway. IL-1 mRNA was enriched in the hypothalamus, and the protein level of IL-1 was increased in the cerebrospinal fluid of anorexic tumor-bearing rats [ 139 , 140 ]. Consistent with this finding, injection of IL-1 receptor antagonist into the hypothalamus improved food intake in rats [ 141 ]. In addition, IL-8 levels were also reported to be correlated with sarcopenia [ 45 ]. IL-8 released by human pancreatic cancer cells and stromal cells could induce muscle atrophy through the CXCR2–ERK1/2 axis [ 47 ]. These studies demonstrate the critical role of interleukins in the development of pancreatic cancer cachexia. Other inflammatory mediators The C-C motif chemokine ligand 2 (CCL2), also referred to as monocyte chemoattractant protein 1 (MCP-1), regulates the CCR2-dependent migration of immune cells. CCL2 was produced by immune cells and stromal cells, but could also be induced in pancreatic cancer cells [ 142 ]. It is tightly linked to cancer cachexia [ 143 ] through metabolic changes in skeletal muscle and adipose tissue, hepatic inflammation, and steatosis [ 48 , 144 ], as well as neuroinflammation [ 49 ]. Although the primary function of lipocalin-2 (LCN-2), also known as neutrophil gelatinase-associated lipocalin (NGAL), was initially identified to be mediating the innate immune response to bacterial infection, it is emerging as an important mediator of cancer cachexia. Its receptor melanocortin 4 receptor (MC4R) is a staple mediator of the central melanocortin system that controls appetite, food intake, and energy expenditure [ 145 ]. When binding LCN-2, it induces suppression of appetite [ 53 ], even eliciting severe hippocampal neuronal dysfunction and cognitive impairment [ 52 ]. Moreover, LCN-2 can also induce peripheral dysregulation, whereby LCN-2 derived from tissue-infiltrating neutrophils was found to induce ferroptosis and wasting in cancer cachexia [ 54 ]. Blocking LCN-2 function by oral administration of an MC4R antagonist revealed that LCN-2 may be a promising candidate for treating cancer cachexia [ 146 ]. Other potential inflammatory mediators related to cancer cachexia have also been reported, such as matrix metalloproteinase 9 (MMP9), which contributes to both cardiac fibrosis and extracellular matrix (ECM) changes in skeletal muscle [ 59 ]. Tumor-derived MMP1 was reported to cause fat-body remodeling and muscle detachment through activation of the transforming growth factor (TGF-β) signaling pathway [ 60 ]. CXCL16 can predict cachectic symptoms in newly diagnosed pancreatic cancer patients [ 147 ]. In addition, interferon-γ (IFN-γ) was found to induce remarkable wasting and reduced food intake in a JAK-STAT3 and NF-κB dependent manner [ 57 , 58 ]. S100A8 and S100A9 are calcium- and zinc-binding proteins that were recently identified as novel cachexia-inducing factors in pancreatic cancer patients [ 61 ]. Since systemic inflammation is the result of an intricate network of myriad inflammatory factors with diverse cellular origins, there was still a need to continue to explore the inflammatory factors that play a central role in this network, thus providing new insights into the genesis of pancreatic cancer cachexia and potential interventions. TGF-β superfamily The TGF-β superfamily consists of a series of pleiotropic cytokines that regulate vital biological processes and also participate in the development of cancer. With the evolving knowledge of TGF-β superfamily members beyond TGF-β itself, including myostatin, activin A (Act-A), and growth differentiation factors (GDFs), they are also emerging as potential targets for cachexia intervention. Sartori et al. demonstrated the importance of SMAD2 and SMAD3, two key candidates for TGF-β signal transduction, in controlling muscle mass [ 148 ]. Excess TGF-β mediated cachectic phenotypes, including muscle weakness, atrophy, and fibrosis [ [62] , [63] , [64] ], while blockade of TGF-β mitigated metabolic dysregulation and mortality in murine models [ 149 ]. Myostatin (MSTN or GDF8) and Act-A are TGF-β family members that limit muscle mass and muscle growth under physiological conditions [ 150 ]. They are primarily secreted by skeletal muscle in response to inflammatory cytokines but are also produced by cancer cells. These two TGF-β family ligands share multiple key components in their signaling cascades. For example, both of them are capable of binding the activin type II receptors Act RIIA and Act RIIB, while both their activities can be inhibited by follistatin (FSTL-3) and follistatin-related protein (FLRG) [ 151 ]. In cachectic patients, serum myostatin and Act-A levels were found to be increased and were significantly associated with muscle atrophy [ 68 ], progressive weight loss [ 152 ], and shorter survival [ 66 ]. In preclinical cancer models, systematic administration of myostatin was sufficient to induce profound muscle and fat loss analogous to that presented in human cachexia syndrome [ 153 ]. Myostatin was found to downregulate Akt/TORC1 signaling [ 69 , 154 ], concomitant with increased ubiquitin-proteasome pathway (UPP) activity [ 70 ]. Genetic inactivation of myostatin or the administration of an antagonist successfully abrogated the expression of MuRF1 and MAFbx/Atrogin-1, as well as autophagy-related genes [ 72 ], to ameliorate muscle atrophy and dystrophy, which improved muscle strength in murine studies [ 71 , 155 , 156 ]. Similarly to myostatin, Act-A also upregulated atrophic ubiquitin ligases and impaired the PI3K/Akt signaling pathway [ 68 ]. In addition, the detrimental effects of Act-A could be exacerbated by IL-6, while Act-A reversely curbed IL-6-induced APPR in the liver. Moreover, orthotopic pancreatic tumor-derived activin βA levels were associated with cachexia severity and induced subsequent activin expression in distant organs, including the liver, spleen, heart, kidney, skeletal muscles, and fat, thereby elevating the serum level of activin, which further induced activation of p38 MAPK and myotube atrophy [ 65 ]. Blockading its binding to ActRII or inhibition of the NF-κB and p38 MAPK pathways abolished cachectic effects in mice [ 157 , 158 ]. Overwhelming evidence also indicates that GDFs are cachexia mediators. GDF15 (also known as macrophage inhibitory cytokine 1 [MIC-1]), is a stress-response cytokine and a distant member of the TGF-β superfamily [ 159 ]. Higher circulating GDF15 levels were correlated with anorexia and low muscle mass in cancer patients [ 160 ]. In recent years, the mechanism of GDF15-mediated cachexia has been gradually refined. It acts by binding to its recently described receptor, glial-derived neurotrophic factor receptor alpha-like (GFRAL), which requires association with the coreceptor RET to activate intracellular signaling that promotes systemic metabolic effects and regulates energy homeostasis, as proved in mouse and nonhuman primate models [ 73 , 161 , 162 ]. In stressful states such as infection, injury, and cancer, the serum levels of GDF15 are upregulated to activate the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis, thus causing anorexia [ 74 ], further leading to weight loss [ 77 , 163 ], muscle atrophy, lipolysis, and cardiac muscle wasting through autophagy or the UPP [ 75 , 76 , 164 ]. GDF15-induced nausea and emesis also contribute to the exacerbation of anorexia [ 165 ]. In vivo , studies reported that tumor-derived GDF15 was sufficient to elicit a cachectic phenotype [ 78 ], while several preclinical studies also showed that blocking the GDF15–GFRAL axis through neutralizing antibodies restored muscle function, body weight, and appetite [ 79 , [166] , [167] , [168] , [169] ]. Phylogenetically, GDF11 is one of the nearest neighbors of myostatin, sharing receptors and performing similar functions. Harper et al. attempted to mitigate pathological cardiac hypertrophy and fibrosis by administering high doses of GDF11 in mice [ 170 ]. However, they reported devastating adverse effects of severe cachexia and premature death [ 170 ]. Other studies also showed that supraphysiological administration of GDF11 induced anorexia as well as skeletal and cardiac muscle atrophy [ 80 , 82 ]. These cachexia-mediating effects were partly attributed to the simultaneous upregulation of GDF15 [ 81 ]. Interestingly, the MEK inhibitors trametinib and U0126 rescued the inhibited differentiation and atrophy of myocytes by blocking the Ras-ERK pathway [ 83 ]. These results corroborate the role of the non-Smad signaling pathway in mediating the functions of TGF-β family members. In contrast to the negative regulatory role of most TGF-β family ligands, the bone morphogenetic protein (BMP)-SMAD1/5/8 axis is a protector of skeletal muscle mass [ 171 ]. Its downstream SMAD1/5/8 activates the mTOR signaling pathway to induce anabolism [ 172 ] while downregulating the expression of FBXO30, a ubiquitin ligase inducing muscle loss [ 173 ]. In mouse models and cancer patients, diminished BMP signaling was already observed as early as the onset of muscle wasting. The downregulation of BMP may be attributed to the increased expression of the BMP inhibitor Noggin triggered by tumor-mediated IL-6 and activin, which block BMP signaling in muscle fibers and motor nerves, thus causing impairment of the neuromuscular junction (NMJ), denervation, and muscle loss [ 174 ]. Catabolic factors In addition to the above-mentioned pro-inflammatory cytokines and TGF-β superfamily members, pancreatic cancer cells secrete a series of catabolic factors, contributing to the pathophysiology of cancer cachexia. Two of the best-characterized catabolic mediators are proteolysis-inducing factor (PIF) and lipid mobilizing factor (LMF, or zinc-a2-glycoprotein, ZAG). PIF is a proteoglycan that was initially isolated from a cachexia-inducing murine tumor (MAC16) in 1996 [ 175 ]. The presence of its human homolog was verified in human cancer cell lines [ 176 ] and detected in the urine of 80% of pancreatic cancer patients [ 177 ]. Urinary PIF levels were significantly correlated with weight loss [ 178 ], and PIF isolated from the urine of cachectic cancer patients was sufficient to induce cachexia in mice [ 179 ]. Two studies found no glycosylation sites in the human homolog, although they are critical for the proteolysis-inducing activity of murine PIF [ 180 , 181 ]. Despite this cross-species heterogeneity, both murine and human PIF receptors showed the same immunoreactivity, similar molecular mass, and function [ 182 ]. Moreover, murine and human PIF inhibited protein synthesis and promoted protein degradation in myotubes to a similar extent [ 84 ]. PIF-mediated proteolysis mainly involves UPP [ 183 ], regulated by NF-κB signaling [ 184 ]. In myotubes, PIF also induced the activation of PKCα, which might facilitate the phosphorylation and degradation of NF-κB inhibitor alpha (IκBα), an inhibitor of NF-κB [ 85 ]. Other muscle degradation cascades induced by PIF rely on the MAPK-ERK signaling pathway and the Ca 2+ -dependent degradation system. Smith and Tisdale found that PIF could induce phosphorylation of ERK to increase proteasome expression, and this effect was abolished by MAPK inhibitors [ 86 ]. Other studies reported that PIF could lead to a drastic rise of intracellular Ca 2+ , which in turn triggered a Ca 2+ -activated system to cause protein degradation [ 185 ]. However, evidence delineating the mechanism by which PIF inhibits protein synthesis is relatively scarce. The underlying mechanism may be the activation of double-stranded RNA-dependent protein kinase (PKR), which subsequently leads to increased eukaryotic initiation factor (eIF2) phosphorylation, thus impeding translation initiation [ 186 ]. Furthermore, a secondary function of PIF in the liver was observed in two studies. PIF was found to activate both NF-κB and STAT3 signaling pathways in hepatocytes and hepatic immune cells, resulting in the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines and decreased transferrin expression, thereby playing a role in hepatic acute-phase response (APR) [ 87 , 187 ]. Similar to PIF, LMF was also purified from the MAC16 adenocarcinoma model as well as from the urine of cachectic patients with unresectable pancreatic cancer [ 188 ]. Felix et al. further detected significantly elevated LMF levels in the serum and peritumoral stroma of cachectic pancreatic cancer patients [ 189 ]. Under physiological conditions, LMF is released from human white adipose tissue (WAT). This secretion is sufficient for the local catabolism of fat tissue itself, but only has a limited contribution to the circulating LMF pool [ 190 ]. In cancer patients, the serum level of LMF is profoundly increased, both because of the tumor-derived LMF and the abnormal secretion from adipose tissue induced by inflammatory cytokines [ 191 ]. This cachexia-related protein induces lipolysis, lipid mobilization, browning of WAT, and energy dissipation in both mouse and human adipocytes [ 88 , 89 ]. LMF binding to the β 3 -adrenoceptor induces intracellular cAMP production [ 192 ], leading to the upregulation of peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor γ (PPAR-γ), early B cell factor 2 (EBF2) [ 89 ], as well as uncoupling proteins-1, -2 and -3 (UCP-1/2/3) in adipose tissue, in addition to the expression of UCP-2 in skeletal muscle, liver, and cancer cells [ 90 , 193 ]. PPAR-γ, EBF2, and UCPs are key regulators of fat catabolism and brown fat cell fate [ 194 ], while UCP-2 was found to be involved in the detoxification of free radicals, partly accounting for the chemotherapy resistance of cachexia-inducing tumors [ 193 ]. Inadera et al. found that LMF can interfere with lipogenesis through SREBP-1 [ 195 ]. In addition, LMF was reported to act as an allosteric inhibitor of amine oxidase copper-containing 3 (AOC3), interfering with the relevant pro-inflammatory functions, suggesting versatile roles of LMF in cancer cachexia [ 196 ]. In addition to PIF and LMF, new candidates that may mediate pathological catabolism in pancreatic cancer cachexia are emerging. For example, serum insulin-like growth factor-binding protein 2 (IGFBP-2) levels may serve as a robust biomarker for malnutrition and muscle wasting in pancreatic cancer patients [ 92 ]. In the Drosophila model, tumor-secreted ImpL2 (IGFBP homolog in D rosophila ) induced systemic organ wasting [ 96 , 197 , 198 ], while the tumor cells could overcome the wasting effect by locally elevated Wnt signaling [ 199 ]. Tumor-derived IGFBP-3 impaired insulin/insulin-like growth factor (IGF) signaling to induce lipid loss [ 93 ], while also resulting in muscle wasting [ 95 ] and steatosis [ 94 ]. In addition, tumor metabolites such as lactate can also induce wasting. G-protein-coupled receptor 81 (GPR81) is the major mediator of this catabolic effect, independent of the well-established PKA catabolic pathway but relying on the Gi–Gβγ–RhoA/ROCK1–p38 signaling cascade [ 109 ]. Other molecules, including leptin [ 97 , 98 ], growth factors (VEGF/PDGF, etc.) [ 99 ], parathyroid-hormone-related protein (PTHrP) [ [100] , [101] , [102] ], insulin-like 3 peptide (INSL3) [ 104 ], pancreatic adenocarcinoma upregulated factor (PAUF) [ 105 ], and angiotensin II (Ang II) [ 107 , 108 , 200 ], were also identified as potential mediators of cachexia in pancreatic cancer. Tumor-derived extracellular vesicles In addition to secretory proteins, pancreatic cancer cells can also release a broad range of extracellular vesicles (EVs) with encased biologically active molecules, including proteins, metabolites, lipids, RNA, and even DNA. The cargos of EVs are encapsulated by a phospholipid membrane, facilitating their systemic transport and their role as messengers that mediate intercellular and inter-organ communication. In the pathophysiological process of pancreatic cancer, tumor-derived EVs are involved in several cancer hallmarks, including immune escape, drug resistance, remodeling of the tumor microenvironment (TME), metastasis, and cachexia [ 201 ]. For example, tumor cells were found to secrete EVs carrying heat shock protein 70 (Hsp70) and Hsp90, which induce severe catabolism and atrophy in mice via the activation of toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) [ 110 ]. Wu et al. suggested that this cachexia-inducing pathway was more profound in pancreatic cancer, as its tumor cells released remarkably higher levels of Hsp70 and Hsp90 (∼6-fold on average) than those of lung and renal cancer [ 103 ]. Other cachexia-inducing proteins such as IL-6, PTHrP, prolyl 4-hydroxylase subunit beta (P4HB), and adrenomedullin were also detected in EVs [ [111] , [112] , [113] , [114] ]. In addition to proteins, non-coding RNA (ncRNAs) are closely linked to cancer cachexia. Tissue miRNA profiling revealed that miRNAs were involved in the wasting of muscle (miRNA-206, miRNA-21, and miR-423-3p) and fat (miR-378) [ 118 , [202] , [203] , [204] ]. For example, cancer-derived exosomal miR-204-5p could target the von Hippel-Lindau (VHL) gene and reduce its expression, which led to the activation of leptin signaling and increased hypoxia-inducible factor 1A (HIF1A). The thus-activated hypoxic response subsequently triggered lipolysis and browning of WAT [ 116 ]. Other studies also found that MV-encapsulated cargos such as miR-21 could promote skeletal muscle apoptosis [ 117 , 205 ]. Conversely, inhibiting the release of EVs alleviated cachectic symptoms [ 206 , 207 ]. Multi-organ Alterations in Pancreatic Cancer Cachexia The systemic changes induced by pancreatic cancer are not limited to skeletal muscle and adipose tissue but influence multiple organs simultaneously. These effects may arise from tumor-derived cachexia-inducing factors, mechanical compression, and structural destruction, or can be the result of neuromodulation. Abnormalities in the functioning of these organs contribute to multi-organ presentations, and might in turn aid in the progression of pancreatic cancer cachexia, forming a vicious cycle that ultimately leads to terminal refractory cachexia. Skeletal muscle: The imbalance between catabolism and anabolism Loss of skeletal muscle mass is a defining manifestation of cancer cachexia, which results in loss of lean body weight, weakness, and reduced mobility, and is closely linked to the overall survival rate in pancreatic cancer patients [ [208] , [209] , [210] , [211] ]. Importantly, atrophy of the respiratory muscle leads to respiratory failure, an important cause of cancer-related death. Considering its salient importance, muscle wasting is one of the best-studied presentations of cancer cachexia. Overactivated proteolysis in skeletal muscle Generally, muscle atrophy is attributable to an imbalance between protein degradation and synthesis ( Figure 3 ). The overactivation of catabolic pathways such as JAK-STAT, MAPK-JNK/p38, NF-κB, and TGF-β-Smad induces excessive proteolysis in myocytes under cachectic conditions. This proteolytic effect is predominantly mediated by the downstream activation of the ubiquitin-proteasome pathway (UPP). The muscle atrophy F-box protein 1 (MAFbx, also known as atrogin-1) and the muscle RING finger containing protein 1 (MuRF1, also known as Trim63) are key components of the UPP. During cachectic muscle atrophy, the expression of MAFbx and MuRF1 is abnormally upregulated. MAFbx mainly catalyzes the ubiquitination of proteins that facilitate protein synthesis, while MuRF1 ubiquitylates the myofibrillar apparatus and components of the cytoskeleton, such as myosin heavy chain, myosin light chain, actin, and myosin-binding protein C. These factors result in the activation of the above-mentioned catabolic pathways that can transcriptionally upregulate E3-ligases to trigger the UPP, leading to protein degradation. For example, Yang et al. found that knockdown of Zrt- and Irt-like protein 4 (ZIP4) profoundly increased the survival and ameliorated muscle wasting and cachexia of the cell line-derived xenograft (CDX) model after distal pancreatectomy [ 215 ]. They found that the EVs released from pancreatic cancer cells containing HSP70 and HSP90 could stimulate the expression of Atrogin-1 and ubiquitin-protein ligase E3 component N-recognin 2 (UBR2) in myocytes through the activation of the p38-MAPK signaling cascade. This EV secretion process requires the CREB-regulated expression of RAB27B, while CREB acts as a zinc-finger transcription factor, the activation of which is dependent on ZIP4-mediated influx of Zinc ions [ 215 ]. In 2022, the same research group further emphasized the importance of Zip4 in pancreatic cancer cachexia. They found that acetyl-coenzyme A synthetase short-chain family member 2 (ACSS2) was induced by metabolic stress in tumor cells. They next identified Zip4 as a downstream target of ACSS2 based on transcriptomic data. Upregulated Zip4 was found to activate extensive signaling cascades, including increased phosphorylated glycogen synthase kinase 3 beta (GSK3β), which mediates the secretion of tumor necrosis factor superfamily member 10 (TRAIL, or TNFSF10), and decreases the release of anti-cachexia factors such as fibroblast growth factor 2 (FGF2) and IL-15. TRAIL then induces UPP activation in muscle tissues. Importantly, Zip4 was also found to potentiate macropinocytosis in tumor cells. The loss of skeletal muscle might result in the release of nutrients, such as branched-chain amino acids (BCAAs), which are taken up by tumor cells through macropinocytosis to maintain tumor fitness in the nutrient-deficient microenvironment [ 34 ]. In another study, Shi et al. further demonstrated that Zip4 promotes miR-373 expression through CREB [ 216 ]. MiR-373 acts as a transcriptional inhibitor of the vital phosphatase PHLPP to inhibit the PI3K/Akt signaling pathway. The activated Akt thereby upregulates cyclin D1 and phosphorylates STAT5, leading to tumor cell growth and the secretion of TGF-β, which ultimately leads to muscle atrophy [ 216 ]. This series of studies also revealed the pivotal role of Zip4 in the release of cachexia-inducing factors. Several other studies elucidated the detailed mechanisms of UPP activation in cachectic skeletal muscle induced by pancreatic cancer, including the increased oxidative stress [ 217 ], overactivation of the TGF-β and JNK pathway [ 218 , 219 ], altered zinc homeostasis [ 220 ], and downregulation of myocilin [ 221 ]. In addition to metabolic stress, the interaction with immune cells also induces pancreatic cancer cells to release excessive proteolytic factors. Liu et al. recently identified a novel feedforward loop between pancreatic cancer and macrophages that results in muscle wasting [ 222 ]. In this tumor-immune cell crosstalk, pancreatic cancer cells recruit and reprogram macrophages by the CCL2/CCR2 axis. Disruption of the CCL2/CCR2 axis or depletion of macrophages attenuated muscle atrophy in CDX models. Activated macrophages in turn release CCL5, which triggers a non-autonomous TWEAK-secretion program via the CCL5-CCR5-TRAF6-NF-κB signaling pathway in pancreatic cancer, after which TWEAK initiates muscle atrophy through the activation of NF-κB signaling and subsequent upregulation of MuRF1 [ 222 ]. Immune cells are not only enriched in the tumor microenvironment to govern the production of circulating cachexia-inducing factors but also infiltrate skeletal muscle tissues to directly affect proteolysis. For example, the infiltration of CD163 + macrophages in the cachectic skeletal muscle was found to be inversely correlated with muscle mass in pancreatic cancer patients. These macrophages aggravate muscle degeneration through the secretion of pro-inflammatory factors such as IL-1 and IL-6 [ 133 ]. Similar crosstalk between immune cells and skeletal muscles could also be induced by activated mast cells and Ly6G + myeloid cells, while depletion of these immune cells led to attenuation of wasting [ 12 , 223 ]. Apart from UPP, the autophagy-lysosome system and the Ca 2+ -activated degradation pathways are crucial regulators of proteolysis in skeletal muscle. Autophagy at the basal level is essential for the maintenance of biological function and structure in skeletal muscle, while its hyperactivation by excessive exposure to stress stimuli can drive severe proteolysis, as proved in esophageal, colorectal, and breast cancer [ [224] , [225] , [226] ]. In addition, UPP-inducing FoxO was found to trigger autophagy [ 227 ]. Mitophagy is a type of autophagy that specifically eliminates dysfunctional mitochondria. Mitophagy and related mitochondrial oxidative stress are actively involved in the muscle atrophy process of cancer cachexia [ 226 , 228 ], and targeting mitochondrial function may be a potential approach to improve muscle atrophy and energy status [ 229 , 230 ]. The calcium-activated degradation pathway constitutes a distinct proteolysis system. Elevated intracellular Ca 2+ levels activate calpains that can initiate the disassembly of myofibrils by specific breakdown of key structural proteins. This process facilitates further degradation by the UPP and autophagy. The activation of Ca 2+ -dependent proteolysis has been observed in the skeletal muscles and heart of cachectic model rats [ 231 ], and PIF might be the major regulatory factor [ 185 ]. Impaired protein synthesis Anabolic pathways of the skeletal muscle such as MAPK-ERK, PI3K-Akt-mTOR, and BMP-Smad1/5/8 are commonly impaired in cancer cachexia. Among them, the insulin/IGF1-PI3K-Akt-mTOR pathway plays a central role in regulating protein synthesis in skeletal muscle ( Figure 3 ). As a central regulator of cell growth and protein synthesis, mTORC1 promotes protein synthesis by phosphorylating ribosomal protein S6 kinase β-1 (S6K1) and eukaryotic translation initiation factor 4E-binding protein 1 (4EBP1). S6K1 enhances the translation of mRNAs encoding ribosomal proteins and elongation factors, while phosphorylated 4E-BP1 releases eukaryotic initiation factor 4E (eIF4E), allowing the initiation of cap-dependent translation. In addition to protein synthesis, this signaling pathway can inhibit the FoxO-induced UPP, especially via Akt phosphorylation. Under the influence of pancreatic cancer, this signaling cascade is disrupted at multiple layers. At the genetic level, polymorphisms of Akt were found to predict the risk of developing cachexia in pancreatic cancer patients [ 232 ]. The compromised endocrine function of the pancreas and hepatic inflammation result in insufficient serum levels of insulin and IGF. In addition, pancreatic cancer cells can secrete IGFBP-2 and IGFBP-3, which restrain the binding of insulin and IGF to insulin-like growth factor receptor (IGFR) [ 92 , 94 , 95 ]. Pancreatic adenocarcinoma upregulated factor (PAUF) is a recently described protein governing tumor growth and metastasis. Its association with body weight loss and muscle wasting has been proven in mice and pancreatic cancer patients. C2C12 myotubes treated with PAUF show a rapid inactivation of PI3K-Akt-FoxO3a signaling cascade and atrophic phenotypes [ 105 ]. Moreover, inflammatory factors such as IL-6, and TGF-β may potentially interfere with mTORC or other pivotal molecules in this pathway, further impairing protein synthesis in cachectic skeletal muscle [ 233 , 234 ]. Dysregulation of muscle progenitor cells Muscle progenitor cells function abnormally in the cachectic environment of pancreatic cancer, leading to a truncated source of repair and replenishment for skeletal muscle damage. Act-A derived from pancreatic cancer induces the expression of Twist1 in satellite cells [ 235 ]. Notably, Twist1 is a transcription factor essential for the specification of mesenchymal stem cell fate during embryogenesis. The overexpression of Twist1 resulted in extensive protein degradation, as well as the synthesis of another TGF-β family ligand in satellite cells, myostatin. However, it did not affect the expression of myoblast differentiation regulators such as MyoD, MHC, and Pax7. Autocrine myostatin acts synergistically with tumor-derived Act-A to induce Twist1 expression and functional impairment in satellite cells, forming a vicious cycle. He et al. reported a counterintuitive finding that pancreatic cancer cachexia was correlated with a specific type of skeletal muscle damage, which resulted in the activation of both satellite and non-satellite muscle progenitor cells [ 236 ]. Muscles from tumor-bearing mice and pancreatic cancer patients contained accumulating activated progenitor cells in the interstitium but displayed profoundly impaired regenerative capacity. This effect was attributed to the activation of the NF-κB signaling pathway by the serum cachectic factors. Surprisingly, they found that although the muscle progenitors exhibited a myogenic phenotype characterized by high expression of the self-renewing transcription factor Pax7, these cells underwent an aberrant process of both activation and differentiation. The overexpression of Pax7 was concomitant with the abnormal upregulation of CD34, a marker usually downregulated upon satellite cell activation, leading to the reduction of MyoD and myogenin, which are essential drivers of normal muscle regeneration. Accordingly, genetic ablation of Pax7 or exogenous supplementation of MyoD reversed muscle wasting by restoring the differentiation and fusion capacity of myoblasts. These studies illustrate the critical role of impaired muscle progenitor cell function in pancreatic cancer cachexia ( Figure 3 ). Other potential mechanisms In addition to the aforementioned dysregulations, other mechanisms also potentially account for the impairment of skeletal muscle in pancreatic cancer cachexia. Muscle apoptosis has been observed in cachectic mouse models and patients suffering from cancer or COPD [ 228 , 237 , 238 ]. Evidence from colon, esophageal, and lung cancer indicates that TNF-α, LCN-2, as well as tumor-derived extracellular vesicles containing P4HB or specific microRNAs, could lead to apoptosis or ferroptosis of skeletal muscle cells, partly through the downregulation of BCL-2 [ 54 , 112 , 118 , 122 ]. Importantly, studies also found that the pancreatic cancer cell lines PC1, Panc-2, and MIA-PACA could secrete microvesicles containing miR-21 to trigger apoptosis in C2C12 myoblasts through the TLR7/JNK axis [ 117 ]. Muscular dystrophy denotes a separate group of muscle disorders characterized by severe muscle wasting, commonly linked to genetic mutations in the components of the dystrophin-glycoprotein complex (DGC). Despite marked disparities in the pathogenesis of atrophy and muscular dystrophy, DGC was also observed to be functionally or structurally disrupted during cancer cachexia [ 239 , 240 ], which not only resulted in membrane damage but also activated the UPP to trigger protein degradation [ 241 ]. However, the specific roles of these mechanisms in skeletal muscle damage in pancreatic cancer patients remain to be elucidated. Adipose tissue: Overactive lipolysis and thermogenesis The depletion of fat depots is another major clinical manifestation as well as a powerful parameter for early detection and prognostic assessment of pancreatic cancer cachexia [ 242 , 243 ]. Fat wasting frequently occurs before or independently of muscle atrophy [ 244 ], and there are significantly more differentially expressed genes in adipose tissue than in the muscles of pancreatic cancer patients [ 245 ], which implies that adipose tissue is more sensitive to the stress of cancer cachexia [ 23 , 24 ]. Lipolysis in pancreatic cancer cachexia Similar to muscle wasting, the loss of adipose tissue is partly attributable to reduced food intake, which reduces the global nutrient supply. In addition, tumor-induced systemic inflammation induces a whole-body hypermetabolic state, resulting in the mobilization of stored triglycerides to support the high energy demand with fatty acids. Accordingly, the predominant mechanism of fat loss in cancer cachexia is lipolysis. Rydén et al. found that insulin-induced lipogenesis and the numbers of adipocytes exhibited no statistically significant difference among cachectic cancer patients, weight-stable cancer patients, and cancer-free controls, while the fat cell size was profoundly decreased [ 246 ]. This finding corroborates that loss of fat mass in cancer cachexia patients was mainly attributed to atrophy rather than inhibited lipogenesis or adipocyte death. Lipolysis is finely regulated by three lipases, including adipose triglyceride lipase (ATGL), hormone-sensitive lipase (HSL), and monoglyceride lipase (MGL) ( Figure 4 ). Evidence from both animal studies and cancer patients indicated that cAMP-PKA-controlled ATGL and HSL overexpression was the main factor driving abnormal lipolysis in cancer cachexia, while selective inhibition of ATGL and HSL could protect mice from fat wasting and weight loss [ 247 , 248 ]. Although insulin and IGF-activated PI3K can deplete intracellular cAMP to inhibit lipolysis under non-catabolic conditions, cancer cachexia is frequently concomitant with pancreatic endocrine dysfunction or insulin resistance, diminishing this inhibitory effect. The significant intracellular increase of these two lipases is accompanied by an enhanced lipolytic effect of catecholamines and natriuretic peptides, two major regulators of adipocyte lipolysis that increase cAMP and cGMP levels to phosphorylate lipases by PKA and PKG [ 249 ], as well as the increased expression of several lipolytic factors, including LMF, TNF-α, and IL-6. In addition, the β 1 adrenoreceptor (ADRB1) was found to be overexpressed in adipose tissue, greatly increasing the catecholamine-induced lipolytic rate and intracellular HSL levels [ 250 ]. These results also illustrate that adipose tissue is more susceptible to lipolytic hormones in the systemic inflammatory environment of cancer cachexia. Although seemingly contradictory, these results may reflect two aspects of a feedback inhibitory mechanism, which is insufficient to counteract the overactivation of lipolysis. Moreover, Rohm et al. pointed out that CIDEA may mediate cachexia-triggered AMPK degradation, thus aggravating fat wasting [ 251 ]. The dual roles of CIDEA in fat wasting merit further investigation. The role of adipose tissue browning To meet the body's abnormally increased energy expenditure due to pancreatic cancer, adipose tissue undergoes a shift from a reserve to a thermogenic state, i.e., from WAT to brown adipose tissue (BAT). WAT mainly stores excess energy as triacylglycerols (TAG), while BAT specializes in thermogenesis, oxidizing lipids to generate heat to maintain normal body temperature. The browning of WAT requires an increasing number of mitochondria, which results in the characteristic brown color and expresses uncoupling protein 1 (UCP-1) to dissipate energy as heat by uncoupling the electron transport chain from ATP production, a process referred to as non-shivering thermogenesis ( Figure 4 ). In mouse models and pancreatic cancer patients, UCP-1 expression and WAT browning, as well as an elevated body temperature, were found at the initial stage of cancer cachexia, prior to skeletal muscle atrophy [ 17 , 39 , 40 , 254 ]. The mediators of WAT browning show considerable overlap with those of lipolysis. Several pancreatic cancer-derived lipolytic factors are capable of inducing excessive thermogenesis in WAT, such as glucose-regulated protein 75 (GRP75) [ 255 ], LMF [ 90 ], IL-6 [ 39 ], PTHrP [ 100 ], and miR-204-5p [ 116 ]. However, although LCN-2 promoted ATGL expression to cause lipolysis in KPC mice, it instead inhibited UCP-1 expression in WAT adipocytes, leading to a dramatic loss of BAT, which consequently led to hypothermia [ 56 ]. Considering the early occurrence of WAT browning in the development of cancer cachexia, such relevant biomarkers might potentially serve as novel indicators for the early detection of cachexia. The crosstalk between adipose tissue and skeletal muscle It should be noted that in addition to its energy storage function, adipose tissue is also a major endocrine organ that releases hormones and adipokines. For example, subcutaneous adipose tissue was estimated to be accountable for up to 35% of systemic IL-6 levels [ 256 ]. Under cachectic conditions, the browning of adipose tissue further enhances its adipokine secretion to exert systemic metabolic effects. Some of the secreted proteins can serve as serum markers of lipolysis, such as pentraxin 3 [ 257 ]. Anorexigenic factors such as leptin aggravate the reduction of nutrient intake. Adiponectin, Act-A, and IL-6 might further facilitate the development of pancreatic cancer [ 258 ] or exacerbate the wasting of skeletal muscle. For example, Rupert et al. demonstrated an interactive network among pancreatic cancer, skeletal muscle, and adipose tissue [ 36 ]. In patient-derived xenograft (PDX) models, IL-6 from PDAC cells initiated a profound alteration in the transcriptional landscape of both myocytes and adipocytes, leading to increased IL-6 expression in fat tissue and IL-6 plus IL-6R in skeletal muscle. Surprisingly, despite a high level of mRNA expression in muscles, the change of IL-6R protein levels in the quadriceps was not significant in KPC mice. Moreover, IL-6R protein was found to be enriched in adipose tissue, implicating that the high level of IL-6R mRNA expressed in myocytes resulted in the production of soluble receptors, which accumulated in fat tissue to induce lipolysis through the JAK-STAT signaling pathway activated by both tumor-derived and autocrine IL-6. Accordingly, depletion of IL-6 in tumor cells mitigated adipose wasting and myosteatosis, as well as atrophy in the skeletal muscle [ 36 ]. In addition to adipokines, the adipose tissue-derived free fatty acids might promote the development of insulin resistance and accumulate in myocytes [ 259 ]. The accumulation of reactive lipid metabolites such as ceramide in skeletal muscles might impair mitochondrial function, generate reactive oxygen species (ROS), and induce muscle atrophy [ 260 ]. Such crosstalk leads to a vicious circle, perpetuating the systemic metabolic dysregulation in pancreatic cancer cachexia. Impairment of pancreatic function The pancreas is a pivotal organ with both exocrine and endocrine functions, playing an important role in body homeostasis. The exocrine pancreas produces and secretes enzymes that are essential for digestion. These enzymes enter the small intestine and enable the digestion and absorption of nutrients. The exocrine pancreas also secretes bicarbonate to neutralize the stomach acid to create an optimal pH environment for digestive enzymes. The endocrine aspect of the pancreas involves the islets of Langerhans. The primary hormones produced by the pancreas are insulin and glucagon, which are crucial for the regulation of glucose homeostasis. In pancreatic cancer patients, both the exocrine and endocrine functions are disrupted, leading to malnutrition and altered glucose metabolism, which exacerbate the process of cachexia. Pancreatic exocrine insufficiency (PEI) PEI is defined as insufficient pancreatic enzyme activity to maintain normal digestion, which is attributed to reduced enzyme production, altered enzyme activation, or early enzyme degradation [ 261 ]. Steatorrhea is a hallmark symptom of PEI, but it does not occur until pancreatic enzyme levels are reduced to below 5%–10% of normal production. Several tests are available for assessing PEI, such as fecal elastase-1 (FE-1), coefficient of fat absorption (CFA), and the 13 C breath test with mixed-chain triglycerides (MCT). In 2022, Khan et al. provided a staging system of PEI based on the results of the FE-1 test, CFA, serum fat-soluble vitamins, and clinical symptoms [ 262 ]. PEI is highly prevalent among pancreatic cancer patients. In a prospective study, 66% of patients with pancreatic head tumors were affected by PEI at diagnosis, and within several months, PEI was observed in 92% of the cases [ 263 ]. The incidence of PEI might be correlated with tumor site, as a meta-analysis found that the pooled prevalence of PEI in pancreatic head tumor patients was 56%, significantly higher than in body-tail tumor patients (32%), possibly due to the higher risk of main pancreatic duct obstruction [ 264 ]. Iatrogenic factors also contribute to PEI, as both pancreaticoduodenectomy (PD) and chemotherapy were found to be associated with a greater risk of PEI. Over half of patients without pre-operative exocrine insufficiency developed PEI after PD [ 265 , 266 ], while those undergoing distal pancreatectomy only presented transient and less severe functional impairment [ 267 , 268 ]. The development of PEI has a multifaceted course. Mechanistically, pancreatic head tumors can obstruct the pancreatic duct and lead to fibrosis, hindering the draining of pancreatic secretions [ 269 ]. The invasion of pancreatic cancer remodels the intra- and peri-tumoral microenvironment into a dense desmoplastic, poorly perfused, and hypoxic stroma. This extensive stromal alteration shrinks the functional area and compromises the capacity of the pancreas to regenerate functional tissue. Brune et al. indicated that even neoplastic precursor lesions such as intraductal papillary mucinous neoplasms (IPMNs), and pancreatic intraepithelial neoplasia (PanINs) could induce lobular atrophy [ 270 ]. In addition to the direct effects on exocrine function caused by obstruction and structural destruction, pancreatic cancer also pathophysiologically impairs pancreatic secretion. Under physiological conditions, mechanical or chemical stimuli such as smell, swallowing, and nutrients trigger the release of endogenous cholecystokinin (CCK) and secretin, which are central intestinal hormones that promote pancreatic secretion. Pancreatic cancer can impair the physiological pancreatic secretion at multiple levels. The pancreas is innervated by dense sympathetic nerve fibers, so the increasing secretion of adrenergic factors reversely potentiates tumor development, leading to a cancer-nerve feedforward loop [ 271 ]. Although evidence for an inhibitory effect of sympathetic nerves on pancreatic exocrine function is controversial, the remodeling of innervation can potentially dysregulate its physiological function. Parasympathetic and cholinergic receptors, commonly regarded as facilitators of pancreatic secretion, are overexpressed in tumor tissues. However, pancreatic cancer cells often lose their normal secretory function, and these highly expressed receptors instead become drivers of tumor growth [ 272 ]. Moreover, perineural invasion (PNI), which is a prominent hallmark of pancreatic cancer observed in over 80% of patients, provides an alternative route for cancer metastasis and pain, which can be detrimental to the nervous control of pancreatic exocrine function. Apart from nervous dysregulation, alterations in pro-secretory hormones and receptors might also contribute to PEI. Although the levels of these intestinal hormones were comparable in pancreatic cancer patients and healthy controls [ 273 ], several studies observed changes in their receptors. Patients with neoplastic pancreatic diseases showed reduced levels of secretin receptors or expressed alternatively spliced variants [ 274 ]. CCK receptors were also differentially expressed in pancreatic cancer [ 275 ], as CCK-A receptors were selectively expressed in cancer lesions. These distinct characteristics might compromise the binding of secretin and CCK to their respective receptors, indicating that reduced tissue sensitivity to pro-secretory signals, rather than the hindered hormone secretion, is the main contributor to the development of PEI. Although the mechanism of pancreatic cancer-induced PEI remains incompletely understood, robust evidence points to an association between PEI and cancer cachexia. Prospective studies observed that sarcopenia in patients with pancreatic disease was strongly associated with the presence of PEI [ 276 ]. Postoperative PEI was found to interfere with the patient's weight gain [ 277 ]. Danai et al. found that altered exocrine function was the main driver of adipose tissue wasting in genetically engineered mouse models [ 278 ]. The researchers implanted PDAC cells isolated from autochthonous tumors subcutaneously or orthotopically into the mouse pancreas to exclude the effect of PDAC-derived systemic factors. Although the mice were injected with the same number of tumor cells and the solid tumors in the respective locations exhibited similar histological features, significant peripheral fat loss was seen only in mice bearing pancreatic in situ tumors, and this cachectic effect could be reversed by a diet supplemented with pancreatic enzymes. These results demonstrated an independent and direct role of PEI in cancer cachexia [ 278 ]. However, there is relatively little evidence for the mechanism through which PEI induces cachexia. PEI-related maldigestion with concomitant abdominal discomfort, bloating, and diarrhea may impair the patients’ appetite. Deficiency of pancreatic digestive enzymes further leads to a reduction in the efficiency of food absorption, contributing to malnutrition [ 279 ]. Gross nutritional deficiencies result in the inability of skeletal muscle and fat to maintain physiological structure and function, and a reduction in the levels of antitumoral micronutrients may promote tumor progression, thus exacerbating cancer cachexia. For example, the plasma levels of several n-3 polyunsaturated fatty acids were reduced in pancreatic cancer patients compared with other cancer patients without weight loss [ 280 ]. Moreover, previous studies indicated that these fatty acids could sensitize skeletal muscle to insulin to induce anabolic effects and inhibit catabolic pathways [ 281 ]. Lack of fat-soluble vitamins such as vitamin D and vitamin E was also frequently observed in PEI patients [ 282 , 283 ]. The pivotal role of vitamin D in cachexia and sarcopenia was well reported [ 284 ]. Firstly, the reduced binding of vitamin D to its receptor VDR decreases the activation of the MAPK/ERK signaling pathway and suppresses the expression of MyHC [ 285 , 286 ]. Secondly, reduced vitamin D levels lead to feedback overexpression of VDR, resulting in negative regulation of myogenesis and aberrant differentiation [ 287 ]. The role of other nutritional factors that mediate PEI-induced cachexia needs to be explored to provide valuable evidence for nutrition support therapy. Changes in pancreatic endocrine function Pancreatic cancer can destroy the structure of pancreatic islets, and the resulting endocrine dysfunction is mainly reflected in diabetes mellitus. Pancreatic cancer-induced diabetes mellitus is similar to type 2 diabetes mellitus in that it manifests as an impairment of β-cell function and insulin resistance in peripheral tissues. Mechanistically, these two phenotypes are partially attributed to pancreatic cancer-secreted exosomes containing adrenomedullin, which promotes endoplasmic reticulum stress and increases reactive oxygen/nitrogen species. Studies also reported that S100A8 and β-cell-derived islet amyloid polypeptide (IAPP) may be potential mediators [ 288 , 289 ]. Tumor-derived N-terminal peptide of S100A8 was found to impair glucose catabolism, thus inducing hyperglycemia in vivo [ 290 ]. IAPP could reduce glycogen synthesis in myoblasts [ 291 ]. In contrast to PEI, the abnormal endocrine function seemed to resolve after PD surgery [ 292 , 293 ], but this conclusion was still controversial [ 294 ]. Diabetes can occur even a year ahead of the diagnosis of pancreatic cancer, and new-onset diabetes has been widely recognized as an important manifestation of early-stage pancreatic cancer [ 295 ]. More than half of pancreatic cancer patients suffer from diabetes mellitus [ 264 ]. Diabetes was associated with both PEI [ 296 ] and weight loss [ 297 ]. The underlying mechanism might be decreased levels of serum insulin, which impair systemic anabolisms [ 298 ]. Alterations in the nervous system: Neuroinflammation and anorexia Pancreatic cancer cachexia is accompanied by dysregulation of the central nervous system (CNS), primarily in the form of anorexia. Anorexia leads to reduced overall nutritional intake and exacerbates the progression of cachexia. The central melanocortin system within the arcuate nucleus of the hypothalamus is the key coordinator of appetite. It contains neurons expressing orexigenic factors, including agouti-related protein (AgRP) and neuropeptide Y (NPY) that stimulate appetite and reduce energy expenditure, as well as neurons expressing anorexigenic factors such as pro-opiomelanocortin (POMC) as well as cocaine- and amphetamine-regulated transcript (CART), which play an opposite role to the former two. In addition to these traditional neurons, an increasing number of neurons associated with appetite regulation are being discovered. For instance, a recent study found that hypothalamic BNC2 neurons activated by leptin could acutely suppress food intake [ 299 ]. Under physiological conditions, signaling inputs from peripheral hormones such as insulin, leptin, GLP-1, and ghrelin modulate the action of these neurons to alter appetite. Under the abnormal conditions of cachexia, increased systemic levels of inflammatory factors such as TNF-α, IL-1b, and CCL-2, or invasion of the CNS by circulating myeloid cells [ 300 ], lead to profound neuroinflammation, which critically changes the composition and gene expression in multiple cell types of the mediobasal hypothalamus (MBH). For example, tumor-derived pro-inflammatory factors induce the expression of IL-1 and TNF-α mRNA in the hypothalamus, initiating an inflammatory cascade. This hypothalamic inflammation subsequently results in the reduction of AgRP/NPY and increased expression of POMC/CART [ 140 ]. Single-cell RNA sequencing of MBH from a pancreatic cancer mouse model found inflamed endothelial cells, stressed oligodendrocytes, and several subtypes of microglia [ 301 ]. Burfeind et al. observed profound infiltration of microglia and astrocytes both in MBH and blood, whereby microglia were found to be protective against cachectic manifestations such as activation of HPA, anorexia, and muscle catabolism during pancreatic cancer [ 302 ]. In addition to inflammation, tumor or immune cell-secreted anorexigenic factors regulate appetite directly or through alterations of NPY/AgRP and POMC/CART signaling. In pancreatic cancer cachexia, activated neutrophils secrete LCN-2 [ 303 ], which crosses the blood–brain barrier (BBB) and binds to MCR4 in MBH, resulting in anorexia [ 55 ] and wasting [ 56 ]. Yeom et al. established a D rosophila cancer anorexia model by overexpressing yorkie ( yki ), a transcriptional coactivator of the Hippo pathway, and found that Dilp8, the Drosophila homolog of mammalian INSL3, mediated anorexia via the downregulation of orexigenic short neuropeptide F (sNPF) and upregulation of anorexigenic nucleo-binding 1 (NUCB1) [ 104 ]. The same phenomenon was observed in mouse models of pancreatic cancer. In mice, tumor-derived or intracerebrospinally injected INSL3 stimulated the hypothalamic cells to express NUCB2 and reduce NPY. In pancreatic cancer patients, serum INSL3 was significantly associated with anorexia [ 104 ]. Leptin and serotonin are also regulators of the melanocortin system and appetite [ 304 ]. However, there is still little evidence for their association with pancreatic cancer cachexia [ 305 ]. GDF15 is increasingly recognized as one of the most critical anorexigenic factors in the progression of cancer cachexia. GDF15 was reported to be enriched in the serum and associated with cachectic symptoms in advanced pancreatic cancer patients [ 306 ]. Moreover, its primary receptor, the heterodimer of GFRAL and RET, was predominantly expressed in the hindbrain, which corroborated the central role of GDF15 in anorexia and might explain an important reason why anti-GDF15 therapy achieved such excellent efficacy in clinical trials, as we will discuss later in the Implications for Clinical Interventions section. Apart from direct effects on the melanocortin system, pancreatic cancer conveys inflammatory signals to the CNS by PNI. In the KPC mouse model, ablation of sensory neurons through neonatal capsaicin injection not only slowed the growth and progression of orthotopic tumors but also prevented astrocyte activation and neuronal damage in the CNS [ 307 ]. Similarly, Imoto et al. reported that pancreatic cancer activated astrocytes in the spinal cord via PNI, resulting in loss of weight, skeletal muscle, and fat [ 308 ]. Depression and fatigue are common psychiatric symptoms of pancreatic cancer [ 309 ]. These presentations are attributable to neuroinflammation and can be greatly ameliorated by genetic knockout of myeloid differentiation factor 88 ( MyD88 ), which plays a pivotal role in the innate immune system [ 10 ]. These phenotypes, plus the pain, diarrhea, and abdominal discomfort induced by pancreatic cancer, can further exacerbate the loss of appetite in pancreatic cancer patients. Additionally, depression itself can be one of the major triggers of weight loss and inadequate nutritional intake. Moreover, such systemic inflammation-induced dysregulation of the nervous system can reversely orchestrate the immune system through effects on leukocyte trafficking and function, potentially further aggravating systemic inflammation and cancer cachexia [ 310 ]. Liver: Altered metabolism and acute-phase response The tumor-induced systemic inflammation and subsequent cachexia can reshape hepatic metabolism and function. Cachexia-inducing factors, particularly IL-6, can induce extensive transcriptional alterations in hepatocytes [ 136 , 311 ]. The metabolic alterations in the liver encompass not only increased hepatic gluconeogenesis but also impaired ketogenesis [ [212] , [213] , [214] ]. Flint et al. found that in both pre-cachectic and cachectic PDAC mice, the hepatic mRNA levels of ACADM and HMGSC2 were significantly reduced, thus suppressing the expression of PPAR-α, which mediates the conversion of free fatty acids into ketones [ 35 ]. The reduced ketogenesis results in hypoketonemia, which exacerbates metabolic stress during periods of caloric deficiency. This in turn triggers a stress-induced increase in serum glucocorticoid levels, which suppress the intratumoral immune response, causing the failure of immunotherapy [ 35 ]. This result also corroborated that the development of cachexia requires intact glucocorticoid signaling [ 312 ]. Apart from metabolic reprogramming, pro-inflammatory cytokines initiate the hepatic APR, a spontaneous reaction normally in response to infection and injury. During APR, hepatocytes actively synthesize acute-phase proteins (APPs) such as CRP, serum amyloid (SA), fibrinogen, and α1-acid glycoprotein (AGP), but reduce the production of proteins such as albumin, resulting in hypoalbuminemia. Although it remained to be proved whether APR could promote the progression of cachexia, Reeds et al. proposed that the priority of active hepatic APP synthesis triggered the mobilization of proteins from skeletal muscle and other tissues, leading to a negative nitrogen balance [ 313 ]. Moreover, APR facilitates the intrahepatic infiltration of inflammatory cells such as CD68 + macrophages, which produce IL-1 and IL-6 to aggravate systemic inflammation [ 9 , 314 ]. Disrupted hepatic protein synthesis may in turn contribute to the production of cachexia-inducing factors such as Act-A [ 65 ], as well as the reduction of anabolic factors such as IGF. Moreover, APPs themselves might act as mediators of muscle wasting, as observed in several other diseases [ 315 , 316 ]. Additional organs: Gut, heart, lung, and blood system In addition to the well-described participation of the above-mentioned organs, others are also affected by or contribute to the progression of pancreatic cancer cachexia. The gut microbiota is inextricably linked with the carcinogenesis, progression, and therapeutic responsiveness of pancreatic cancer. The cancer-induced systemic inflammation and PEI can reshape the composition of gut microbiota, as well as impair the integrity of the intestinal barrier [ 317 ], thus facilitating not only the entry of microbiota-derived endotoxin and metabolites into the bloodstream [ 318 , 319 ] but also the translocation of gut microbiota into other organs, such as the pancreas. The extensive alterations of the serum metabolome, proteome, and intratumoral implantation of bacteria all contribute to the progression of pancreatic cancer [ 320 ], and may influence cachectic presentations such as muscle atrophy [ 321 , 322 ] and neuroinflammation. One direct example emphasizing the key role of gut microbiota in pancreatic cancer cachexia was shown by Li et al., who found that KPC allografts exhibited increased intestinal permeability, which was correlated with the degree of cachexia [ 323 ]. The gut microbiota-secreted lipopolysaccharide (LPS) induced neuroinflammation via the upregulation of hypothalamic CCL-2 and IL-6 expression. This inflammation could be further exacerbated by tumor-derived prostaglandin E2 (PGE2), leading to anorexia and muscle catabolism. The addition of PGE2 alone was sufficient to amplify the hypothalamic IL-6 release when incubated with low levels of LPS, while the COX-2 inhibitor celecoxib rescued the pancreatic cancer-induced cachexia. This study provided novel insights into the complicated tumor–gut–brain crosstalk [ 323 ]. Although there is a dearth of studies focusing on the role of specific bacterial species in cancer cachexia, exploring the mechanisms by which the gut microbiota, and even the oral or intratumoral microbiota, influence cachexia may provide promising therapeutic approaches. Wasting of the heart muscle is a common and lethal manifestation of cancer cachexia, contributing to the significant increase in cardiac mortality among cancer patients. Myocardial atrophy is usually accompanied by cardiac remodeling, dysfunction, and aberrant metabolism, presenting as a pattern of reduced fractional shortening, which indicates compromised cardiac contractility, reduced heart rate, and abnormalities in other hemodynamic parameters [ 324 ]. This myocardial dysfunction is associated with a switch between the MyHC isoforms, i.e., from the adult myosin heavy chain-A (MHC-A) to the fetal myosin heavy chain-B (MHC-B), accompanied by a shift from adult glucose transporter 4 (GLUT4) to the fetal GLUT1. Similar to skeletal muscle, studies indicated a role of the UPP in cardiac atrophy [ [325] , [326] , [327] ]. However, multitudinous studies pointed out that skeletal and cardiac muscle presented distinct responses to cancer cachexia, i.e., the cachectic presentations of the heart were not dependent on the UPP, but were mainly attributed to mTOR-regulated autophagy overactivated by cachexia-inducing factors [ 328 , 329 ]. Apart from circulating factors, the cardiotoxicity of cancer treatment might exacerbate heart impairment. More importantly, chronic cardiac failure itself could result in cardiac cachexia, worsening the patient's status and making cancer cachexia an obstinate syndrome. Lastly, pancreatic cancer cachexia has been associated with impaired pulmonary function [ 330 , 331 ], anemia [ 332 ], thrombosis [ 333 ], osteopenia [ 334 ], and splenomegaly [ 335 ]. All these organic functional impairments constitute the basic pathophysiological mechanisms underlying the nature of pancreatic cancer cachexia—a multi-organ syndrome initiated by the cancer, but exacerbated by complicated inter-organ crosstalk. Other Factors Influencing Pancreatic Cancer Cachexia Several other factors were reported to influence the development of pancreatic cancer cachexia. For example, male patients had a higher cachexia prevalence than females in the Chinese population (40.5% vs 32.7%) [ 4 ]. In mouse models of pancreatic cancer cachexia, male mice were found to develop earlier and more severe cachexia than female mice [ 335 , 336 ]. This sexual dimorphism might be the result of the overactivation of Act-A-ACVR2B signaling in male mice, while the skeletal muscle of female mice expressed higher levels of activin inhibitors such as FSTL1 and FSTL3. Lifestyle factors such as smoking may also exacerbate cachexia, as nicotine was found to induce IL-8 secretion from the stroma of pancreatic cancer [ 337 ]. The interaction between obesity and cancer cachexia is still the subject of active debate, as both share numerous pathological similarities such as chronic inflammation and altered glucose metabolism. However, some studies also suggest obesity may potentially be protective against the development of cachexia [ 338 ], potentially due to the excess energy reserves in obese patients. Finally, the dysregulation of the circadian rhythm, which has gained increasing attention for its role in cancer development, also contributes to the progression of cancer cachexia [ 339 ]. EXPERIMENTAL MODELS OF PANCREATIC CANCER CACHEXIA The establishment of preclinical models is indispensable for studying the molecular features and regulatory mechanisms that drive the gradual transition from early to refractory cachexia. The models for pancreatic cancer cachexia are listed in Table 3 , Table 4 . For example, cell lines such as C2C12 myoblasts and 3T3-L1 adipocytes can serve as facile tools to examine the effects on skeletal muscle and adipose tissue, respectively. Animal models allow the investigation of systemic alterations and longitudinal progression of cancer cachexia. Various CDX models were established to investigate cancer cachexia. The colon-26 (C26) model of colorectal cancer (CRC) [ 340 ] and LLC model of Lewis lung carcinoma [ 341 ] have been widely applied to elucidate the mechanisms of cancer cachexia, recapitulating significant weight loss, muscle, and fat wasting, as well as disorders of hepatic and neuroendocrine functions, such as hypoglycemia and hypercortisolism. For pancreatic cancer, numerous cell lines present options for CDX models, such as Mia PaCa-2, FC1412, and Pan02. However, there is great heterogeneity among the reported CDX models, whereby different cell lines exhibit distinct cachexia-inducing capacities. For example, Pa04C elicits significant cachexia in nude mice, while PanC1 only has a limited cachexia-inducing capacity [ 342 ]. The number of injected cells also affects the cachectic pattern in CDX models. Wyart et al. successfully generated a slow-paced model of pancreatic cancer cachexia by reducing the number of subcutaneously injected cells to 0.7 million, which delayed the appearance of the apparent cachexic presentation until five weeks after injection [ 343 ]. The route of administration is also important. Orthotopic injections are invasive and might lead to complications, while ectopic injections usually result in the formation of tumors growing at a high rate that cannot reflect the impact of the tumor on its original organ. Notably, cell subtypes with different cachexia-inducing capacities might also be present within the same cell line, which provides a good control group for the study of cachectic mechanisms [ 222 , 344 , 345 ]. Compared with CDX models, PDX may better simulate the pathophysiological status of human pancreatic cancer cachexia. PDX models established from tumor cells of patients with more severe cachexia exhibited more pronounced cachectic presentations and similar molecular patterns, such as IL-6 expression, as well as a comparable response to therapeutic agents [ 36 ]. In addition, unlike the LLC and C26 models, the cachectic response of the PDX model was independent of the tumor size, as was observed in pancreatic cancer patients [ 346 ]. However, most PDX models utilized nude mice or non-obese diabetes scid gamma (NSG) mice, lacking the antitumoral immune response. Furthermore, the ability of tumor cell implantation models to recapitulate human cachexia needed to be scrutinized, because they do not form spontaneous tumors and lack native components of the tumor microenvironment. Genetically engineered mouse models (GEMMs) develop naturally occurring tumors with mutational signatures following a progressive trajectory similar to human cancer. LSL-Kras G12D/+ ; LSL-Trp53 R172H/+ ; Pdx1- Cre (KPC), LSL - Kras G12D/+ ; LSL-Trp53 fl/fl ; Pdx1- Cre (KP −/− C), and LSL-Kras G12D/+ ; Ink4a/Arf fl/fl ; Pdx1- Cre /+ (KPC/INK4) are the most widely used GEMMs in the study of PDAC [ 347 , 348 ]. These models develop tumors with a rich desmoplastic stroma as well as metastasis, causing systemic dysfunction in skeletal muscle, fat, heart, liver, and CNS. LSL-Kras G12D /+ ; Pdx1 -Cre (KC) mice mainly develop premalignant lesions (pancreatic intraepithelial neoplasia [PanIN]), which progress spontaneously to invasive and metastatic tumors at a low frequency, providing a good experimental model for the precursor lesions and early cachexia of pancreatic cancer. At the age of 15 weeks, an age before the formation of invasive cancer, wasting of fat and skeletal muscle can already be observed in KC mice, indicating that cachexia occurs as early as the stage of premalignant lesions [ 278 ]. However, most of the above-mentioned models can not fully recapitulate the clinical patterns of human cachexia, as the tumors that they form tend to be large, typically exceeding 10% of total body weight, and are often highly aggressive, giving rise to significant cachectic manifestations within a short time frame (normally 1–2 weeks). Furthermore, in pancreatic cancer patients, 95% of differentially expressed genes are downregulated in muscle biopsy samples, whereas they are predominantly upregulated whether in the LLC, C26, or KPC models. The LSL-Kras G12D/+ ; Ptf1a ER - Cre/+ ; Pten fl/fl (KPPC) mice are a newly established GEMM that models human pancreatic cancer-induced cachexia. This model allows the induction of tumors in older mice (1 year of age) to achieve experimental conditions mimicking cancer in older patients, and exhibits a steeper survival curve than that of KPC mice, facilitating the testing of anti-cachexia drug efficacy. Importantly, the gene expression pattern of cachectic muscles from the KPPC model resembled that of pancreatic cancer patients, providing a more robust tool to investigate the mechanism of cachexia [ 349 ]. However, caution must be taken as the KPPC model did not recapitulate a metastatic phenotype of pancreatic cancer, which is found in most patients. Although these early animal models provided important insights into the mechanisms of cachexia, the questions of when and why cachexia begins remained unanswered. To address this, Winnard et al. generated a reporter system by grafting a myoblast cell line expressing a dual tdTomato:GFP construct into the biceps femoris muscle region of the CDX model [ 350 ]. This reporter allows noninvasive live imaging of early cachectic events, and a strong tdTomato fluorescence signal from skeletal muscle grafts can be detected in mice with weight loss of only 1.2%–2.7% and tumor burdens of only 79–170 mm 3 , providing a powerful tool for monitoring the initiation of cachexia [ 350 ]. In addition to evolving animal models, the advent of emerging technologies has brought new opportunities for this area. Patient-derived organoids (PDO), as in vitro cultured 3D models, can mimic the architecture of their source organ/tissue and thus exhibit biological features similar to those found in vivo [ 351 ]. PDO models have significant potential for exploring the mechanisms of pancreatic cancer development and high-throughput drug screening, while their high degree of concordance with in vivo tumors enables us to explore the mechanisms and interventions of pancreatic cancer cachexia using co-culture, implantation, or secretomics [ 352 , 353 ]. Organ chips, also known as organ-on-a-chip, are microfluidic cell culture devices that mimic the structure and function of human organs. These chips integrate living cells in a microenvironment to simulate physiological conditions, offering a platform for studying organ-level responses to drugs and diseases without human or animal testing [ 354 ]. This newly developed model may simulate the complex interactions between cancer cells, stromal cells, muscle, and other tissues, reflecting the systemic effects of cachexia, and facilitate the investigation of metabolic changes, inflammatory responses, and cell signaling pathways implicated in cachectic progression. They also enable real-time monitoring and precise control over experimental conditions, enhancing study reproducibility and reliability in identifying potential therapeutic targets for managing cachexia in pancreatic cancer patients. Therefore, organ chips may potentially offer promising avenues for studying cancer cachexia. IMPLICATIONS FOR CLINICAL INTERVENTIONS The complex mechanism of pancreatic cancer cachexia has complicated the development of anti-cachexia treatments. To date, no available therapies have been approved by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for clinical use. Although the current state is not favorable, emerging basic and clinical research continues to offer new insights and opportunities for managing cancer cachexia. In this section, we discuss several therapeutic strategies that have shown promise in the management of cancer cachexia. These include nutritional interventions, exercise, pharmacological treatments, psychological support, and multimodal care ( Figure 5 ). Nutritional Interventions Nutritional interventions aim to support patients by improving their food intake, managing metabolic alterations, and maintaining muscle function while reducing treatment-related toxicities. Nutritional counseling encourages cancer patients to consume diets rich in calories and protein to counteract the hypercatabolic state. In patients with cancer cachexia, the administration of specialized nutrition can decrease muscle wasting by reducing inflammation and mitigating metabolic changes. It has been demonstrated that omega-3 fatty acids can enhance food intake, muscle mass, and body weight in cachectic patients [ 367 ]. A cohort study of pancreatic cancer patients found a correlation between omega-3 fatty acid supplementation and body weight gain [ 368 ]. Another supplement, l -carnitine, helps to turn fat into energy and exhibits both anti-inflammatory and anti-proteolytic effects. A multicenter clinical trial indicated that l -carnitine supplementation improved body weight and quality of life in patients with advanced pancreatic cancer [ 369 ]. Additionally, the essential amino acid leucine and its metabolite β-hydroxy β-methylbutyrate (HMB) have been shown to benefit protein metabolism [ 370 ]. A systematic review confirmed that HMB supplementation could benefit muscle mass and function in cachectic patients [ 371 ]. Preliminary research suggested that a ketogenic diet—a high-fat, low-carbohydrate regimen that induced the production of ketone bodies—might alleviate cancer cachexia, but clinical trials have yet to confirm its benefits, and ketogenic diets might even lead to insufficient energy intake and weight loss because of unpalatability. The European Society for Parenteral and Enteral Nutrition (ESPEN) guidelines on nutrition in cancer patients do not recommend using a ketogenic diet before, during, and after the application of anticancer agents [ 372 ]. Interestingly, iron therapy has shown efficacy in enhancing muscle strength in cancer patients, although further research is needed [ 373 ]. Exercise Exercise can have a beneficial effect in cancer cachexia by mitigating muscle atrophy and enhancing muscle function [ 374 ]. ESPEN guidelines recommend maintenance or increased physical activity in cancer patients [ 372 ]. Although several types of exercise, such as aerobic, resistance, and flexibility exercises were recommended by the American College of Sports Medicine (ACSM) [ 375 ], randomized controlled trials involving pancreatic cancer or head and neck cancer patients indicated that resistance training was more effective in alleviating cancer cachexia, as those who engaged in progressive resistance training exhibited greater improvements in muscle mass and function, as well as in quality of life compared to those following self-selected exercise routines [ 376 , 377 ]. Pharmacological Treatments Recent progress in understanding the nutritional and metabolic alterations and the underlying biological pathways has facilitated the development of novel pharmacological strategies. These approaches aim to enhance food intake, quench inflammation, and regulate metabolism. Appetite stimulants Anorexia is one of the most common and severe symptoms in patients with cachexia. Appetite stimulants such as progestins and steroids have been proven effective in managing anorexia in cachectic patients. Progestogens (megestrol acetate and medroxyprogesterone acetate) might stimulate appetite through the release of neuropeptide Y (NPY) in the hypothalamus and inhibition of pro-inflammatory cytokine secretion. Corticosteroids were found to reduce prostaglandin activity and suppress pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1 and TNF-α [ 378 ]. Nevertheless, these benefits were transient (less than four weeks) and accompanied by potential side effects, including insulin resistance and infections. Thus, ESPEN nutritional guidelines recommend that corticosteroids be used only for 1–3 weeks [ 372 ]. Cannabinoids such as cannabidiol (CBD) have long been investigated for potential therapeutic applications. However, the available evidence does not support their efficacy in stimulating appetite in patients with cancer cachexia [ 15 ]. Dronabinol and nabilone, both synthetic forms of tetrahydrocannabinol (THC), were FDA-approved to manage nausea and vomiting related to cancer chemotherapy. However, their effectiveness in treating cancer cachexia remains uncertain due to limited and inconsistent research findings. As a result, they are not currently recommended for cachexia management [ 372 ]. Some clinical trials explored appetite-regulating neuropeptides, including ghrelin (often called “hunger hormone”) and its mimetics such as anamorelin, for their potential in treating cancer cachexia. Ghrelin transmits hunger signals to the CNS to increase appetite. Furthermore, it stimulates the secretion of growth hormones, inhibits muscle catabolism, and promotes intestinal peristalsis [ 379 ]. The available evidence indicates that the short-term use of synthetic gastric starvation hormones is safe and well tolerated [ 380 ]. Anamorelin, a synthetic ghrelin receptor agonist, enhanced ghrelin secretion and mimicked its appetite-stimulating effects [ 381 ]. An integrated analysis of two Phase II clinical trials indicated that anamorelin improved body weight, lean soft tissue mass, and quality of life in patients with various solid tumors [ 382 ]. Depression contributes to anorexia in cancer patients, while the application of antidepressants such as olanzapine and mirtazapine may alleviate depressive symptoms, as well as nausea and vomiting, to improve anorexia in cachectic patients, which was recommended by clinical guidelines [ 15 ]. Additionally, leptin is emerging as a promising candidate for addressing the profound appetite loss associated with cancer cachexia, but this strategy requires further research. Anti-inflammatory agents Cancer cachexia is associated with pro-inflammatory cytokines, and high levels of TNF-α are believed to be critical in its progression. Thalidomide, which inhibits TNF-α synthesis, has effectively reduced weight loss and preserved lean body mass in patients with advanced malignant tumors [ 383 ]. However, the anti-TNF-α monoclonal antibody infliximab failed to mitigate weight loss in patients with non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) and pancreatic cancer [ 384 , 385 ]. Similar to TNF-α, IL-6 is a key factor in the progression of cachexia. Tocilizumab, an anti-IL-6 monoclonal antibody, has shown promise in mouse models of cancer cachexia, improving the overall prognosis [ 386 ]. Additionally, tocilizumab was found to alleviate inflammation, improve nutritional status, treat anemia, and enhance physical condition in patients with refractory cancer cachexia [ 387 ]. Another anti-IL-6 antibody, ALD518, has demonstrated good tolerability and the potential to enhance quality of life in Phase I and II clinical trials in patients with advanced NSCLC [ 388 ]. IL-6 acts via the JAK-STAT pathway, making JAK inhibitors another focus of cachexia research. The JAK2 inhibitor ruxolitinib, in conjunction with capecitabine, was found to prolong survival in patients with metastatic pancreatic cancer [ 389 ]. However, a Phase II clinical trial of ruxolitinib combined with regorafenib for recurrent or refractory metastatic CRC was prematurely terminated due to the absence of significant improvement in overall survival (OS) [ 390 ]. Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), such as cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2) inhibitors, indomethacin, and ibuprofen, play a role in reducing the release of APPs and cytokines such as IL-6. COX-2 participates in the induction of IL-6 and TNF expression through prostaglandin synthesis. The selective COX-2 inhibitor celecoxib has shown promise in two Phase II clinical trials, improving quality of life and body weight in cachectic patients with gastrointestinal tumors [ 391 , 392 ]. Lundholm et al. found that indomethacin prolonged survival in patients with pancreatic cancer [ 393 ]. Additionally, ibuprofen was reported to reduce CRP levels, enhance body weight and muscle mass, as well as aid in weight management in pancreatic cancer patients [ 394 ]. TGF-β and GDFs exert both strong anorexigenic and inflammatory effects in cachectic patients. Some studies highlighted the potential of trabedersen, an antisense oligonucleotide that targets the transcript for TGF-β2, in improving the OS of pancreatic cancer patients [ 395 ]. NGM120 is an antibody that blocks the GDF15 receptor GFRAL. The initial results of a Phase Ia/Ib study of NGM120 showed its pathway engagement evidenced by increased lean body mass and body weight. Importantly, the anti-GDF15 monoclonal antibody ponsegromab has recently finished its Phase II clinical trial ( NCT05546476 , 2022). In this 12-week trial including 187 patients with solid malignancies, patients treated with ponsegromab had significantly greater weight gain and overall activity levels than those in the placebo group, without an increase in adverse events [ 396 ]. In addition, the weight gain increased notably in a dose-dependent manner. The prominent effect of ponsegromab in reducing cachexia symptoms confirmed the role of GDF-15 as a key driver of cancer cachexia, offering hope to fill the gap in anti-cachexia drugs through continuous exploration of underlying mechanisms and new therapeutic targets. Although similar to GDF15, GDF11 was reported to promote muscle atrophy associated with cancer cachexia, and its therapeutic potential has not yet been assessed in clinical trials. Furthermore, the MEK1 and MEK2 kinase inhibitor selumetinib has demonstrated efficacy in preclinical studies to alleviate skeletal muscle loss through ERK inhibition and AKT activation [ 397 ]. A Phase II study to evaluate oral selumetinib in the treatment of KRAS G12R -mutant PDAC is ongoing [ 398 ]. Metabolic agents Comprehensive metabolic dysregulation of cancer cachexia complicates treatment strategies. Several new drugs were developed to counteract the extensive protein degradation associated with skeletal muscle atrophy or to activate protein synthesis pathways to improve the outcomes of cachectic patients. Various agents have been developed to interfere with the anti-myogenic Act-A-SMAD signaling pathway. Although ActRIIB treatment prolonged the lifespan of mice with rhabdomyosarcoma, a clinical trial designed to treat malnourished patients was terminated due to unsatisfactory results [ 399 ]. Another ActRII blockade antibody, BYM338, is currently being investigated in a clinical study ( NCT01433263 ) for its potential to address unintentional weight loss in patients with lung or pancreatic cancer. Testosterone and its synthetic derivatives, known as anabolic steroids, enhance muscle mass by stimulating protein synthesis. While low doses of testosterone can improve insulin sensitivity, high doses may lead to insulin resistance. In a randomized controlled trial involving 475 cancer patients, flumethasone was found to be less effective than megestrol and dexamethasone in boosting appetite [ 378 ]. Conversely, nonsteroidal selective androgen receptor modulators (SARMs) could retain anabolic effects with minimal impact on the androgen pathway, offering a promising alternative. SARMs have demonstrated the potential to increase muscle mass without side effects associated with traditional anabolic steroids. Currently, the POWER trial is investigating the efficacy of the SARM enobosarm in a Phase III clinical trial for cancer patients [ 400 ]. The β 2 -adrenergic agonists have been demonstrated to be powerful promoters of muscle growth. They were found to offer protective benefits for skeletal muscle and the heart by inhibiting protein degradation [ 401 ]. Research involving formoterol and other β 2 -adrenergic agonists has demonstrated promising outcomes in the treatment of muscle atrophy in tumor-bearing rat models [ 402 ]. However, the activation of β-adrenergic signaling reduced body fat and might participate in pancreatic cancer progression. Thus, β-adrenergic receptor blockers, commonly known as β-blockers, have also been employed to treat cachexia. Espindolol, a non-specific β 1 /β 2 -adrenergic receptor antagonist, has been demonstrated to have pro-anabolic, anti-catabolic, and appetite-stimulating effects, which are mediated by β-receptors and the central 5-HT1A receptor. In the ACT-ONE trial, espindolol was effective in reversing weight loss and enhancing grip strength among non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) and CRC patients [ 403 ]. Natural compounds found in commonly consumed foods are also gaining attention for their potential in anticancer treatment. Given their safety and efficacy, these compounds were also investigated to prevent muscular dystrophy and cachexia in cancer patients. Notably, resveratrol [ 404 ], myricanol [ 405 ], and diarylheptanoids [ 406 ] derived from curcumin have been tested for their capacity to inhibit muscle proteolysis, manifesting promising results. Finally, extensive preclinical evidence revealed the roles and therapeutic potential of heterogenous cachexia-inducing factors in this lethal disease. For well-characterized factors such as IL-6, TNF-α, PIF, and LMF, despite a clearly understood mechanism in cancer cachexia, there is still a lack of targeted drugs that showed promising efficacy in clinical trials, possibly reflecting the complex essence of cancer cachexia. The identification of emerging cachexia-inducing factors such as GDF15, LCN-2, TWEAK, and IGFBPs, or their respective receptors not only bridges gaps in our understanding of the cachexia mechanisms but also offers valuable therapeutic targets for designing antibodies and small molecular inhibitors. Tumor-derived metabolites such as lactate illustrate the pivotal role of metabolic reprogramming in cancer cachexia, providing new opportunities for metabolic therapies. Blocking the release of tumor-derived EVs can cut off the systemic transport of vesicle-encapsulated factors, which may be the prime culprit of cancer cachexia. In addition to the secreted factors, it is also important to curb the tumor itself. Targeting key regulators such as ZIP-4, which plays a hinge role in pancreatic cancer progression, metastasis, metabolic dysfunction, and factor secretion, may bring multifaceted benefits. Further research and drug development are needed to explore their translational potential in human trials. Psychological Support Patients with tumors are three to four times more likely to experience depression compared to the general population, and those suffering from cancer cachexia show an even higher propensity for depressive states. In addition, depression is a contributor to anorexia. Thus, active management of depression might kill two birds with one stone. The typical treatment regimen involves pharmacological interventions, including selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) such as fluoxetine and norepinephrine, as well as specific serotoninergic antidepressants (NaSSAs) such as mirtazapine. PERT PEI contributes to indigestion in pancreatic cancer patients. Pancreatic enzyme replacement therapy (PERT), administered orally, has been demonstrated to be effective in promoting weight gain, preventing further weight loss [ 407 ], and prolonging overall survival [ 408 ]. Several clinical guidelines, including ESPEN guidelines on nutrition in cancer patients, recommend the application of PERT in patients with pancreatic cancer. Multimodal Care The development of pancreatic cancer and the associated cachexia is the consequence of multilayer, multi-organ, and multifactor interactions. Accordingly, effective prevention and treatment strategies require comprehensive and multimodal approaches. These include not only pharmacological treatment but also nutritional intervention, exercise, and psychosocial support. Such a multidisciplinary effort requires collaboration among oncologists, radiologists, surgeons, dietitians, nutritionists, and physical therapists. For instance, a multimodal feasibility study involving patients with malignant lung and pancreatic cancer showed that a combination of nutritional supplements and celecoxib, in conjunction with exercise, contributed to improved weight maintenance [ 409 ]. In addition to the booming treatment strategies to counteract cancer cachexia, the origin of cachexia—the cancer itself—should never be ignored. The biological mechanisms driving tumor growth and metastasis are closely linked to the development of cachexia. Moreover, cancer cachexia can increase the toxicity of cancer treatments, which in turn may exacerbate cachexia itself. Therefore, an integrated treatment strategy that simultaneously curbs cancer and cachexia will be the most effective measure for enhancing the quality of life and prolonging the survival of cancer patients. CONCLUDING REMARKS AND PERSPECTIVES Cancer cachexia is a multifaceted syndrome with a remarkably high prevalence in pancreatic cancer patients. Cachexia has a catastrophic impact on the quality of life and prognosis of pancreatic cancer patients, which has led to ever-increasing interest in this field. The definition, diagnosis, and classification of cancer cachexia have gone through several modifications, reflecting the evolving knowledge of this syndrome. Current diagnostic criteria and staging systems, such as the CASCO, offer frameworks for identifying and classifying cachexia. However, there is still a lack of internationally recognized criteria for the diagnosis and classification of cancer cachexia that can be widely applied in clinical practice. In addition, given the severity and refractory nature of cancer cachexia, early diagnosis and intervention are particularly important. In patients with pancreatic cancer, signs of cachexia often precede the cancer diagnosis by months or even years [ 6 , 7 , 17 ]. Thus, signs such as abnormal weight loss may indicate the presence of pre-cachexia in a relatively early stage. Blood markers such as IL-6, TNF-α, and other molecules that play an important role in the development of cachexia likewise have the potential to serve as tools for early surveillance of cachexia. More precise biomarkers and diagnostic tools to detect cachexia at earlier stages and to effectively monitor disease progression are still urgently needed. Although primarily characterized by loss of skeletal muscle and fat, burgeoning studies suggest that cachexia is more a course of multi-organ interactions. In terms of clinical manifestations, pancreatic cancer cachexia not only involves skeletal muscle and adipose tissue with resulting weight loss and emaciation, but also leads to anorexia, fatigue, indigestion, cardiac and hepatic dysfunction, endocrine dysregulation, and even osteoporosis, resulting in systemic abnormalities. In terms of mechanisms, cachexia-inducing factors derived from cancer cells, immune and stromal cells, or affected tissues are the main contributors to these systemic alterations, leading to systemic inflammation and metabolic dysregulation. Functional changes in the involved organs, such as anorexia, APR, gut dysbiosis, and neuroendocrine dysregulation, in turn exacerbate the progression of the cachectic condition through insufficient nutrient intake and abnormal release of hormones or cachexia-inducing factors. In situ , pancreatic cancer also impairs the pancreatic exocrine and endocrine function to foster indigestion and glucose dysregulation. This intertwined network of interactions results in a complex and intractable cachectic pathophysiology. Therefore, future research directions and therapies for pancreatic cancer cachexia should not exclusively focus on muscle atrophy and fat loss without considering the systemic alterations. Diverse models have been used to study pancreatic cancer cachexia, with mouse models predominating. The multitude of model options also raises the question of which model can best simulate the reality of cachexia in pancreatic cancer patients. Tumor cell implantation models are easy and fast to establish, but form too aggressive tumors and lack key components of the TME. GEMMs appear to serve as more appropriate models for pancreatic cancer cachexia, but the genetically induced pattern of muscle and fat loss is still distinct from that observed in patients. The emerging KPPC model, despite being much closer to patients in terms of gene expression patterns in wasting tissues, also fails to mimic the metastatic characteristics of pancreatic cancer. The development of novel animal models that better recapitulate pancreatic cancer cachexia is, therefore, necessary, and cutting-edge technologies such as organoids and organ chips may also shed new light. Finally, therapeutic strategies are evolving, with a focus on targeting the underlying inflammatory and metabolic pathways. Anti-inflammatory agents, anabolic therapies, and nutritional support are being actively investigated, but effective treatments remain scarce. Furthermore, the root of pancreatic cancer cachexia is ultimately pancreatic cancer itself. Therefore, it is crucial to explore the molecular targets and pathways that play a pivotal role in the development of cachexia as well as the progression of pancreatic cancer per se. Targeting such key molecules may simultaneously curb pancreatic cancer progression and associated cachexia. In conclusion, recent studies have provided multitudinous inspirational insights into the diagnosis, staging, mechanisms, experimental models, and therapeutic strategies of pancreatic cancer cachexia. Although our focus is primarily on pancreatic cancer, many of the findings presented in this review can be extrapolated to other cancers and other diseases causing cachexia. Future research efforts are expected to develop and validate novel biomarkers for the early detection and monitoring of cachexia. Additionally, the exploration of interventional approaches that target both cachexia and pancreatic cancer holds promise for improving patient outcomes.
