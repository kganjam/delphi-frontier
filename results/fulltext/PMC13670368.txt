INTRODUCTION Dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM) is a leading cause of heart failure and sudden cardiac death, characterized by ventricular dilation and impaired systolic function. Most DCM cases are idiopathic but some result from genetic mutations, environmental factors or acquired injury ( Heymans et al., 2023 ; Stergiopoulos and Lima, 2019 ; Hershberger and Jordan, 2024 ). Men tend to be more frequently and severely affected by DCM than women, suggesting sex-specific differences in disease susceptibility and progression ( Alcos and Loepke, 2021 ). Although over sixty genes have been linked to familial DCM, the underlying disease mechanisms remain incompletely resolved ( Franaszczyk et al., 2017 ). Primary mutations in sarcomere proteins, especially those affecting the thin filament, provide powerful entry points for dissecting core pathways of cardiac dysfunction. Among these, pathogenic variants in actin alpha cardiac muscle 1 (ACTC1), although rare, are particularly informative because actin is essential for myofibril assembly, contractile force generation and Ca 2+ regulation ( Frank et al., 2019 ; Greve et al., 2024 ; Garg et al., 2024 ; Despond and Dawson, 2018 ). Additionally, ACTC1 mutations can produce either hypertrophic or dilated phenotypes, and affected individuals often exhibit variable disease onset and severity, with incomplete penetrance observed within the same family ( Lakdawala et al., 2012 ). A coding DNA (c.) mutation of nucleotide cytosine (C) to thymine (T) at position 383 (c.383C&gt;T) mutation in human ACTC1, is associated with familial dilated cardiomyopathy ( Lakdawala et al., 2012 ; Olson et al., 1998 ). This C&gt;T nucleotide transition leads to a threonine-to-isoleucine amino acid substitution at position 128 in the nascent ACTC1 protein, resulting in the ACTC1 p.T126I variant of the mature ACTC1 protein. In vitro assays determined that the p.T126I variant causes reduced Ca 2+ sensitivity of thin filaments, reduced myosin ATPase activity and sliding velocity ( Teng and Dawson, 2020 ). However, how this mutation triggers progressive heart failure in vivo , potential contribution of sex-specific disease pathogenesis and the underlying molecular mechanisms is still unclear. Zebrafish are a powerful vertebrate model for cardiac research due to their genetic tractability, external fertilization and short generation time, which enable detailed longitudinal studies of heart development and disease at embryonic, juvenile and adult stages ( Narumanchi et al., 2021 ; González-Rosa, 2022 ). Their cardiac structure, physiology and gene expression profiles are highly conserved with humans, making zebrafish a valuable system to investigate human cardiomyopathies ( Bowley et al., 2022 ). These features facilitate precise dissection of molecular and functional consequences of disease-causing variants − such as the ACTC1 T126I variant − in a living system. Here, we generate and characterize zebrafish carrying the Acta1b (p.T126I) mutation, orthologous to the human DCM-linked ACTC1 p.T126I variant. By integrating longitudinal analyses of cardiac phenotype, function, morphology and gene expression, we reveal that T126I induces variable, adult-onset DCM with striking sex-specific differences in remodeling and progression. These findings highlight how a thin filament mutation drives progressive dilated cardiomyopathy via sex-specific molecular remodeling, emphasizing sex as a key modifier of disease progression. RESULTS Distinct phenotype manifestations, variable-onset mortality and sex-specific survival in zebrafish carrying the human ACTC1 p.T126I variant In humans, the c.383C&gt;T (p.T126I) mutation in ACTC1 has been associated with dilated cardiomyopathy ( Lakdawala et al., 2012 ). Our prior in vitro work has demonstrated that the ACTC1 T126I variant reduces thin filament Ca 2+ sensitivity, myosin ATPase activity and actomyosin sliding velocity ( Teng and Dawson, 2020 ). To explore in vivo consequences, we investigated phenotypic and molecular changes in zebrafish harboring the orthologous T126I mutation ( acta1b hsc230 ). Zebrafish carrying one or two copies of acta1b hsc230 exhibited a spectrum of cardiac phenotypes with variable onset. A subset (10−15%) of mutant larvae displayed severe early cardiac dysfunction characterized by bradycardia, pericardial and body edema, heart degeneration, and absence of swim bladder inflation, resulting in lethality between 4 and 7 days post fertilization ( Fig. S2A-C ). A large proportion of mutants survived larval stages without overt abnormalities; these are the focus of this study to characterize the adult-onset cardiac phenotypes and disease pathway. Adult homozygous and heterozygous mutants appeared morphologically normal until 12 months of age. Thereafter, the incidence of phenotypic abnormalities and mortality increased, and began to accelerate at ∼19 months − one exception being a homozygous male with severe edema at 6 months ( Fig. 1 , Fig. S2D-Q, S3A,B ). Body surface areas, which are based on individual weights, were comparable between most groups, except for homozygous females, who were significantly larger than wild-type females and homozygous males at older stages ( Fig. S3C ). Phenotypes were variable among and within sex and genotype groups, consistent with incomplete penetrance and variable expressivity seen in cardiac actinopathies in humans ( Franaszczyk et al., 2017 ; Sucharov et al., 2019 ; Keseroglu et al., 2023 ). Acta1b hsc230 adults were considered pre-symptomatic if they showed no overt mutant phenotype but displayed underlying cardiac dysfunction or morphological abnormalities. Mutants were categorized as symptomatic once they exhibited one observable sign of cardiovascular disease, and severity was determined based on the number or scale of symptoms ( Fig. S3A,B ). Both heterozygous and homozygous mutants of both sexes developed pericardial effusion correlating with ventral protrusion of the heart ( Fig. 1B,C,E-I ; Fig. S3B ) ( Wang et al., 2017 ). Some homozygous males exhibited severe generalized body edema, thereby mimicking gravid females ( Fig. 1C , Fig. S2H ). Remarkably, some adult mutants displayed progressive pupil-size reduction, predominantly in the left eye, days to weeks before cardiac symptoms ( Fig. 1J–M , Fig. S3B ). Additional abnormalities, such as spontaneous hemorrhaging, gasping-like behavior and difficulty swimming, were also observed ( Fig. S3B ). When using an adapted frailty assessment analogous to human cardiac patients ( Chandiramani and Ali, 2025 ), mutants scored significantly lower and showed reduced feeding responsiveness, impaired swimming, and increased stationary periods ( Fig. S3D ). Survival tracking until 24.5 or 27 months revealed sex- and genotype-dependent differences ( Fig. 1N ): survival was lowest in homozygous females (11.7%) and males (20.7%), while heterozygous males showed substantially higher survival (62.5%). These results demonstrate that zebrafish carrying the ACTC1 p.T126I variant exhibit heterogeneity in disease onset, severity and survival, with female mutants displaying the poorest outcomes. The nature and progression of the mutant phenotype suggests underlying cardiac functional deficits. Cardiac dysfunction in aged and young adult acta1b hsc230 mutants Given the variation in mutant phenotypes and greater mortality observed in female mutants, we wanted to determine if cardiac function is compromised in the acta1b hsc230 mutants. High-frequency echocardiography of 24.5- and 27-month-old adults revealed pronounced pericardial edema and altered cardiac morphology in mutants compared to wild-type controls ( Fig. 2A–C ). Most mutant groups, except heterozygous males, exhibited significantly lower heart rates and a subset (22−37.5%) of individuals showed reduced ejection fraction, consistent with impaired systolic function ( Fig. 2D,E ) ( Wang et al., 2017 ). Both 24.5- and 27-month-old heterozygous and homozygous acta1b hsc230 mutants exhibited significantly reduced ratios of early (E) and atrial (A) peak ventricular inflow velocities (E:A ratio) and prolonged early ventricular filling phases, indicative of diastolic dysfunction ( Fig. 2G,H ) ( Lee et al., 2016 ). Collectively, these parameters indicate combined systolic and diastolic impairment reminiscent of dilated cardiomyopathy. To determine whether the pronounced cardiac abnormalities in older adult T126I mutants were preceded by subtler cardiac changes, we performed echocardiography on homozygous acta1b hsc230 zebrafish aged 4, 6 and 12−15 months. Pre-symptomatic, sex-specific functional abnormalities were apparent at 4 months, with reduced E:A ratio in females, and lower heart rates and prolonged filling time in both sexes ( Fig. 3A,B , Fig. S4A ). By 6 months, most measures were comparable to wild type, except for persistently low E:A ratios in females ( Fig. 3A,B , Fig. S4A ). At 12−15 months, when a subset (25−33%) of mutant fish became symptomatic, their heart rates were comparable to those of wild-type fish ( Fig. 3A ). Mean E:A ratio and E wave duration were similar between wild-type and mutant fish of the same sex; however, there was substantial variability in both E:A ratios and E wave durations ( Fig. 3B , Fig. S4A ). End-systolic and end-diastolic volumes in both pre-symptomatic and symptomatic male mutants and symptomatic female mutants showed a coordinated increase in chamber volume ( Fig. 3C,D ), allowing ejection fraction to be maintained at this stage ( Fig. 3E ). Female acta1b hsc230 zebrafish with severe edema at this stage ( Fig. S2L,M ) exhibited larger ventricular dimensions than female wild-type and female pre-symptomatic acta1b hsc230 fish during both systole and diastole in echocardiographic assessments ( Fig. 3F-I ), consistent with chamber enlargement as part of disease progression. Heart weight and heart size were both increased in homozygous mutants and, although to a lesser extent, in heterozygotes fish at 24−27 months ( Fig. 4G,H ). Sex-specific transcriptional alterations in the heart of acta1b hsc230 mutants To evaluate whether cardiac phenotypes observed in acta1b hsc230 zebrafish mutants correspond to dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM) at the molecular level, we measured the mRNA expression of established DCM marker genes natriuretic peptide A ( nppa) , natriuretic peptide B ( nppb ), GATA-binding protein 4 ( gata4 ) and myocyte enhancer factor 2ca ( mef2ca ). At 6 months, the nppb , encoding a canonical marker of cardiac stress and remodeling ( Hirata et al., 2001 ; Shih et al., 2015 ), was significantly upregulated in homozygous mutants, whereas nppa was significantly downregulated ( Fig. 6A ). By contrast, mRNA levels of the key cardiac transcription factors gata4 and mef2ca , which mediate hypertrophic responses ( Akazawa and Komuro, 2003 ; Kolodziejczyk et al., 1999 ; Morimoto et al., 2008 ), were downregulated, consistent with impaired adaptive signaling typical of DCM. Sarcomere genes amhc , vmhc , vmhcl , tnni1b , tnnt2a and tpm4a showed stable or reduced expression ( Fig. 6B ), which is in contrast to their upregulation during hypertrophic growth and further supports a dilated remodeling profile. By 12−15 months, expression of nppa and nppb was significantly downregulated in male mutants, whereas expression in female mutants returned to levels seen for wild-type fish ( Fig. 6A ). Expression of gata4 remained consistently downregulated and that of mef2ca was no longer detected at this later timepoint ( Fig. 6A ). Among the sarcomere genes, amhc showed further downregulation at 12−15 months in male acta1b hsc230 mutants, vmhc expression remained stable across timepoints in mutant males but increased in females, and vmhcl was markedly upregulated in males only ( Fig. 6B ). The thin filament genes tnnt2a, tnni1b and tpm4a demonstrated either stable expression across timepoints or a modest increase back to their respective wild-type levels ( Fig. 6B ). Fig. 6. Given the potential for genetic compensation among zebrafish actin paralogues ( Sztal et al., 2018 ), we assessed the expression of all six paralogues. At 6 months, we found that female mutants showed increased expression of acta1b , while males exhibited a decrease ( Fig. 6C ). At the same timepoint, Acta1a was significantly upregulated in all mutants, suggesting a possible compensatory response ( Fig. 6D ). Other actin paralogues, i.e. actc1a and actc2 , showed reduced expression while actc1b showed sex-dependent variability ( Fig. 6E ) at 6 months. By 12−15 months, acta1b expression was significantly downregulated in both sexes, and acta1a expression was also reduced below wild-type levels ( Fig. 6C,D ). Expression of actc1a remained stable, whereas that of actc2 showed modest upregulation in acta1b hsc230 males, and actc1b expression was no longer detected in either sex at this later timepoint ( Fig. 6E ), indicating a loss of actin paralogue compensation as the disease progresses ( Sztal et al., 2018 ). Since our previous in vitro data has indicated that the actin T126I mutation reduces Ca 2+ sensitivity ( Teng and Dawson, 2020 ), we now investigated the expression of genes in the Ca 2+ -handling pathway. At 6 months, zebrafish of both sexes exhibited downregulation of the Na + −Ca 2+ exchanger solute carrier family 8 member A1 ( slc8a1a , previously known as NCX1 ) and the SERCA activity regulator phospholamban ( pln1 ) ( Fig. 6F ) ( Khananshvili, 2013 ; Koss and Kranias, 1996 ). Ryanodine receptor 2 ( ryr2b ) mRNA expression was elevated in both sexes, potentially increasing Ca 2+ release from the sarcoplasmic reticulum ( Kushnir and Marks, 2010 ). However, expression of ATPase sarcoplasmic/endoplasmic reticulum Ca 2+ transporting 2a (atp2a2a; also known as and, hereafter, referred to as serca2 ) was differentially regulated between sexes, i.e. decreased in females but increased in males ( Fig. 6F ), suggesting sex-specific compensatory mechanisms in Ca 2+ homeostasis in response to desensitized thin filaments. By 12−15 months, slc8a1a expression remained consistently downregulated, pln1 was further downregulated in both sexes, ryr2b expression returned to its relative wild-type levels and serca2 expression remained elevated in mutant males but close to wild-type levels in mutant females ( Fig. 6F ), indicating persistent sex-specific differences in Ca 2+ -handling adaptions. Finally, considering that Acta1b p.T126I affects the function of thin and thick filaments of the sarcomere, we wanted to determine if any genes from the stress-response pathway were dysregulated. At 6 months, mRNA expression of heat shock transcription factor 1 ( hsf1 ) a master regulator of heat shock proteins ( Zhou et al., 2017 ; McLendon and Robbins, 2015 ), was downregulated in both sexes ( Fig. 6G ). However, the mRNA expression of BAG cochaperone ( bag3 ) essential for sarcomere maintenance and autophagy ( Martin et al., 2021 ; Arndt et al., 2010 ), was slightly elevated in females but decreased in males ( Fig. 6G ), suggesting early sex-dependent differences in proteostasis and sarcomere quality control. By 12−15 months, hsf1 expression for both male and female mutants remained below the expression for wild-type fish, and expression of bag3 deccreased in both sexes below the levels seen in wild-type counterparts ( Fig. 6G ), thereby indicating progressive disruption of the cardioprotective stress response ( Norton et al., 2011 ; Su et al., 2016 ). DISCUSSION We have established a zebrafish model carrying the orthologous ACTC1 p.T126I variant linked to familial dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM) in humans, providing new in vivo insights into sarcomeric DCM pathogenesis and progression. Through comprehensive longitudinal characterization of both male and female zebrafish, we demonstrated that this thin filament mutation induces progressive cardiac dysfunction of variable onset with notable sexual dimorphism in disease severity, survival and remodeling. The acta1b hsc230 mutants developed overt cardiac symptoms, such as pericardial effusion, severe body edema and exhaustion. A significant number of mutants developed reduced pupil size in the left eye, potentially due to fewer blood vessel branches in the left eye ( Alvarez et al., 2007 ) and compromised blood flow in our aging acta1b hsc230 mutants. Marked sexual dimorphism emerged, with female mutants exhibiting more-severe disease progression, reduced survival and sustained early diastolic dysfunction compared to males, who showed partial functional recovery at younger adult stages ( Figs 1 – 3 , Figs S2, S3 ). This contrasts with epidemiological data indicating male predominance for DCM in humans, yet aligns with emerging evidence that sex differentially influences molecular remodeling and disease trajectory ( Hershberger and Jordan, 2024 ; Yu et al., 2018 ). Additionally, in zebrafish aged 12−15 months we observed a divergence of phenotypes, when homozygous mutant fish separated into a functionally compensated group with relatively preserved or elevated cardiac function, and a decompensated group with overt cardiac dilation and dysfunction ( Fig. 3 , Figs S2-S4 ). This heterogeneity is consistent with incomplete penetrance and variable expressivity ( Franaszczyk et al., 2017 ; Keseroglu et al., 2023 ) and suggests that the rate of disease progression from a compensated state through remodeling and heart failure is different among individuals. Our findings highlight the importance of investigating sex-specific pathogenesis to better understand how divergent compensatory programs direct disease progression and therapeutic response in sarcomeric cardiomyopathies. Morphological analyses showed that acta1b hsc230 mutant hearts are mostly normal in early adulthood but develop progressive remodeling characterized by atrial enlargement, ventricular distortion and trabecular disorganization ( Fig. 4 ). Histological and immunofluorescence data indicate focal cardiomyocyte atrophy marked by myofibrillar loss at all adult stages examined ( Fig. 5 ). The extent of cardiac atrophy and number of affected individuals increased significantly over time ( Yu et al., 2018 ), and is likely to reflect maladaptive or insufficient repair contributing to cardiac dysfunction and heart failure in the aging actin mutants. Transcriptional profiling supports these divergent covert and overt phenotypes. Upregulation of nppb at 6 months confirms cardiac stress and remodeling ( Fig. 6 ) ( Hirata et al., 2001 ; Shih et al., 2015 ). At 6 and 12−15 months, mRNA expression of hypertrophy-associated transcription factors gata4 and mef2ca (no expression at 12−15 months) was downregulated, and stable or reduced expression of sarcomeric genes distinguish the observed molecular phenotype from hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, consistent with a DCM remodeling program. The exception to this trend is the sex-specific differential regulation of vmhc and vmhcl at 12−15 months, with expression of vmhc increasing only in females only and expression of vmhcl increasing only in males ( Fig. 6B ). This sex-specific difference suggests that male and female hearts adopt distinct ventricular myosin mechanisms to compensate as the disease progresses. The differential regulation of actin paralogue expression ( Fig. 6C-E ) suggests partial genetic compensation at early stages of the disease with potential sex-dependent modulation that influences the severity of compensation. It is unclear why female mutants fish would increase expression of acta1b hsc230 in the heart while mutant males decrease expression of the mutant paralogue. However, this paralogue compensation response appears to weaken by 12−15 months, consistent with progression towards decompensation. Notably, altered expression of Ca 2+ -handling genes, especially sex-specific changes in serca2 , upregulation of ryr2b , and downregulation of pln1 and slc8a1a , suggests disrupted Ca 2+ -homeostasis as a key contributor to contractile dysfunction ( Fig. 6F ). This manipulation of Ca 2+ pathway genes suggests attempts to increase cytosolic Ca 2+ concentration, aligning with our prior demonstration that Acta1b p.T126I reduces thin filament Ca 2+ sensitivity. Resulting Ca 2+ overload in cardiomyocytes may impair ventricular relaxation and, thus, underlie the diastolic dysfunction seen in mutant animals. These disturbances are likely to trigger adaptive and maladaptive signaling cascades. Furthermore, dysregulation of proteostasis pathways, including reduced hsf1 and sex-dependent changes in bag3 expression ( Fig. 6G ), implicate impaired sarcomere maintenance and autophagic quality control in progressive dysfunction. Mechanistically, the T126I variant impairs sarcomeric contractility primarily through reduction of Ca 2+ sensitivity in thin filaments, leading to compromised force generation. This insufficiency may elicit compensatory increases in cytosolic Ca 2+ to maintain contraction but chronic Ca 2+ overload can disrupt cardiomyocyte electrical and mechanical properties, induce mitochondrial damage, and activate maladaptive signaling ( Wang et al., 2020 ; Vassalle and Lin, 2004 ; Santulli et al., 2015 ). These processes promote progressive myocardial remodeling, sarcomere disarray and, ultimately, heart failure − as observed in our acta1b hsc230 mutants. Taken together, our findings identified crucial molecular and functional pathways driving sarcomeric DCM with sex-specific modulation alongside the acta1b hsc230 mutation. Therefore, our zebrafish model provides a valuable platform for dissecting early and late disease stages, elucidating sex as an important modifier and testing targeted interventions to mitigate progressive heart failure in sarcomeric cardiomyopathies. MATERIALS AND METHODS Generation of a zebrafish model of human ACTC1-dilated cardiomyopathy Zebrafish ( danio rerio ) have six cardiac actin paralogues that are expressed either constitutively or at specific developmental stages within the heart. There are two closely related actin genes, actc1a and acta1b , both of which are predominantly expressed in cardiac tissue (notice: in zebrafish actc1a is duplicated on chromosome 19 and 20; actc1a on chromsome 20 has previously been referred to as actc1c ). The actc1a gene duplicates on chromosome 19 and 20 are 100% identical at both the gene and protein sequence levels and share 99.47% sequence identity with human ACTC1 ( Fig. S1 ). In comparison, acta1b is a single-copy gene and Acta1b protein exhibits 98.94% sequence identity with ACTC1 ( Fig. S1 ) ( Robert and Gouet, 2014 ). Due to the redundancy of actc1a , which may complicate functional analyses through genetic compensation and would require simultaneous targeting of both loci, acta1b represents a more tractable and practical model for introducing and studying human ACTC1 mutations in zebrafish ( Prill et al., 2023 preprint). The zebrafish line acta1b hsc230 that carries the human c.383C&gt;T (p.T126I) mutation in acta1b was generated by utilizing CRISPR/Cas9 technology with plasmid-donor DNA in the AB strain background and validated by the Zebrafish Genetics and Disease Models Core, SickKids Research Institute ( Fig. S1 ). Founders positive for the knock-in mutation were outcrossed to wild-type AB fish to generate an F 1 generation, which was provided for our study. Zebrafish husbandry, line maintenance and ethics All protocols were carried out following the guidelines stated by the Canadian Council for Animal Care and the University of Guelph (AUP#5144). The acta1b hsc230 mutant strains were maintained as homozygotes and heterozygotes, along with the homozygous Tupfel Long Fin/AB strain (TLAB) wild-type strain, in the Hagen Aqualab at the University of Guelph. All zebrafish were housed in a recirculating aquatic system at 28.5°C, under a 12:12 h light:dark cycle, in accordance with established zebrafish husbandry recommendations ( Westerfield, 2007 ). Embryos were obtained by crossing heterozygous adults; offspring were raised to adulthood for line maintenance and experimental analysis; this study examined adults from the F 4 −F 6 generations. Age-matched homozygous acta1b hsc230 and heterozygous acta1b hsc230 /+ mutants were followed longitudinally and assessed at 24−27 months for phenotypes, mortality, heart function and health. For analysis at younger stages, 6- and 12–15-month stages, acta1b hsc230 mutants were compared to TLAB wild-type controls. If an animal showed signs of stress, suspected pain or their quality of life had been significantly impacted by the progression of the disease, they were euthanized by submersion in an ice slurry for 45 min until gill movement ceased and they did not respond to touch. In most cases, once animals became symptomatic, they survived only briefly and were either euthanized or died shortly thereafter due to declining welfare. Genotyping acta1b hsc230 mutants Acta1b exons were amplified from genomic DNA extracted from homozygous and heterozygous acta1b hsc230 larvae and adult fin clips using acta1b 5′-GGGTATCCTCACTCTGAAATA-3′ (forward) and 5′-CGGTTGTGACGAAAGAATAA-3′ (reverse) primers. PCR products were purified and submitted for Sanger sequencing through the University of Guelph Agriculture and Food Laboratory Service. Sequencing chromatograms were analyzed using 4Peaks (v 1.8) software to distinguish wild-type, heterozygous, and homozygous acta1b hsc230 genotypes. Frailty assessment of adult zebrafish Fish were netted and allowed to acclimatize in a 1.1-l tank with a divider to ensure primarily lateral movement. Tests were recorded using an iPhone XS camera (Apple Inc, Cupertino, CA, USA) positioned 1.5 feet from the tank on the same table. Two consecutive minutes of swimming was used to assess the speed [i.e. whether fish could swim a distance of 91.44 cm (20 inches) in 15 seconds] and casual endurance of individual fish. This was followed by stimulation, and 1-min recovery repeated three times in total. Finally, fish food pellets (Gemma 300; Skretting, Vancouver, BC, Canada) were added to the front of the tank when the fish was at the back. Response to food was timed based on three tiers, i.e. at 15 s, 30 s and 1 min. Additional test categories were normal anatomical appearance, swimming horizontal and level, and continuous swimming. All assessments were based on a binary system, in which a positive response scored one point and absent or negative responses scored zero points. 6-month-old wild-type and acta1b hsc230 fish were excluded from the frailty assessment due to signs of stress not observed in the 24-27-month-old cohort. Echocardiography and analysis Adult zebrafish were anesthetized by submersion in facility water containing a combination of 0.15 g/l MS-222 (tricaine; TCI America, Portland, OR, USA), 0.3 g/l NaHCO 3 (Fisher Scientific, Mississauga, ON, Canada) and 7.5 ml/l isoflurane (TCI America) ( Lee et al., 2016 ). Fish were monitored for gill movement, alertness and stress while sedated and were not anesthetized for more than 3 min. Fish were returned alone to fresh facility water in a small breeding tank to recover and monitored for hemorrhaging, rapid gill movement, arched tail (sign of stress) or gasping behavior. After 20 min, fish were regrouped and returned to their home tank within the facility. Cardiac function in adult zebrafish was acquired and assessed at 4, 6, 12−15 and 24−27 months of age using a high-frequency ultrasound imaging system (Vevo 3100 LT; FUJIFILM Visual Sonics, Toronto, ON, Canada) as previously described ( Shaftoe et al., 2023 ). Ultrasound images and measurement of cardiac parameters were performed using Vevo 3100 imaging software as previously described ( Shaftoe et al., 2023 ). Averages of groups by genotype were done due to limited tank space to house all fish individually during assessments. Heart dissection and physiological assessment Adult zebrafish were euthanized by submersion in an ice slurry for 45 min until cessation of gill movement and lack of response to stimuli. Fish aged 6 and 12−15 month were freshly dissected after euthanasia, while 19−27-month-old fish were fixed in formalin (Fisher Scientific, Canada) at 4°C. Before dissection of the thoracic cavity, the length (tip of mouth to caudal peduncle) and weights of all adult zebrafish were recorded. Lateral and ventral images were taken of individuals using the iPhone XS camera (Apple Inc; USA) freehand and mounted to a Zeiss SteREO Discovery V8 microscope (Zeiss; Oberkochen, Germany). A sagittal cut followed by two transverse cuts to form a window were made on the ventral surface of the thoracic region to allow visualization of the heart before removal ( Singleman and Holtzman, 2011 ). Distal regions of the bulbus arteriosus and sinus venosus were cut to release the heart. The hearts were imaged, measured and weighed before proceeding to downstream experimental analysis. Cryosectioning, histology and immunofluorescence Hearts designated for histological analysis were washed 3× in phosphate buffered saline (PBS) for 10 min, washed in a series of sucrose baths, and washed again in tissue freezing medium Cryomatrix (Epredia, Kalamazoo, MI, USA) ( Uribe and Gross, 2007 ). Hearts were sectioned at 12 µm thickness on a cryostat (Leica CM3050 S; Leica, Concord, ON, Canada) and baked onto Superfrost Plus slides (Fisher Scientific, Canada) for 2 h at room temperature. Heart sections were sent to the University of Guelph Animal Health Laboratory Histology Suite for hematoxylin and eosin staining. Stained heart sections were imaged using an iPhone X camera (Apple Inc) mounted to an upright Leica DM 5000B for whole-heart imaging. High magnification (40×) images of sectioned hearts were captured using the Leica DM 5000B and Volocity imaging software (Quorum Technologies, Puslinch, ON, Canada). For antibody staining of sectioned tissue, cryosections were hydrated for 3 min in PBTD (PBS, 0.1% Tween 20, 1% DMSO) in square Petri dishes. Antibody staining was performed as previously described ( Prill et al., 2015 ) with rhodamine:phalloidin (1:500 dilution; PHDR1-A; Cytoskeleton, Denver, CO, USA). Stained tissue was imaged using a Diskovery Spinning Disk confocal microscope (Quorum, Canada) and images were processed using Volocity imaging software (Quorum, Canada). RNA extraction and quantitative PCR Freshly dissected hearts obtained from fish aged 6 months and 12−15 months were flash frozen in liquid nitrogen before RNA extraction. RNA was extracted from flash-frozen hearts, using the TRIzol-chloroform method as previously described ( Prill et al., 2015 ). mRNA was normalized to 100 ng/µl prior to cDNA synthesis (5X All-In-One RT PCR Mastermix; Bio Basic, Markham ON, Canada). All quantitative PCR (qPCR) was performed using GB-Amp InFluor Green qPCR Mix (GeneBio Systems Inc, Burlington, ON, Canada), primers ( Table S1 ) and Applied Biosystems QuantStudio 7 Pro Real-time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The relative fold-change in expression of each gene was determined using the ΔΔCt method ( Livak and Schmittgen, 2001 ). Ct values were first normalized to the reference gene ( rpl13a ), then compared to the same-sex wild-type control group, which was set to a value of 1. Statistical analysis Physiological, frailty scores and ultrasound results were compared using two-way ANOVA with Tukey's post hoc test for multiple comparisons. Distribution of gene expression data was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. For two-group (mutant versus wild-type) comparisons, Welch's unpaired t -test was used as the primary statistical analysis. If a group failed normality, Mann–Whitney U test was performed to confirm the conclusion was not dependent on the parametric assumption.
