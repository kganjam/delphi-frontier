1. Introduction Vitamin E encompasses a group of four tocopherols and four tocotrienols [ 1 , 2 ]. The most abundant form in animals is α ‐tocopherol (aTOH), which is known to effectively protect cell membranes and other lipids from lipid peroxidation by acting as a fat‐soluble chain‐breaking antioxidant. The vitamin E requirement has been previously established in various fish species, including Atlantic salmon ( Salmo salar ), ranging from 30 to 60 mg/kg in first‐feeding fry [ 3 ] and 120 mg/kg in fry [ 4 ], as reflected in the NRC recommendation [ 5 ]. More recently, the practical aTOH requirements were suggested to be higher in parr and post‐smolt Atlantic salmon, ranging between 150 and 310 mg/kg [ 6 ]. The vitamin E requirement in Atlantic salmon during smoltification remains unknown. Smoltification is considered to be a highly energy‐demanding period in the life cycle that prepares for the fish’s migration to the ocean by increasing their salinity tolerance [ 7 – 9 ]. Atlantic salmon smolt were shown to accumulate aTOH in the liver tissue, while the levels in other organs such as the kidney and viscera decrease [ 10 ]. This mobilization of vitamin E during smoltification, especially in the kidney, might be linked to osmoregulatory adaptation but likely also reflects a change in demand due to an increased metabolic rate. Smoltification is largely hormone‐driven by insulin‐like growth factor I, cortisol, and growth hormone [ 7 , 11 ]. Atlantic salmon injected with growth hormone was shown to experience oxidative stress, accompanied by a decrease of liver antioxidant levels, including vitamin E [ 12 ]. This suggests that even a marginal deficient vitamin E status during smoltification could contribute to an imbalance in the cellular antioxidant system, posing a substantial threat to the robustness of fish during seawater transfer, where the fish is exposed to a variety of environmental stressors threatening the long‐term welfare [ 13 ]. Previous studies have indicated that the estimated vitamin E requirements vary depending on the selected response criterion [ 14 ]. Traditional biomarkers used to establish vitamin E requirements in fish include growth performance, tissue homeostasis, blood parameters, and oxidative stress markers [ 3 , 15 – 18 ]. However, the dietary levels necessary to achieve optimal growth seem to be lower than those indicated by immune biomarkers [ 19 , 20 ]. Further, it has been suggested that the vitamin E requirement in Atlantic salmon may increase under stressful environmental conditions [ 21 ]. Therefore, a more comprehensive understanding of the vitamin E levels needed to achieve optimal fish welfare is essential, especially during critical life stages such as smoltification, when fish experience significant metabolic and environmental changes. A reevaluation of the vitamin E requirement in Atlantic salmon might also be warranted in view of changing diet formulations [ 22 ]. Specifically, dietary antioxidants work synergistically to protect cells from oxidative damage, and in the past, the vitamin E requirement in Atlantic salmon at the fry stage was obtained by manipulating aTOH levels as a single ingredient [ 3 , 4 ]. In contrast, the much higher aTOH requirements in Atlantic salmon at the parr and post‐smolt stage were estimated from nutrient package design experiments involving the simultaneous manipulation of various micronutrient levels in the feed [ 6 ]. Vitamin E is well‐known to interact with both vitamin C and selenium [ 23 – 25 ]. Thereby, vitamin C was shown to protect against vitamin E deficiency in a dose‐dependent manner, possibly related to synergistic mechanisms in the protection against tissue oxidation. However, vitamin C also plays a role in vitamin E homeostasis by regenerating vitamin E radicals [ 26 ]. In addition, supplemental selenium was shown to increase hepatic tocopherol activity in fish, while a double deficiency quickly results in detrimental symptoms including anemia, muscular dystrophy, and increased mortality [ 23 ]. Due to changes in legislation, the use of synthetic antioxidants is decreasing in commercial feeds, driving a high inclusion of aTOH in both feeds and feed ingredients as a natural alternative, yielding diet formulations possibly containing excess vitamin E [ 27 ]. Therefore, this study aimed to establish practical dietary vitamin E requirements in Atlantic salmon during smoltification using feed formulated with ingredients common in commercial diets today. To evaluate vitamin E requirements under challenging conditions, the fish were additionally exposed to mild hypoxia and elevated temperature immediately after seawater transfer. Together, these factors were applied to simulate environmental stress as expected during transfer to the sea. Following, the long‐term effect of feeding graded levels of dietary vitamin E during smoltification was monitored for an additional 5 months in a sea cage while fed a common commercial diet. 2. Material and Methods 2.1. Ethical Statement The experiment was performed according to the Norwegian laws and regulations concerning experiments with animals for research purposes. Norway has fully implemented EU Directive 2010/63/EU in regional law. Prior to the beginning of the trial, authorization was obtained from the Norwegian Food Safety Authority (Mattilsynet, ID 30333) for the planned activities. 2.2. Experimental Diets The basal feed mix was formulated with ingredients used in modern commercial Atlantic salmon smolt feeds (Table 1 ). The 3 mm extruded pellets were produced by Skretting Aquaculture Innovation’s pilot feed plant (Stavanger, Norway). The protein and lipid contents of the diets were 44% and 20% according to proximate composition analysis (Section 2.7 for description of analytical procedure). The level of vegetable oil with 4% was below that used in commercial smolt feed today but was chosen to limit the introduction of tocopherols and tocotrienols from raw materials. Fish Husbandry and Feeding During Tank Rearing The feeding trial was conducted at Matre Research Station, the Norwegian Institute of Marine Research’s experimental fish facility. Sixteen 360 L (0.95 × 0.95 m, 0.4 m water depth) tanks in a flow‐through system were used in this trial, corresponding to duplicate tanks per treatment, except for VE38 and VE710, which were run in triplicate to reduce uncertainty in the regression models (Figure 1 ). Each tank was stocked with 110 Atlantic salmon at an initial body weight of 81 ± 2 g. Prior to the beginning of the trail, the fish were acclimatized to the environmental conditions for 2 weeks. The fish were fed ad libitum over the whole duration of the trial, while uneaten feed was collected during rearing in the flow‐through system at the freshwater and seawater phases to estimate feed intake. Smoltification was initiated after 6 weeks by changing the light regime from a 12 h light: 12 h darkness cycle to continuous light. Figure 1 Experimental design. Fish were sampled under freshwater conditions (12°C, dissolved oxygen: &gt;80%) after 11 weeks of feeding as freshwater smolt (FW‐smolt). During week 12, the salinity level in the tanks was gradually increased to 22 ppt to create saltwater conditions for the remaining fish (Figure 1 ). Simultaneously, challenging environmental conditions were simulated by increasing the water temperature to 14°C and lowering the dissolved oxygen levels in all experimental tanks to 60%, creating mild hypoxia during seawater rearing. In week 13, a total of 15 fish were removed from each tank, respectively, to correct for optimal stocking density. The sampling of the seawater smolt (SW‐smolt) in tanks was done in trial week 18, as described in Section 2.5 . 2.4. Sea Cage Rearing To establish the long‐term effects of feeding graded levels of aTOH in Atlantic salmon during smoltification, 22 of the remaining SW‐smolt from each tank were pooled in a single 5 × 5 m sea cage (Figure 1 ). The fish transferred to the sea cage were randomly selected at the beginning of the feeding trial and had undergone additional treatment of pit‐tagging (week 2) and vaccination (week 6). The fish in the sea cage were fed a commercial diet for 5 months prior to sampling as post‐smolt. 2.5. Sampling Procedure At the FW‐smolt and SW‐smolt samplings, 12 fish per tank were euthanized with an overdose of tricaine methanesulphonate (MS‐222, PharmaQ, Bergen, Norway), respectively. The fish were individually measured for weight and length, scored for external welfare parameters, and opened for nephrocalcinosis (NC) scoring (Section 2.6 ). Eight fish per tank were kept for whole‐body analysis. Radiographs were taken of FW‐smolt collected for whole‐body analysis as described in Section 2.6 . Individual blood samples were collected using heparinized syringes from four fish per tank for hematocrit and hemoglobin analyses. After blood sampling, the fish were dissected for heart, liver, and muscle tissue. The weight of viscera, liver, and heart was recorded to calculate the somatic index of organ weights (SI = organ weight/fish body weight × 100). The liver and muscle samples of individual fish were divided into subsamples according to the planned analysis of malondialdehyde (MDA) and GSH/GSSG. In addition, pooled liver samples of the four fish were created for the determination of vitamin E, vitamin C, and selenium concentration, respectively. The tissue samples were flash frozen in liquid nitrogen at sampling and subsequently stored at −80°C until further analysis. At post‐smolt sampling, a minimum of 40 fish per dietary treatment were measured for total weight and body length. Following, the fish were evaluated for external standard welfare indicators, and radiographs were taken from all available fish to evaluate vertebral deformities. Additionally, fish from the dietary groups VE38, VE260, and VE710 ( n = 12–14 per diet) were dissected to weigh the viscera, liver, heart, and gonads, and the sex of these fish was noted down. 2.6. Welfare Scoring The welfare scoring of fish for first impression, scale loss, damage to gill operculum, and fin status was done according to the LAKSVEL protocol [ 29 ]. NC was scored following Hjelde [ 30 ] using a macroscopic scoring system. Cataract was scored in both lenses under darkened conditions using a Heine slit lamp (HEINE HSL 150 hand‐held slit lamp, HEINE Optotechnik). Indicating the degree of opacification, each lens was then given a score of 0–4, resulting in a total score of 0–8 per fish [ 31 ]. In post‐smolts, cataracts could only be scored macroscopically using the LAKSVEL protocol because no slit lamp was available. Whole fish were radiographed with a Direct Radiology System (Canon CXDI410C Wireless, CANON INC., Kawasaki, Japan) using a portable X‐ray unit (Portable X‐ray Unit Hiray Plus, Model Porta 100 HF, JOB Corporation, Yokohama, Japan) at an 88 cm distance with 40 kV and 4 mAs. Each fish was evaluated for different types of vertebral deformities as described by Witten et al. [ 32 ], and if present, the type and location were recorded. Atlantic salmon with more than 10 deformed vertebrae may have reduced growth, which is a strong indicator of reduced welfare [ 33 ]. 2.7. Nutrient Analysis The feed samples and whole fish were homogenized and analyzed for proximate composition. The dry matter was determined after 24 h in the oven at 105°C. Ash content was measured after combustion in the muffle oven for 16 h at 550°C. Ethyl acetate and acid extraction were used to determine lipid content in feed and fish, respectively. Protein was measured with a nitrogen analyzer as N ∗ 6.25 according to AOAC official methods. Selenium was analyzed in the feed by ICP‐MS. Tocopherols and vitamin C levels were analyzed in feed and tissue by HPLC, as described in [ 34 , 35 ]. 2.8. Biochemical Tissue Analysis and Measurement of Blood Markers MDA in the liver was analyzed as described in [ 36 ]. Reduced and oxidized glutathione were measured spectrophotometrically using the Cuvette Assay kit for GSH/GSSG (Oxford Biomedical Research, US, Product Number GT35) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For hematocrit measurement, whole blood was immediately centrifuged in a Hettrich centrifuge using microhematocrit tubes (Am‐Heparinized, 80 iu/mL, REF162613, Vitrex Medical, Denmark). A CELL‐DYN hematology analyzer (Abbot Laboratories, Illinois, USA) calibrated with Para 12 Control blood (Streck, La Vista, NE, USA) was used to measure hemoglobin according to the manufacturer’s instructions. 2.9. Statistical Analysis All statistical analyses were done using statistical software R (R Core Team 2023). All data was plotted to evaluate the distribution of the response variable in relation to the dietary vitamin E level. The data are presented as the mean and pooled standard deviation across groups calculated using the following formula with sd k representing the standard deviation of a treatment and n k representing the sample size of a treatment: SDpooled = n 1 − 1 sd 1 2 + n 2 − 1 sd 2 2 + … + n k − 1 sd k 2 / n 1 + n 2 + … + n k − k . Further, linear regression, polynomial, and broken‐line models were fitted to the data. Model selection was done using the AIC and R ‐square criteria to find the best model fit. All model selection parameters are detailed in Tables S1 – S3 . Broken‐line models were tested for one or more breakpoints, and their 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were determined using the functions provided by the segmented package (Version 2.1‐0). Linear and polynomial models were plotted using the ggpmisc package (Version 0.6.0) as an extension to ggplot2 [ 37 ]. The welfare score data were converted from categorical to binomial prior to the statistical analysis. Following, a logistic regression with a binomial link was used to evaluate the effect of dietary aTOH level on welfare indicators. 3. Results 3.1. Fish Performance Minor fish loss was observed in the experiment, with survival rates above 99% across all dietary treatments. The dietary aTOH level had no significant effect on the body weight or specific growth rate of FW‐smolt (Table 3 ). However, broken‐line regression indicates that the feed intake increased in FW‐smolt‐fed diets up to an aTOH level of 82 mg/kg (CI 63–101). Welfare Indicators With 98%, almost all FW‐smolt had minor fin damage, but only 7% of the fish scored above 1 without a significant effect of the dietary treatment (Table 4 ). In addition, 47% of the FW‐smolt showed scale loss without any significant difference between the dietary groups. On average, 86% of the FW‐smolt had cataracts, with 32% showing severe cataract scores above four, however, there were no significant differences between groups. The prevalence of NC was not significantly affected by the dietary aTOH level and was at 11% across all FW‐smolt. The prevalence of vertebral deformities in FW‐smolts ranged from 6.25% to 25% across dietary groups, with no significant influence of diet on the occurrence of various types of deformities (Table 5 ). Major welfare issues in SW‐smolt included, similar to FW‐smolt, minor fin damage (98% of the fish) and scale loss (21% of the fish) without any significant difference between dietary treatments (Table 4 ). In SW‐smolt, the prevalence of cataract increased in all dietary groups compared to FW and was highest in fish‐fed VE38 and VE710 and lower at intermediate levels. A similar pattern was observed for severe cataract, which was highest in the group fed the lowest aTOH levels and less prevalent in the groups supplemented with medium levels of aTOH. No long‐term effect of nutritional history could be detected on the standard welfare indicators in post‐smolt (Table 4 ). In all groups, the occurrence of vertebra deformities ranged 10%–25% of the fish. Most deformities could be considered minor, with less than 10 vertebrae affected. The deformity type 7 “complete vertebral fusion” (Figure 2 ) was higher in post‐smolt fed VE38 during smoltification compared to those fed higher levels of aTOH. Discussion 4.1. No Dose–Response Effect of Dietary aTOH on Fish Growth During Smoltification The dietary aTOH levels in the present study, ranging from 38 to 710 mg/kg were able to support normal growth in Atlantic salmon during both the freshwater and seawater phases of smoltification without any observable impact of aTOH levels. This agrees with previous studies in meager ( Argyrosomus regius ), red drum ( Sciaenops ocellatus ), and rainbow trout ( Oncorhynchus mykiss ) where body weight of the fish was not affected by dietary vitamin E supplementation in a dose–response manner [ 15 , 16 , 38 ]. However, body weight has been used to establish aTOH requirements in various fish species, including Atlantic salmon, with optimal dietary levels ranging between 28 and 167 mg/kg in hybrid striped bass ( Morone chrysops female × M. saxatilis male), rohu ( Labeo rohita ) fry, mrigal ( Cirrhinus mrigala ) fry, spotted snakehead ( Channa punctatus ), largemouth bass ( Micropterus salmoide ), and 20 mg/kg, specifically for Atlantic salmon at first feeding [ 3 , 17 , 18 , 39 – 41 ]. The inconsistent effects of aTOH supplementation on growth performance may not only result from species‐specific differences but also from variation in experimental diet formulations. Impaired growth may thereby be considered of low specificity with regard to vitamin E but rather as a general consequence of a nutrient deficiency. Indeed, most requirement studies reporting growth effects were conducted using purified or semipurified diets. Additionally, it has been suggested that aTOH deficiency symptoms may be mitigated in the presence of sufficient vitamin C and selenium [ 26 ]. Previous studies investigating vitamin E requirements in fish span a broad range of vitamin C levels ranging from 40 to 5000 mg/kg, while known selenium requirements were typically met. In Atlantic salmon, the vitamin E requirement at first feeding was established using 1188 mg/kg vitamin C [ 3 ]. In this context, the 399 mg/kg vitamin C used in the present study is above requirements in salmon but falls within a moderate range, especially considering that a combined supra‐supplementation of vitamins might also suppress growth during smoltification. In a previous study, Atlantic salmon smolt given 430–466 mg/kg vitamin E in combination with 1570–1590 mg/kg vitamin C showed lower growth when compared to fish fed moderate levels of 180 mg vitamin E/kg diet and 230–252 mg vitamin C/kg diet [ 42 ]. Besides antioxidants, interactions of vitamin E with dietary levels of other lipid‐soluble vitamins, including vitamin K and A, could similarly be assumed from studies in rodents [ 43 , 44 ] but are less well characterized to date in fish. Micronutrient levels, other than vitamin E, did not differ between treatments in the present study, and therefore, the low specificity of growth responses may explain why tissue vitamin E levels and biomarkers of target pathways were more sensitive to the applied dietary range of vitamin E than growth. The dose–response effect of dietary vitamin E on feed intake observed during the freshwater phase of the present study is not commonly reported in fish. For example, research on rainbow trout demonstrated no significant differences in the feed intake of fish [ 45 , 46 ]. Similarly, in largemouth bass, cosupplementation of graded levels of vitamin E (160–400 mg/kg) and selenium did not significantly impact feed intake in diets with oxidized fish oil [ 47 ]. However, a separate study working with lower levels of vitamin E reported reduced feed intake in largemouth bass fed diets containing less than 73 mg/kg [ 39 ]. These findings suggest that the effect of vitamin E on feed intake may be limited to low dietary levels and may not be evident at all life stages. 4.2. High aTOH Accumulation in Liver Tissue at Excess Supplementation The response pattern of vitamin E in the body tissue towards increasing levels of dietary vitamin E was similar to those observed in previous studies. Notably, in the present study, whole‐body aTOH levels showed a linear increase in FW‐smolt up to a dietary concentration of 436 mg/kg, consistent with the work of Hamre et al. [ 6 ], which utilized graded aTOH levels ranging 48–339 mg/kg. In the present study, the FW‐smolt body aTOH levels did not reach saturation, demonstrating an elevated deposition in fish‐fed diets exceeding 436 mg/kg. However, following transfer to seawater, whole‐body aTOH levels appear to be more tightly regulated in Atlantic salmon, with SW‐smolt displaying saturation when fed 350 mg aTOH/kg. Similarly, Hamre et al. [ 6 ] reported a saturation of aTOH in post‐smolts, but at a dietary aTOH level of 140 mg/kg. The discrepancies in saturation levels may reflect differing nutritional requirements arising from variations in experimental conditions, such as the solitary increase of dietary vitamin E in the present study versus the simultaneous increase of multiple antioxidants in the previous study. Additionally, factors such as the life stage and duration of the experiment, which lasted 6 months in the previous trial conducted in sea cages, compared to a sampling period of only 6 weeks post seawater transfer in the present study performed under standardized environmental conditions (mild hypoxia and 14°C temperature), may have also contributed to the observed differences in tissue response. In the present study, the aTOH levels in muscle tissue followed a linear response, as previously shown for Atlantic salmon [ 48 ] as well as rainbow trout [ 49 , 50 ] and tilapia [ 51 ]. The liver aTOH levels followed a 2nd‐degree polynomial increase as previously reported in Atlantic salmon [ 48 ] and Caspian trout [ 52 ]. At dietary levels below 400 mg/kg, the increase in liver tissue can also be considered linear as previously suggested in red drum‐fed dietary aTOH ranging from 0 to 80 mg/kg [ 16 ] and hybrid tilapia fed dietary aTOH ranging from 0 to 201 mg/kg [ 53 ]. In the present study, this corresponds to a break point of 405 mg/kg in FW‐smolt. Given that the liver is the primary organ involved in vitamin E metabolism [ 54 ], the substantial accumulation at dietary aTOH above 405 mg/kg may indicate an inefficiency in its utilization in excess. Thereby, aTOH is suggested to be the preferred form of vitamin E for retention in the fish liver tissue [ 55 ]. Although gTOH can be readily deposited in the muscle tissue of salmon [ 56 ], in the present study, the gTOH in whole‐body FW‐smolt and muscle tissue of SW‐smolt showed a linear decrease, while the liver showed a linear increase, supporting the theory of a complex interplay between tocopherol forms in the liver leading to a discriminatory deposition in other tissues [ 57 , 58 ]. Vitamin E protects lipids from peroxidation but has also been suggested to affect the body’s lipid metabolism [ 59 ]. Although no differences in whole‐body lipid levels were observed in this or previous studies on salmonids [ 3 , 53 ], the dose‐responses on VSI and HSI may point towards tissue‐specific effects. In FW‐smolt, the VSI showed a linear decrease with increasing dietary aTOH levels, while the HSI significantly increased in SW‐smolt‐fed diets above 396 mg/kg. The observed differences between FW‐ and SW‐smolt in the present study might reflect life‐stage‐specific differences with reduced body lipid stores in post‐smolts due to the high energy demand of smoltification. Similarly, in spotted snakehead, the HSI increased when the fish were fed diets up to 140 mg aTOH/kg diet [ 18 ]. Further, a histopathological evaluation of liver tissue in meager found that both low and high dietary vitamin E result in increased lipid vacuolization [ 38 ]. It was therefore suggested that a healthy lipid metabolism in the liver might be a marker for vitamin E requirements, while it may be disturbed at excess vitamin E. Indeed, TBARS levels as a marker for lipid peroxidation generally decrease under moderate aTOH supplementation in fish; however, in sea bream, it was reported to increase at an excess supplementation of 1000 mg/kg [ 60 ]. 4.3. Dietary aTOH Inhibits Liver MDA Levels With Varying Degrees of Oxidative Stress in FW‐ and SW‐Smolt Common markers for lipid peroxidation include the determination of MDA and TBARS. These markers are easily responsive to dietary vitamin E and have been commonly used to establish aTOH requirements in fish. Similarly, MDA appears to be a stringent criterion with a clear breakpoint in response to dietary vitamin E in the present study. Most studies measured TBARS in the liver tissue, giving optimal dietary aTOH inclusion levels ranging from 31 to 140 mg/kg [ 16 , 18 , 41 ]. In rainbow trout muscle, MDA levels were reported to have a breakpoint of 100 mg/kg [ 61 ], which agrees with the breakpoint obtained in SW‐smolt in the present study at 99 mg/kg. However, in FW‐smolt, breakpoint analysis suggests a requirement of 457 mg/kg, which is closer to that obtained in meager at 451 mg/kg using a muscle TBARS assay [ 38 ]. Overall, the breakpoint of 99 mg/kg as observed in SW‐smolt is closer to the aTOH requirement levels previously defined in Atlantic salmon [ 3 ], while the breakpoint of 457 mg/kg is closer to those obtained in the present study through tissue aTOH levels. Notably, the daily weight gain and HSI were reduced in SW‐smolt compared to FW‐smolt. As a product of lipid oxidation, liver lipid levels (not measured in the current study) can have a direct impact on MDA formation [ 62 ], suggesting that the present observations also reflect life‐stage differences between FW‐ and SW‐smolt, with FW‐smolt experiencing more oxidative stress compared to SW‐smolt. Another factor to be considered is the interaction of vitamin E with other antioxidants. In the present study, liver levels of vitamin C and selenium remained unaffected by dietary aTOH, but SW‐smolt exhibited a significant increase in muscle astaxanthin, with levels up to 45% higher in the highest compared to the lowest aTOH treatment. A similar increase of 8%–14% has been observed by Bjerkeng et al. [ 63 ] when using 200 vs 800 mg/kg vitamin E. This suggests that excess vitamin E may exert a sparing effect in the muscle, where astaxanthin is utilized as an antioxidant under oxidative stress [ 64 ]. Consequently, dietary aTOH may preserve better fillet quality under challenging conditions [ 63 , 65 ]. 4.4. Dietary aTOH Supports Blood Parameters in SW‐Smolt A decrease in the blood parameters Hgb and Hct is usually considered a clear indication for vitamin E deficiency, posing the risk for anemia in fish [ 4 ]. In the present study, no effect of dietary aTOH level on Hgb or Hct could be observed in FW‐smolt, possibly because all diets contained sufficient aTOH to prevent clinical deficiency signs. However, the present results indicate that the optimal dietary aTOH levels to support blood Hct and Hgb values in Atlantic salmon SW‐smolt are 367–450 mg/kg. This is significantly higher than the previously reported levels of 19–140 mg/kg, which were associated with blood parameters in Atlantic salmon and other fish species at different life stages [ 4 , 18 , 40 , 41 ]. The differences might be a result of the natural increase in blood parameters during smoltification. The Hct levels in salmon follow a distinct pattern, reaching peak levels of about 40%–45% in fish post seawater transfer [ 66 ]. In the present study, the Hct levels were generally lower, reaching 40% only in groups fed dietary aTOH above 367 mg/kg. This might be a result of the environmental conditions of the trial employing mild hypoxia and increased water temperature for fish post seawater transfer, both known to affect blood Hct levels in fish [ 67 ]. 4.5. Low aTOH Affects Cataract Formation and Vertebra Fusion in Atlantic Salmon Post seawater Transfer Overall, welfare scores in the present study were low across the various life stages, indicating good fish welfare. However, a high prevalence of severe cataract was observed in SW‐smolt, with the highest number of fish with a cataract score above 4 in the group fed the lowest vitamin E diet, indicating that this level was too low to maintain eye health. The etiology of cataract formation is multifactorial (reviewed by Bjerkås et al. [ 68 ]), and the role of vitamin E might be linked to protection from oxidative stress that can disrupt lens proteins and membrane lipids. Previous studies investigating the effect of dietary vitamin E in Atlantic salmon have not found effects on cataract development, however, using dietary concentrations above the lowest vitamin E diet used in the present study and lower rearing temperatures [ 12 , 69 ]. In Waagbø et al. [ 69 ], high liver aTOH concentrations were, however, associated with increased cataract formation, which could be related to the increase in cataract prevalence and scores for the high vitamin E group in the present study, although no pro‐oxidative effects were seen in the liver after SW transfer despite high accumulation of aTOH. Additionally, although the levels of vertebral deformities in this study were low, post‐smolt fish fed the lowest levels of vitamin E during smoltification had a significantly increased risk of developing a complete vertebral fusion (vertebral deformity type 7). In deformity type 7, two (or three) fused vertebrae remodel into an elongated vertebra with a normal amphicoelous structure with two (or three) neural/hemal aches or ribs [ 70 ]. Development of type 7 deformity has been suggested to reflect an active repair mechanism for vertebral deformities in Atlantic salmon [ 71 , 72 ]. This type of deformity may develop in Atlantic salmon pre‐ and post seawater transfer [ 70 ] but was only observed in post‐smolt in the present study. Why low vitamin E should induce complete fusions in Atlantic salmon is at present not clear. Fish were unfortunately not radiographed at the end of the feeding period with different feeds. Thus, it is not possible to evaluate if the observed effect is related to a negative effect of low vitamin E and/or a positive and therapeutic effect of the elevated vitamin E when the fish switched to a commercial feed as post‐smolts. Related to the current observation, it is noteworthy that oxidative stress plays a vital role during intervertebral disc degeneration in humans [ 73 ] and that vertebra fusion may start with changes in the intervertebral tissue in Atlantic salmon [ 74 – 76 ]. Complete fusions have indeed been induced by exposure to hyperthermic conditions during embryogenesis and juvenile rearing in Atlantic salmon [ 77 ]. Further, Atlantic salmon reared at elevated temperatures with developing deformities showed an upregulation of the redox‐sensitive gene matrix metalloproteinase 13 ( mmp-13 ) [ 78 ]. These observations possibly reflect an interplay between bone pathologies induced by oxidative stress and active repair mechanisms. Existing literature in mice indicates that vitamin E plays an essential role in bone remodeling, reporting high bone mass formation under low aTOH levels due to decreased bone resorption by osteoclasts [ 79 ]. Similar studies on the role of vitamin E on skeletal health in fish are lacking. 5. Conclusion The dietary aTOH levels ranging from 38 to 710 mg/kg were able to support normal growth in Atlantic salmon during smoltification. Fish fed 38 mg/kg showed an elevated prevalence for cataract 6 weeks after seawater transfer and for type 7 vertebra deformity 5 months after transfer to sea cages, suggesting that 82 mg/kg is the minimum dietary aTOH level to avoid welfare issues in farmed Atlantic salmon. An increased deposition of aTOH in the liver tissue above 400 mg/kg was observed. Similarly, liver MDA in FW‐smolt and hematocrit and hemoglobin values in SW‐smolt showed breakpoints at around 367–457 mg/kg, suggesting limited benefits from supplementing above this level and a requirement under the trial conditions of mild hypoxia and elevated temperature post seawater transfer. The linear increase in muscle astaxanthin in response to increasing dietary aTOH further suggests that excess vitamin E may have a sparing effect for antioxidants in the muscle and could improve fillet quality under these challenging conditions.
