1 Introduction Regarded as a “hidden organ” and a “second genome”, the gut microbiota regulates host growth and development, metabolism, immune system maturation, and intestinal homeostasis via the gut–brain axis ( 1 , 2 ). Interaction between the gut-brain axis can happen through neural, endocrine, or immune regulatory pathways and contribute to host metabolism, signal transduction, immunity, and the functioning of important organs such as the intestine, liver, and brain ( 3 ). In endocrine regulatory pathways, the gut-brain axis releases various hormones and polypeptide substances to jointly impact host growth and development, hunger, energy maintenance, and circadian rhythms ( 4 , 5 ). These key hormones include the corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH), thyrotropin-releasing hormone (TRH), thyroid stimulating hormone (TSH), serotonin (known as 5-hydroxytryptamine, 5-HT), glucagon-like peptide (GLP-1), cholecystokinin (CCK), peptide tyrosine-tyrosine (PYY), ghrelin, leptin (LEP), growth hormone-insulin-like growth factor (GH/IGF) axis, and hypothalamic-pituitary-thyroid (HPT) axis related growth hormone (GH) and thyroid hormones (THs) ( 6 – 10 ). However, the interaction between microbial intervention and host hormones during early vertebrate development still remains incompletely revealed. Zebrafish ( Danio rerio ) have been widely used as credible models in environmental toxicology, disease, drug screening, and biomedical research ( 11 , 12 ). Germ-free (GF) zebrafish can provide a tractable system for examining associations between microbial colonization and host growth and development, such as digestion, metabolism, and hormone secretion ( 13 – 15 ). During the early periods of an animal’s life, dysbiosis of the microbiota can affect the gut-brain axis, but restoration can significantly improve the transcriptomic profile and anxiety behavior ( 16 , 17 ). Zebrafish larvae at early life (7 dpf) with innate immunity develop a functional adaptive immune system at the juvenile stage (14–21 dpf), thus GF models have been applied to study microbial regulation from the innate to adaptive immune stages ( 12 , 18 , 19 ). Thus, the high-throughput behavior, transcriptomic profile, and hormones analyses by comparing the GF zebrafish and related models were performed to clearly elucidate the regulation and inner mechanism of diverse microbial intervention strategies. The gut microbiota interacts with hormone secretion, exhibiting a bidirectional regulatory relationship with host growth and development ( 20 , 21 ). In the GH/IGF axis, growth hormone (GH) significantly influences fish growth through factors such as the growth hormone receptor (GHR), insulin-like growth factor (IGF), and insulin-like growth factor receptor (IGFR) ( 22 ). GH ( gh ) secreted by the pituitary gland can bind to the corresponding receptors ( ghra , ghrb ) in the liver and promote the synthesis and secretion of insulin-like factors IGF ( igf1 , igf2 ), which subsequently interacts with the corresponding binding receptors IGFR ( igf1ra , igf1rb ) to increase body length, weight, etc. ( 23 ). Thyroid hormones (THs) secreted by the HPT axis promote organ growth and metamorphic development from embryos to juvenile fish ( 24 – 26 ). Furthermore, the intestinal microbiota also regulate hormones such as 5-HT, GLP-1, CCK, PYY, ghrelin, and leptin which interact with each other via the gut-brain axis endocrine system, influencing host behaviors, metabolism, appetite, energy balance, growth and development, reproduction, and immune functions ( 27 – 29 ). Of these hormones, 5-HT is an important neurotransmitter, 90% of which is produced in the digestive tract, and can affect the host’ intestinal motility, secretion, appetite, and nutrient absorption ( 30 ). Microbially derived short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) are important signaling molecules in the gut-brain axis to stimulate the secretion of 5-HT, PYY, and GLP-1, and then to regulate host physiological processes by probiotic effect on improving the intestinal homeostasis ( 31 – 33 ). Therefore, the correlation between these gut-brain axis hormones and the delayed development were explored to reveal the effects of microbial intervention on GF zebrafish. Early life is an important period for initial microbial colonization or composition and is essential for host growth and development, resistance to pathogens, maturation of the immune system, and establishment of the metabolism ( 34 , 35 ). Disordered or delayed colonization of early intestinal flora was one factor leading to growth delay in children, while a reasonable flora structure promoted health ( 36 ). Probiotics have been shown to improve the host health status, and the library of bacteria isolated from gut microbiota is of great significance for mining of probiotic strains ( 37 , 38 ). Microbial communities that colonize the intestines of early juvenile fish mainly come from the water environment in which they live ( 39 ). Previously, we utilized intestinal bacteria, including genera Pseudomonas , Aeromonas , and Vibrio , with high abundance and diverse metabolic functions to further infect GF zebrafish and found it promoted the host’s immune system and intestinal goblet cells ( 12 , 40 , 41 ). Therefore, these isolated bacteria can be used as experimental mono-association strains to compare specific bacterial infection with conventional community recolonization, which may contribute to insights into the microbial therapeutic or probiotic agents. This study generated and compared conventionally raised (CR), germ-free (GF), recolonized (ex-GF), and gut bacteria–infected (Aeromonas or Vibrio; GF-A, GF-V) models across the innate and adaptive immune developmental stages. The effects of microbiota on regulatory gut-brain axis hormones and important metabolites were explored to see whether microbial intervention can contribute to the improvement of host growth and homeostasis. Further, a correlation analysis between key indexes of development, behavior, intestinal morphology, microbiota structure, gut-brain axis hormones, metabolites, and transcriptomic changes were conducted, which screened potential “biomarkers” for microbial regulation on host developmental delay and immune defects. 2 Methods and materials 2.1 Ethics approval and consent to participate The animal experiments on zebrafish were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Chongqing Medical University (Chongqing, China), and referred to the Administration of Experimental Animals issued by the Ministry of Science and Technology, and standards for experimental animals issued by the State Bureau of Quality and Technical Supervision (Approval ID: GB14922–2001 to GBT14927-2001). 2.2 Chemicals and preparation Information on the chemicals used and preparations made are provided in Supplementary Text S1 . 2.3 Fish maintenance, generation, and identification of germ-free zebrafish at larval and juvenile stage Wild-type AB zebrafish were maintained in filtered water at 28 ± 0.5 °C under a 14 h light/10 h dark cycle and were fed newly hatched Artemia sp. twice daily. Five groups were designated: CR, conventionally raised fish; GF, germ-free fish; ex-GF, GF fish recolonized with a conventional microbial community; GF-A, GF fish mono-associated with Aeromonas sp.; and GF-V, GF fish mono-associated with Vibrio sp. GF fish were generated using our optimized protocols and various identification methods ( Supplementary Text S2 ) ( 42 ). GF zebrafish were generated with treatment by antibiotic germ-free zebrafish medium (AB-GZM) solution, povidone-iodine (PVP-I), and sodium hypochlorite (NaClO). Culture medium was renewed daily, and sterile food was provided from larvae to juvenile stage. Identification was performed using the spread plate of Trypticase Soy Agar (TSA) and blood TSA, tubes of Brain Heart Infusion Broth (BHI) medium and Trypticase Soy Broth (TSB) medium under aerobic and anaerobic conditions, and molecular techniques. For ex-GF recolonization, half of the culture water from the contemporaneous CR group was added to each ex-GF well at 5 dpf, and sterile water was added to the final volume. Thereafter, ex-GF medium was renewed daily using culture water handled in parallel with the CR group. Aeromonas sp. and Vibrio sp. were isolates previously recovered and identified from adult zebrafish intestinal microbiota in our laboratory ( 38 ). At 5 dpf, GF-A and GF-V fish were infected with 10 6 CFU/mL of the corresponding freshly cultured bacteria in 7 mL per well (eight fish per well) for 24 h. At 6 dpf, the inoculum was replaced with sterile medium, and the fish were maintained under sterile conditions until sampling. 2.4 Developmental and behavioral assessment of CR, GF, ex-GF, and mono-associated fish Hatching rate, survival rate, body length, body weight, heart rate, and locomotor behavior were evaluated to determine developmental delay from larval to juvenile stages, based on established key endpoints (summarized in Table 1 ) ( 43 – 46 ). Each treatment group was established in at least three six-well plates, with eight fish per well; the average hatching and survival rates were calculated with replicate groups, whereas individual fish larvae were measured for continuous developmental endpoints. For histological processing, three replicate fish per group were scored, and the scores were considered with these biological replicates. After treatment, fish were washed, anesthetized with 100 mg/L MS-222, and sampled. Samples were fixed in 4% formaldehyde for H&amp;E and AB-PAS staining and analyzed using K-Viewer version 2.7.2.0 ( 47 ). The intestinal ultrastructure was examined by transmission electron microscopy (TEM) after fixation in 2.5% glutaraldehyde ( Supplementary Text S3 ) ( 40 ). Meanwhile, the behavioral ability of fish from all groups (n=6 fish total/group) were randomly selected and was analyzed by Etho Vision XT ® (Noldus Information Technology, Netherlands) ( Supplementary Text S4 ). Finally, the larvae samples were stored at -80°C for the subsequent transcriptomic and gene expression and metabolites assay. Spearman coefficients were calculated from the normalized feature profiles used to construct the heat map. The omics and phenotypic datasets indicated the relation pattern with visualization, OPLS-DA with VIP&gt;1, P value&gt;0.05, and 95% confidence ellipses to distinct patterns of groups. The module-trait from WGCNA (weighted correlation network analysis) with Pearson correlation coefficient and holm method adjust P values indicated the close relationship between gene expression pattern and host phenotype, such as hormone content, and developmental indexes, which were carried out for functional prediction. 2.10 Statistical analysis Statistical analyses were conducted in SPSS version 20.0, figures were produced by using GraphPad Prism 10, and violin plots presented the individual plotted values with the median and interquartile range. Normality and homogeneity of variance were assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov and Levene tests, respectively. Two-group comparisons used two-sided Student’s t-tests, and multi-group comparisons used one-way ANOVA followed by Dunnett’s test against the corresponding control, as applicable. All data was presented with means ± standard error (SEM) of replicates in each group, and symbols *, **, and *** stand for P &lt; 0.05, 0.01, and 0.001 indicating the significant differences between control and treated groups. Transcriptomic and microbiome differential abundance calculated the P values adjusted with Benjamini-Hochberg method. Microbial PCoA based on Bray-Curtis distances was interpreted descriptively. LEfSe used an LDA score threshold &gt; 2.0. PICRUSt2 pathway comparisons used one-way ANOVA followed by Duncan’s test and were considered functionally predictive. When light, the CR and ex-GF fish larvae also displayed higher sensitivity in response to environmental changes, than the GF and bacterial infection models. However, associations of microbiota loss and recolonization with fish development remain incompletely characterized, particularly at early larval GF stages ( 53 , 54 ). In this study, we combined GF, CR, recolonized ex-GF, and mono-associated zebrafish models to examine relationships among microbial status, development, behavior, intestinal morphology, transcriptomic profiles, and whole-body hormones. Multiple endpoints consistently distinguished GF from CR fish at stages with innate and adaptive immune development. The design manipulated microbial status to detect the downstream hormones, receptors, and candidate signaling pathways to establish associations and assess the therapeutic efficacy of partial phenotypic recovery. Key basic indexes at developmental endpoints were lower in GF and bacterial-infected groups than in CR fish at 7 and 14 dpf, and ex-GF fish did not completely recover from these adverse impacts. The shorter body length, lower body weight, reduced survival rate, impaired heart rate, reduced swimming ability, and weak response to light changes proved the delay, with possible mechanism growth inhibition or organ defects. Moreover, lower survival observed at 14 dpf further indicated stage-dependent differences associated with microbial status. Locomotor behavior is an important developmental ability of zebrafish and encompasses swimming, phototaxis, hunting, feeding, and optic-flow responses ( 55 , 56 ). GF and microbial-colonized groups entered the central area less frequently and showed lower movement distance, movement time, velocity, manic and active movement, together with longer static duration, than CR fish. Their velocity responses during both light and dark periods were also affected. Ex-GF fish showed partial recovery toward CR velocities, whereas GF and mono-associated fish remained more distinct, indicating the association between microbial recolonization with behavioral improvement. As part of this study’s experimental design, intestinal morphology was evaluated as a key developmental phenotype associated with microbial colonization status. Histopathological analysis showed more pronounced intestinal morphological alterations and higher H&amp;E scores in GF fish, whereas ex-GF and mono-associated groups showed partial structural improvement. Notably, AB-PAS staining of intestines proved that the goblet cells and mucus layer were obviously less than CR fish in GF and microbial colonized groups at innate immune stage. Similarities were observed between GF-14d and GF-A-14d juveniles regarding developing adaptive immune systems. These findings show that the rebuilding of microbiota or infected bacteria groups could improve the intestinal homeostasis of GF fish, but still do not reach to the normal level. Goblet cells control the secretion and production of mucus layers and then contribute to the intestinal digestive and absorption, protective functions in host ( 47 ). Microbiota plays key roles in intestinal maturation and function establishment ( 53 ), and the absence of them risked host health and system development, which can be identified by GF fish as the credible models to reveal the effects of microbial intervention. Moreover, intestinal folds and microvilli were key to functions such as mucus synthesis, immune system, mucosal barrier, and hormones, which breakdown and irregular brush possibly lead to fish disease ( 57 ). Thus, the weak intestinal homeostasis and lower density of microvilli in GF larvae and juveniles caused by the deficiency of microbiota, may contribute to the delayed development and inferior behavior. TEM further revealed irregular microvilli, altered mitochondria, and localized discontinuities at tight-junction regions. These three complementary modalities therefore provide convergent evidence for a microbiota-associated structural intestinal phenotype across tissue, mucus-cell, and ultrastructural scales. Thus, altered intestinal structure potentially delayed maturation, integrated with the growth, behavioral, transcriptomic, and endocrine differences as one indicator of microbiota-associated developmental phenotype. However, it is still necessary to measure permeability, transepithelial transport, or barrier failure in further in-depth functional research. Transcriptomic profiles differed by microbial status and developmental stage, with key DEGs highlighting biological regulatory pathways that indicate a predominantly suppressive transcriptional program in the absence of microbiota. The targeted qRT-PCR connected the principal phenotypic domains of this study with focused transcriptional outcomes: HPT-axis genes with the T3/T4-related findings; GH/IGF-axis genes with growth; PYY-a with enteroendocrine signaling; and cfbl , zgc:171509 , and mmp13a with immune-response features ( 49 , 50 ). Increased cdkn1a expression in GF-7d larvae may reflect altered cell-cycle regulation, whereas differences in guca1a and nr1d4b suggest changes in signal-transduction and hormone-responsive transcription. Similarly, the pattern of differential expression suggested a dominant suppressive response in GF fish, among which the inhibition of keratin-related genes like krt96 , ion transport ( clca1 ), and transmembrane transport-related gene slc2a6 , could reflect changes in epithelial differentiation or structural integrity, and impacts on cell functions and immune system. When developed from innate to adaptive immune, in GF-14d fish, the down-regulation of core clock genes like per2 and cry1b may indicate the disruption of circadian regulation for metabolic or physiological homeostasis. Further, the 14d-specific DEGs highlighted prolonged treatment as a critical modulator, in which GF-14d vs CR-14d indicated stress-responsive pathways and growth suppression. It also implied that the ex-GF intervention may modulate key pathways differently than GF, as microbiota served as a central metabolic stressor in regulating gene expression. Notably, this stark dichotomy implied that missing microbiota profoundly redirects key physiological processes, whereas decrease of lipid metabolism genes ( lpl , pltp ) in GF-14d indicated adaptive metabolic shifts. In significantly changed pathways, the dominance of immune-related pathways suggested systemic activation of defense mechanisms without microbial colonization in early life of zebrafish to juveniles. The enrichment of inflammatory response, and cell and protein activity implied the disrupted immune system in GF at innate and adaptive immune stages. Additionally, GSEA captured subtle, coordinated shifts through its sensitivity to synergistic gene effects, while PPI network analysis highlighted apoptosis, inflammatory pathways, and cell cycle regulation, linking these transcriptional signatures to immune function and growth impairment. Microbiota analysis revealed the absolute predominance of Proteobacteria phylum as well as the composition differences in early life and juvenile zebrafish; microbial rebuilding was probably driven by transplantation treatment based on GF models. The time-dependent increase of Pseudomonas and Mycobacterium aligned with known role in stress adaptation, while declined genera such as Thermosulfidibacter and Acidovorax in 14d zebrafish may indicate the niche shifts at diverse stages. The PCoA implied microbial rebuilding in early life, but the results gained from the 14d fish implied the community dynamics during maturation. The significantly changed genera in the ex-GF fish vs CR fish were increased over-time and indicated the intervention- and stage- pattern in the ex-GF models as ecological niche favoring certain microbial lineages. Thus, it highlighted that microbial transplantation can drive microbial diversification and restructuring based on the GF models and may contribute to the host development and physiological ability. Further, the enrichment of Fluvicola and Deinococcus and depletion of Shewanella and Rhizobium in ex-GF-7d fish may reflect adaptation to oxidative stress or living condition changes, while the enrichment of Pseudomonas , Mycobacterium , Aeromonas , and Vibrio in the CR-14d and ex-GF-14d group indicated their roles in environmental adaptation. These changed genera may be discovered as key biomarkers associated with microbial intervention and provided insights into host responses. The dominance of Burkholderiales further supported a potential role in organic matter metabolism. In the enriched pathways, the CR-7d and ex-GF-7d groups showed higher abundance of Apoptosis, Biofilm formation, and Endocytosis, while CR-14d and ex-GF-14d groups prioritized cell death mechanisms, indicating autophagy, lysosome, and Necroptosis. The activation of stem-cell signaling in CR-14d and ex-GF-14d implied novel roles in cellular regulation, highlighting the critical interplay between intervention and stages in shaping microbial functionality of zebrafish. These findings proved partial recovery is possible after recolonization and mono-association. In gnotobiotic zebrafish, colonization outcome depends on exposure timing, inoculum, persistence, community complexity, and host developmental stage ( 12 , 53 , 54 ); early microbial deprivation can therefore cause stage-specific host states even after later exposure. Consistent improvement of developmental, behavioral, intestinal, transcriptomic, and endocrine endpoints supported the association with microbial restoration and also indicated that a single colonization cannot substitute for the native shape of the CR microbiota. Together with key morphological endpoints and histopathological observations, alterations in growth-promoting hormones and downstream signaling pathways further confirmed developmental delays at the regulatory level. CRH can stimulate the pituitary gland to secrete the thyroid stimulating hormone (TSH), which then stimulates the thyroid to secrete THs, i.e. the thyroid hormone (T4), and activate triiodothyronine (T3) to regulate physiological functions such as growth, development, metabolism, reproduction, and behavior ( 58 – 62 ). Studies on GF, SPF, and ex-GF mouse models linked microbial status to intestinal cells secretion to regulate the CRH and 5-HT concentrations, and intestinal peristalsis, thereby affecting the endocrine system and neural behavior ( 63 – 65 ). Additionally, it was demonstrated that PYY, CCK, ghrelin, and leptin were also important gut-brain axis hormones that involved microbial functions affecting the body’s physiological activities, weight regulation, and obesity development ( 66 , 67 ). In this study, the deletion of microbiota in GF-7d and GF-14d zebrafish induced the lower content of important hormones such as GH, T3, T4, and 5-HT, and major gut-brain hormones like GLP-1, CKK, and PYY, which probably relate to the developmental delay or weakness of immune system. The GH/IGF axis regulates metabolism, somatic growth, development, reproduction, post-hatching metamorphosis, and behavior. Abnormal function of THs (T3, T4) can contribute to malformation and developmental impairments, among which the HPT axis is responsible for maintaining homeostasis of THs by regulating hormone synthesis, secretion, transport and metabolism development in embryo-larval zebrafish. The GF fish lack microbiota, which led to abnormal development of organ/tissues, such as intestinal cells and function defects, and may contribute to the inhibited hormones production and then caused growth retardation. The level of leptin showed the same trend, but ghrelin was disrupted in ex-GF-7d and GF-V-7d groups. Notably, the ex-GF and bacterial infection groups recovered to some extent but did not reach to the normal levels. The production of key metabolites such as SCFAs depends on the gut microbiota; depletion of these bacteria reduces SCFA abundance, impairing growth and behavior in zebrafish. Concomitant alterations in neuroendocrine factors, including thyroid hormone (T3) and serotonin (5-HT), further demonstrate regulation via the gut–brain axis. The gut-brain or endocrine axis regulated host health and growth and provided the novel microbial intervention strategy on zebrafish ( 68 , 69 ). Finally, the expression of related genes identified the transcriptomic pattern, and the correlation analysis indicated the close inner relationships from microbiota to host health. Effects from microbial deficiency, conventional microbiota reconstruction, and bacterial colonization on intestinal epithelial-related genes showed obvious developmental stage and treatment modality dependence, with the delayed maturation of intestinal absorption- and intercellular-related phenotypes, which is consistent with observations of growth indexes and intestinal histological and ultrastructural abnormalities. Taking previous reports about gut-brain axis hormones into consideration ( 70 ), we suspected that these findings could contribute to potential biomarkers (changed bacteria, hormones, DEGs, and signalling pathways) in evaluating the health risks of shift microbiota induced by the novel pollutants’ toxicity, and microbial regulation or intervention on host growth and immune defects when developed from innate to adaptive period. 5 Conclusion This study demonstrated that, compared with conventionally raised (CR) fish, germ-free (GF) zebrafish exhibit delayed growth and development across both larval and juvenile stages. The microbial rebuilding of ex-GF and bacterial infection of GF-A and GF-V fish proved that intervention could tyrestore behavioral ability, repair the damaged intestinal structure, and increase goblet cells, although these groups still did not reach the status of the unaffected groups. Moreover, transcriptomic profiling and major microbiota in zebrafish showed the potential signaling pathways and interactions between DEGs and genera. Further, the gut-brain axis hormones in GF and microbial intervention groups were altered and closely related to developmental indexes, behavioral features, histopathological score, and DEGs. Finally, these findings provided a primary microbial resource and omics background, novel insights, and important protocols for the generation and application of GF animal models and microbial strategies in evaluating the impacts of emerging pollutants on intestinal homeostasis, as well as the probiotics screen from gut bacterial strains, and biomarker research regions. However, conventional community recolonization and mono-association produced partial, endpoint-, and stage-dependent recovery, with a gap remaining between the complete microbial and developmental degree as CR fish. Thus, more investigations into intestinal functions, immune response, and the mechanism of regulatory pathways are still needed.
