Principles and fundamental concepts of germ cell transplantation We established a technique for interspecific germ cell transplantation in fish by integrating three intrinsic biological characteristics of fish. First, immediately after hatching, fish larvae possess an underdeveloped immune system that exhibits little or no rejection response to xenogeneic cells [ 1 ]. Second, during gonad formation, only somatic cells are initially present in the genital ridge, whereas primordial germ cells emerge outside it and later migrate to the ridge through chemotaxis [ 2 , 3 ]. Third, a subpopulation of fish germ cells exhibits remarkable sexual plasticity, even in species that are originally gonochoristic [ 4 ]. By exploiting these features, we found that injection of a testicular cell suspension into the peritoneal cavity of newly hatched larvae of a different species resulted in the migration of a subset of donor-derived cells—presumably spermatogonial stem cells—toward the recipient genital ridges, where they became incorporated and initiated gametogenesis [ 5 ]. Remarkably, when these testis-derived germ cells were transplanted into female recipients, they underwent oogenesis and differentiated into fully functional eggs upon recipient maturation [ 5 ]. Because most fish species repeatedly produce many eggs throughout their lifetime, germline stem cells are also present in the ovaries. Transplantation of ovarian cell suspensions into newly hatched male recipients resulted in the incorporation of donor-derived cells—presumably oogonial stem cells—into the recipient genital ridge, followed by their differentiation into functional spermatozoa [ 6 ]. For the initial establishment of this transplantation system, we used salmonids as a model because their relatively large larvae facilitate experimental manipulations such as cell transplantation. Specifically, we transplanted germ cells from rainbow trout ( Oncorhynchus mykiss ) into masu salmon ( Oncorhynchus masou ) recipients [ 7 , 8 ]. As a result of this experiment, the recipient salmon produced donor-derived rainbow trout gametes, which indicates that this germ cell transplantation system is feasible even between different species within closely related taxa. Although rainbow trout females typically produce only several tens of thousands of eggs during their lifetime, transplantation of ovarian germ cells into recipients led to the production of hundreds of millions of sperm per spawning season [ 6 ]. These findings demonstrated that ovarian germline stem cells possess self-renewal and differentiation capacities comparable to those of spermatogonial stem cells when placed within the testicular (spermatogonial stem cell) niche. Furthermore, sterilization of recipients prior to transplantation resulted in the exclusive production of donor-derived gametes. In the abovementioned experiments involving rainbow trout donors and masu salmon recipients, triploid masu salmon were chosen as recipients. In salmonids, triploidy can be easily induced by suppressing the extrusion of the second polar body, which is achieved by immersing fertilized eggs in water at approximately 28℃ for approximately 15 min at 10–15 min after fertilization [ 8 , 9 , 10 ]. These triploids develop and grow normally but are sterile due to meiotic failure [ 11 ]. The triploid masu salmon that received rainbow trout germ cells produced only donor-derived eggs and sperm in both sexes, and in vitro fertilization using these gametes successfully yielded 100% donor-derived rainbow trout offspring [ 8 ]. We subsequently generated completely germ cell-deficient recipients by knocking down or knocking out the dead end ( dnd ) gene, which is an essential gene for germ cell survival and differentiation, using antisense morpholino oligonucleotides or clustered, regularly interspaced, short palindromic repeats (CRISPR)/CRISPR-associated 9 (Cas9)-mediated genome editing [ 12 , 13 ]. Thus, through a relatively simple cell transplantation procedure, the production of surrogate broodstock that generate heterospecific eggs and sperm has become possible. Therefore, this approach is expected to be applied broadly across diverse fish taxa. When applying the germ cell transplantation technologies to diverse fish species, one of the most critical factors to consider is the developmental stage of the recipient. In general, recipients during primordial germ cell (PGC) migration or immediately after the completion of migration are most suitable for transplantation. In many cases; however, successful transplantation is still possible as long as the recipient PGCs have not yet become fully enclosed by multiple layers of gonadal somatic cells. This technology offers immense potential for the aquaculture. In fish, the cryopreservation of eggs or embryos is extremely difficult because of their large size and high lipid and yolk contents. In contrast, germline stem cells are approximately 10 µm in diameter and contain little lipid or yolk, allowing them to be readily cryopreserved. This capability has already been implemented in long-term preservation of genetic resources from elite fish strains carrying desirable genetic traits. Moreover, the eggs and sperm of large-bodied species that are difficult to maintain as broodstock can now be produced in smaller, easily managed surrogate species. More recently, this technology has been investigated for accelerating selective breeding, an area in which aquaculture lags far behind livestock industries, as well as for generating sterile fish to prevent environmental risks associated with farmed fish escaping from net pens. Recent studies have also explored the possibility of developing new aquaculture targets using germ cells isolated from freshly purchased, nonliving fish obtained from markets as donors. In the following sections, we review recent advances and emerging applications of this germ cell transplantation technology in fish. Application of surrogate broodstock technology for selective breeding Improving the quality of cultured fish requires selective breeding to enhance desirable traits. However, the history of aquaculture is far shorter than that of agriculture or livestock production involving terrestrial animals. Although successful breeding programs have been established, for example, in Atlantic salmon ( Salmo salar ) [ 40 ] and red sea bream ( Pagrus major ) [ 41 ], the application of selective breeding remains limited, particularly among marine species. One of the major obstacles is the long generation interval of many cultured fish species. In recent years, genomic information has increasingly been utilized in selective breeding, enabling more accurate genomic prediction-based selection [ 42 ]. Nevertheless, when the generation interval is long, genetic improvement inevitably proceeds slowly. Many marine species require several years to reach sexual maturity, and this extended generation interval is the primary reason why genetic progress in aquaculture has lagged far behind that achieved in plants and terrestrial livestock. Therefore, developing technologies that shorten the generation time is a top priority for dramatically increasing breeding efficiency. Surrogate broodstock technology represents a breakthrough approach for overcoming this limitation. By transplanting donor-derived germ cells from large, slow-maturing species into small, early-maturing recipients, donor-derived gametes can be produced within the gonads of the recipient fish. This approach allows the production of the next generation according to the recipient’s life cycle rather than the donor’s life cycle, thereby drastically reducing the generation time ( Fig. 2 Fig. 2. Reduction in the generation interval using surrogate broodstock technology. ). For example, tiger puffer ( Takifugu rubripes ) normally requires 2–3 years to reach sexual maturity, but by using the grass puffer ( T. niphobles ), which matures within a year, as the recipient, the generation interval can be reduced by 1–2 years [ 30 ]. Similarly, Chinook salmon ( O. tshawytscha ) typically spawns once after 3–5 years, but when rainbow trout, which matures in one year for males and two years for females and can spawn repeatedly, is used as the recipient, the generation interval can be reduced by 1–3 years [ 36 ]. In Pacific bluefin tuna ( Thunnus orientalis ), which usually matures after 3–5 years and reaches a body weight of approximately 70 kg, the use of eastern little tuna ( Euthynnus affinis )–Atlantic little tuna ( Euthynnus alletteratus ) hybrids as recipients with a body weight of approximately 1 kg enables the production of donor-derived sperm within only eight months, thereby achieving a 2.5–4-year reduction in the generation time [ 21 ]. Ongoing efforts are also being made to extend this approach to species with extremely long maturation periods, spanning more than a decade, such as sturgeons and the Mekong giant catfish ( Pangasianodon gigas ) [ 43 ]. In practical breeding schemes, the evaluation period required to assess desirable traits remains a bottleneck. However, by integrating surrogate broodstock technology with genomic selection, which enables early selection based on genome-wide information [ 44 ], all stages—from seed production and selection to the generation of offspring—can be greatly accelerated. In this framework, genomic selection improves selection accuracy, whereas surrogate broodstock technology shortens the generation time; together, these methods act synergistically to substantially accelerate genetic improvement. Thus, surrogate broodstock technology could serve as a valuable complementary tool for selective breeding in aquaculture. Furthermore, combining germ cell cryopreservation with germ cell transplantation enables the reproduction of offspring derived from cryopreserved donor germ cells [ 19 , 24 , 25 , 28 , 29 , 31 , 33 , 34 , 35 ]. This approach allows established elite strains to be maintained stably over long periods without genetic degradation while requiring minimal space and cost. Traditionally, genetic lines have been preserved through successive live breeding. Such maintenance demands careful mating strategies to avoid inbreeding while preserving the genetic background underlying desirable traits and requires keeping many broodfish alive in tanks or ponds. This conventional method is not only labour- and cost-intensive, with high space, feed, and personnel requirements, but also vulnerable to losses caused by natural disasters or disease outbreaks. In contrast, with the combined use of cryopreservation and germ cell transplantation, diverse genetic strains can be securely stored as frozen germ cells in liquid nitrogen. When necessary, these germ cells can be transplanted into recipient fish to regenerate the donor strain and produce the next generation. Such advantages are particularly relevant in aquaculture systems such as those in Japan, where production is typically small-scale and diversified across many species, rather than focused on a single species, as in Atlantic salmon aquaculture. In conventional selective breeding programs, strong selection pressure is often avoided to minimize inbreeding, which necessitates maintaining many broodfish and conducting selection over extended periods. However, in areas with limited facilities and resources, efficient improvement requires the rapid fixation of beneficial traits under strong selection pressure. By cryopreserving germ cells derived from selected elite strains before inbreeding depression occurs, these valuable genetic resources can be stably maintained over the long term. When cryopreserved germ cells are transplanted into recipient fish, donor-derived gametes can be produced, allowing seed production to continue using fish that preserve the optimal genetic background and phenotype. Thus, surrogate broodstock technology represents a powerful platform for accelerating selective breeding and managing elite genetic resources in aquaculture. Integration of gene editing and surrogate broodstock technology for the mass production of sterilized fish As mentioned earlier, with the increasing global demand for seafood, the genetic performance of aquaculture species has been enhanced through selective breeding. Domesticated strains are now widely applied in global salmon aquaculture, and in recent years, the use of genetically improved stocks has gradually expanded to other cultured fish species [ 45 ]. In parallel, genetic engineering technologies such as gene transfer and the CRISPR/Cas9 system have advanced considerably. However, most marine aquaculture operations are conducted in open sea cages, which raises serious concerns about escapees from farming sites—often due to cage damage caused by strong storms or attacks by marine mammals and sharks. [ 46 , 47 , 48 ]. Escaped fish can readily interbreed with their wild counterparts, and such hybridization events have frequently been reported [ 49 , 50 ], leading to the genetic disruption of wild populations. Therefore, producing sterile farmed fish is considered a promising and environmentally responsible strategy for mitigating such risks. Traditionally, triploidization techniques have been developed in several cultured species to produce functionally sterile fish [ 51 ]. Among salmonids, triploidization remains the most practical and widely applied method, as it can achieve relatively high induction efficiency. However, triploidization does not always yield 100% sterility, and incomplete induction is not uncommon. Even when the frequency of fertile diploids is low, the use of large numbers of fish in aquaculture means that escapees with residual fertility can still occur. Molecular biology-based approaches have recently been adopted to establish completely sterile populations. By disrupting the function of genes expressed in germ cells and essential for germline development, researchers have succeeded in producing sterile fish. The dnd gene is one such factor needed for germline development and germ cell survival in zebrafish ( Danio rerio ) [ 52 ]. Knock out of dnd via the CRISPR/Cas9 system has successfully produced sterile salmon and rainbow trout [ 13 , 53 ]. However, large-scale production of these fish is not feasible, as dnd KO fish completely lack germ cells and therefore produce no eggs or sperm. On the other hand, sterilization can also be achieved by disrupting genes expressed in gonadal somatic cells that support germline development. For example, the follicle-stimulating hormone receptor ( fshr ) gene is expressed in gonadal somatic cells and is required for the support of gametogenesis. Female fshr KO medakas exhibit small ovaries containing immature germ cells and are infertile because of inhibited vitellogenesis, whereas male KO medakas have normal testes and produce functional sperm [ 54 ]. In these surrogate female recipients, XX fshr -mutant germ cells differentiated into functional eggs. When mated with sex-reversed XX fshr mutant males, the recipients produced large numbers of offspring. All offspring were XX females and fshr mutants and were thus completely sterile. Moreover, these parental fish are expected to continuously produce sterile progeny. This system demonstrates the potential of the surrogate broodstock approach for the mass production of sterile fish in aquaculture. Unlike in medaka, male fshr KO Atlantic salmon were recently reported to be completely sterile [ 56 ]. These fish remained immature and contained only type A spermatogonia, even after they reached the age of sexual maturity. Similarly, both sexes of fshr KO rainbow trout were sterile (Yoshizaki et al ., in preparation) and carried only immature germ cells in their gonads. Therefore, mass production of sterile salmonids is expected to be possible by transplanting fshr mutant germ cells into sterile recipients with intact somatic environments, followed by mating between the recipients. This combined strategy, i.e., knocking out genes functioning in somatic cells for germline development and employing the surrogate broodstock system, represents a promising silver bullet for the stable and scalable production of sterile farmed fish. From postmortem fish to new aquaculture species Although global aquaculture production has continued to expand, the number of newly domesticated species has increased only modestly. The limited number of fish species that can be successfully domesticated imposes a major constraint on the further expansion of aquaculture. The establishment of new aquaculture species typically requires the collection of wild adult fish, the development of maturation and spawning protocols, and the establishment of stable seed production systems, a process that may take many years and often succeeds only with a few species that are readily available and tolerant of captive conditions. Within this context, the lack of change in the fish species farmed in Japan provides a clear example. Despite Japan’s astonishing diversity of more than 4,500 fish species [ 57 ], more than 90% of its aquaculture production has concentrated on just a few staple species for decades (Ministry of Agriculture, Forestry and Fisheries, 2024). Among the major species, aquaculture of Japanese amberjack ( Seriola quinqueradiata ) and Pacific bluefin tuna ( Thunnus orientalis ) relies almost entirely on the capture of wild juveniles, and a stable artificial supply of juveniles has been achieved only in recent years [ 58 , 59 , 60 ]. The second most farmed species, red sea bream ( Pagrus major ), saw the development of artificial spawning induction techniques in the 1960s, making it arguably Japan's most domesticated marine species [ 41 ]. In contrast, most species have never been evaluated for aquaculture potential simply because collecting live adult fish to obtain gametes is difficult. Recently, Yang et al . [ 61 ] reported a striking discovery that could overcome this situation: viable germline stem cells can be retrieved from dead rainbow trout within one day. When trout were kept in 10℃ rearing water after death, germline stem cells could be collected relatively efficiently if the fish were retrieved from the tank within 12 h postmortem. When transplanted into allogeneic recipients, the cells were incorporated into the gonads of the recipient with little loss of efficiency compared with freshly collected cells. Moreover, the incorporated germ cells subsequently differentiated into spermatogonia or oocytes, depending on the recipient’s sex. Although the efficiency decreased markedly after 24 h, the germline stem cells that remained viable for transplantation were still recoverable. These experiments identified an at least a 12-h grace period between the death of an individual and the complete loss of all germ cells. Importantly, transplanting germ cells from a dead fish into viable recipients enables the reacquisition of its genetic resources. The discovery of this grace period has provided a new strategy for developing aquaculture species. Specifically, germline stem cells collected from freshly harvested dead fish in fish markets can serve as the starting material to produce aquaculture juveniles ( Fig. 4 Fig. 4. Our group has applied this strategy to the whitefin trevally ( Kaiwarinus equula ), a carangid renowned for its distinctive flavour but long considered unsuitable for aquaculture because of unstable catches and fragility during handling. Dead fish obtained from set-net fisheries were used to isolate and cryopreserve germ cells. Some individuals yielded motile sperm, which enabled hybridization with Japanese jack mackerel ( Trachurus japonicus ). The hybrids proved sterile, thereby eliminating competition from endogenous gametes and providing excellent recipients. Transplantation of whitefin trevally germ cells into hybrid larvae resulted in the production of functional donor-derived sperm within one year and of eggs within two years, facilitating the successful production of donor-derived offspring ( Fig. 4 ; Morita and Harada, unpublished data). This case demonstrates the feasibility of developing aquaculture species from freshly caught dead fish by exploiting the postmortem grace period. As described above, germ cell transplantation techniques have been validated across diverse taxa ( Fig. 1 ). Hundreds of fish species within these families and their close relatives remain untapped as aquaculture species, making them promising candidates for development using this approach. Conclusions Building on the concepts discussed above, germ cell transplantation technology in fish has a wide range of potential applications. Notably, the use of small-bodied surrogate broodstock or surrogates that reach sexual maturity within a short period can substantially reduce feed consumption. Moreover, in strains produced through accelerated breeding programs, a marked improvement in production efficiency is expected. In addition, the combination of germ cell cryopreservation and transplantation represents a highly effective strategy for the long-term preservation of genetic resources, not only from elite strains used for aquaculture but also from endangered species [ 62 ]. Taken together, these advantages indicate that germ cell transplantation in fish has strong potential to contribute to the realization of environmentally sustainable aquaculture and biodiversity conservation, thereby aligning with the goals of the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs). Beyond the examples presented here, this technique could find even broader uses in the future. As described in the section on the mass production of sterile fish, combining this approach with emerging biotechnologies and new innovations may lead to the development of other unique and transformative applications.
