INTRODUCTION Sexual reproduction is a fundamental evolutionary process that shapes genetic diversity, adaptation, and species persistence ( 1 , 2 ). Among vertebrates, teleost fishes exhibit the greatest diversity of sex-determination mechanisms, encompassing nearly all known modes of sex determination, making them a powerful system for investigating how sex-determining pathways originate and evolve ( 3 , 4 ). To date, at least 28 distinct sex-determining genes have been identified across ∼140 teleost species spanning ∼20 families ( 5 – 24 ), yet none has been definitively found in cyprinids, one of the largest and most widely distributed families. Actinopterygians have undergone three rounds of whole-genome duplication, while cyprinids represent a highly diverse polyploid group containing diploid, tetraploid, and hexaploid lineages derived from multiple independent polyploidization events ( 25 – 28 ). Consequently, cyprinids exhibit notable karyotypic diversity, with chromosome numbers ranging from 2n = 50 to approximately 470 ( 25 , 29 ). This complex genomic architecture poses a substantial challenge for tracing the emergence of sex-determining genes, but it also offers a unique opportunity to investigate their evolutionary origin and diversification. As a globally distributed cyprinid of cultural and economic significance, the common carp ( Cyprinus carpio ) is an AABB allotetraploid species. Its A- and B-genome diploid progenitors diverged from each other approximately 23 million years ago and subsequently merged through hybridization and allotetraploidization approximately 12.4 million years ago ( 25 , 26 , 28 ). Identifying its master sex-determining gene is therefore central to understanding the origin of sex determination and the evolutionary dynamics of sex chromosomes in cyprinids. Fish genomes are typically rich in repetitive sequences ( 40 ), and an additional round of whole-genome duplication in cyprinids further complicates the identification of sex-linked regions. To overcome this challenge, we generated 10× resequencing data for 50 male and 50 female full-sibling progeny and their female parent (101 individuals in total; table S9) and developed a quotient algorithm (see Materials and Methods), inspired by the strategy used to identify the male-determining gene Nix in mosquito Aedes aegypti ( 41 ), to perform comparative analysis between sexes. This approach initially identified a subset of 428 candidate male-specific fragments, from which two high-confidence fragments were selected for further validation (table S10). PCR validation confirmed one 847-bp male-specific fragment (fig. S3A). Examination of read alignments in IGV showed that this fragment is embedded within a longer male-specific insertion at the terminal region of ChrB25 ( Fig. 1B ). Using primers designed from flanking X/Y-homologous sequences, PCR produced a single 1.5-kb band in XX individuals, whereas XY individuals produced both a 1.5-kb band and an additional ∼3-kb band (fig. S3B). Cloning and Sanger sequencing confirmed that the male-specific region within the 3-kb fragment spans 1487 bp (data S1). Consistent with the depth-based stepwise screening results, an independent genome-wide association study based on read-depth variation further supported this localization, identifying significantly male-biased coverage signals in the same terminal ChrB25 interval containing the 1487-bp male-specific sequence (fig. S4). The male specificity and conservation of this 1487-bp sequence were further validated across geographically diverse common carp populations (fig. S3, C to F). Fluorescence in situ hybridization (ISH) on metaphase chromosomes of XX, XY, and YY ( 32 , 42 ) carp revealed that an X/Y-homologous probe hybridized to two chromosome pairs in all karyotypes, whereas the male-specific probe hybridized exclusively to XY and YY males, with twice as many signals in YY males as in XY males ( Fig. 1, C to E , and fig. S5, A to G). These findings establish the 1487-bp fragment as a Y-linked sequence, which was further supported by droplet digital PCR (ddPCR) and quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) analyses (fig. S5, H and I). pangu is a male-specific transcript expressed before testis differentiation To determine whether the Y-linked sequence is transcriptionally active, we annotated it using time-series gonadal transcriptomes spanning the critical windows of sex determination and differentiation in common carp (fig. S6) ( 42 – 44 ). A male-specific transcript mapping to this Y-linked region was identified (fig. S7A). Rapid amplification of cDNA ends (RACE-PCR) revealed that the transcript comprises two exons following the canonical GT-AG splice rule, with a full length of 447 bp ( Fig. 1F , fig. S7C, and data S1). The putative coding sequence spans 231 bp and encodes a peptide of 76 amino acids (8.35 kDa) ( Fig. 1F and fig. S8C). Although the predicted protein is short, conserved domain analysis identified a helix-loop-helix (HLH) domain, but did not provide strong evidence for a canonical adjacent basic DNA-binding region ( Fig. 1F and fig. S8, B and C). However, BLAST searches against the NCBI nr database did not detect significant similarity to annotated protein-coding genes (fig. S8A), suggesting that this locus represents a previously uncharacterized and highly divergent gene. Given its potential role in male development, we therefore designated it pangu , after a primordial male creator in Chinese mythology who separated heaven and earth at the dawn of creation. We determined that pangu was expressed exclusively in testes ( Fig. 1G and fig. S9). Developmental expression analyses detected the transcripts as early as 19 dpf, with peak expression at 64 dpf followed by a decline after 92 dpf ( Fig. 1H and fig. S9B), consistent with testis transcriptome data (fig. S7B). Single-cell RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) of 60–80 dpf testes revealed maximum pangu expression in a distinct subset of somatic cells ( Fig. 1, I and J ). These cells were classified as putative Sertoli cells, characterized by canonical markers ( amh , dmrt1 , and sox9b ) (fig. S10; table S13), whereas germ-cell markers ( vasa , dnd , and piwil ) were confined to germ-cell clusters. In situ hybridization further suggested Sertoli-cell-associated expression of pangu ( Fig. 1, K and L , and fig. S11, A to H). Notably, established regulators of sex differentiation, including amh , dmrt1 , cyp19a1a , and foxl2 , peaked at later stages than pangu (fig. S11, I to N), placing pangu upstream in the sex-differentiation cascade. Together, its Y-linked genomic localization and early, Sertoli cell-restricted expression mirror the defining feature of known master sex-determining genes ( 7 , 9 , 12 , 13 ), strongly supporting pangu as the master sex-determining gene in common carp. pangu is a master sex-determining gene in common carp To test whether pangu functions as the master sex-determining gene, we performed in vivo loss- and gain-of-function analyses. For loss of function, CRISPR-Cas9–mediated mutagenesis was used to disrupt pangu in XY and YY males ( 32 , 42 ), generating mutant families (XY pangu Δ288 and YY pangu Δ288/+) ( Fig. 2, A and B ; figs. S12 to S14; and Table 1 ). Beginning at 35 dpf—the onset of sex differentiation (fig. S6C)—the gonads of XY pangu Δ288 carp developed an ovarian cavity and subsequently underwent feminization, including oogenesis and vitellogenesis, ultimately forming ovaries ( Fig. 2, C to F , and fig. S15). During the critical window of sex differentiation (35 to 120 dpf; fig. S6C), XY pangu Δ288 gonads exhibited a marked up-regulation of female-specific gene foxl2 accompanied by pronounced down-regulation of the male-specific genes amh and dmrt1 , showing an expression pattern closely resembling to that of wild-type females ( Fig. 2, G to I ). To examine changes at the protein level, we generated antibodies for common carp (fig. S16, A to E). Immunofluorescence across 35 to 120 dpf revealed reduced Amh and Dmrt1 and increased Foxl2 expression in XY pangu Δ288 gonads compared with XY males, consistent with a feminized gonadal phenotype ( Fig. 2, J to Q , and fig. S16). Serum hormone assays further demonstrated significantly reduced testosterone (T) and 11-ketotestosterone (11-KT) levels, with elevated estradiol (E2), in XY pangu Δ288 fish compared with XY males, resembling the hormonal profile of females (fig. S17). By contrast, YY pangu Δ288/+ mutants, which retained one functional pangu allele ( Fig. 2, A and B ), exhibited normal male gonadal development and morphology, as well as the expression of sex-related genes, proteins, and hormones. Heterologous expression of Pangu in HEK293T cells revealed clear nuclear localization, which may be associated with a KKRR motif within the HLH domain (fig. S22), consistent with Pangu functioning as a nuclear protein. To identify genes responsive to pangu activity, we compared transcriptomic profiles of XX, XY, XY pangu Δ288, and XX Tg( pangu ) gonads during the sex-determining period. Multiple-intersection analysis of these datasets identified 63 overlapping genes, among which five— cyp19a1a , cyp17a1 , foxl2b , amh , and dmrt1— were annotated as sex differentiation–related genes ( Fig. 4, A and B , fig. S23, and table S17), representing putative downstream targets of pangu . To further resolve their temporal expression dynamics, we performed qRT-PCR across finer developmental stages encompassing the sex determination and differentiation windows (fig. S6C). Consistent with the early role of ovarian aromatase observed in many other fish species ( 45 – 47 ), cyp19a1a exhibited a pronounced sex-biased expression difference as early as 19 dpf in common carp, preceding cyp17a1 and other genes ( Fig. 4C and fig. S24). Notably, cyp19a1a was strongly up-regulated in XY pangu Δ288 mutants but markedly down-regulated in XX Tg( pangu ) fish at this early stage, whereas other genes remained largely unchanged across genotypes ( Fig. 4D ). These findings indicate that cyp19a1a acts as an early downstream gene responsive to pangu perturbation and likely represents the primary target through which pangu exerts its regulatory effects. Accordingly, during the critical window of sex differentiation, cyp19a1a remained strongly up-regulated in XY pangu Δ288 gonads but was down-regulated in both XX Tg( pangu ) and XY pangu Δ288 Tg( pangu ) gonads, paralleling the expression profile of wild-type females (fig. S25A). Immunofluorescence at the onset of sex differentiation further revealed elevated Cyp19a1a protein levels in XX and XY pangu Δ288 gonads than in XY, YY pangu Δ288/+, XX Tg( pangu ), and XY pangu Δ288 Tg( pangu ) gonads ( Fig. 4, E to L ), confirming that cyp19a1a expression is responsive to pangu perturbation in vivo. Functional analyses show that pangu is both necessary and sufficient for male sex determination by repressing cyp19a1a expression. To test whether Pangu can suppress cyp19a1a transcription in vitro, we performed dual-luciferase reporter assays in HEK293T cells and Koi fin fibroblasts (kFFs), a caudal fin–derived cell line from Koi carp ( Cyprinus carpio rubrofuscus ) ( 48 ). In HEK293T cells, Pangu repressed the promoter activity of both cyp19a1a copies in a dose-dependent manner ( Fig. 4M ), and similar inhibitory effects were observed in kFFs ( Fig. 4N ). Consistently, a mutant form of Pangu lacking an intact HLH domain lost its ability to repress cyp19a1a promoter activity (figs. S14B and S25B), suggesting that Pangu has the capacity to inhibit cyp19a1a promoter activity in vitro and that the intact HLH domain is required for this activity. We next validated this regulatory relationship in vivo by disrupting cyp19a1a in XX and XY pangu Δ288 females. Loss of cyp19a1a in XX fish resulted in complete female-to-male sex reversal (fig. S26), consistent with previous reports ( 49 – 51 ), highlighting a critical role of cyp19a1a in female sex differentiation. Moreover, XY fish with mutated pangu developed into ovaries, whereas additional disruption of cyp19a1a in this background restored its testicular development ( Fig. 4, O to Q , and fig. S26). This genetic rescue demonstrates epistasis between pangu and cyp19a1a , functionally placing cyp19a1a downstream of pangu in the male sex-determination pathway. Collectively, these results support that pangu acts as a male-determining factor by promoting pretesticular differentiation, at least in part, through repression of cyp19a1a expression in common carp ( Fig. 4R ). Conservation of pangu within the genus Cyprinus Common carp comprise two major subspecies: the East Asian subspecies ( Cyprinus carpio haematopterus ), which includes Yellow River carp, Red carp, and Koi, and the European subspecies ( C. carpio carpio ), represented by the German mirror carp ( 25 , 33 ). We confirmed the presence of pangu in males from representative strains of both subspecies (figs. S3 and S27). In addition, another species within the Cyprinus clade, Cyprinus acutidorsalis , also harbors a male-specific pangu (fig. S27E). Furthermore, loss- and gain-of-function assays in Red carp (figs. S28 and S29) and Koi (figs. S30 and S31) produced reciprocal sex-reversal phenotypes, consistent with those observed in Yellow River carp. Collectively, these results demonstrate that pangu is a conserved male switch across the genus Cyprinus . DISCUSSION We identified and functionally validated pangu as a Y-linked master sex-determining gene in common carp and provide evidence that implies a downstream pathway through which it initiates male differentiation ( Fig. 4R ). This finding provides a reference point for understanding how additional sex-determining genes arise and act within conserved gonadal differentiation pathways, with three implications: (i) pangu is the first sex-determining gene validated in cyprinids; (ii) pangu represents a previously uncharacterized gene with no detectable similarity to known sex-determination or differentiation gene families using standard homology searches; and (iii) Pangu determines sex through a mechanism in which its HLH domain modulates the canonical sex-differentiation pathway in fish. Notably, pangu showed a very low and restricted expression pattern in the developing male gonad. This may indicate that pangu acts in a rare or transient Sertoli-lineage cell population to initiate, rather than broadly maintain, the male differentiation program, a possibility informed by single-cell studies of progenitor-derived Sertoli and rare supporting-like lineages in developing mouse gonads ( 52 , 53 ). Future studies should define whether pangu -positive cells represent an early Sertoli progenitor-like state and elucidate how they activate the downstream male-determining pathway. Our preliminary data support cyp19a1a as an early downstream effector of pangu ; however, the current evidence does not establish whether Pangu represses cyp19a1a directly in vivo. Given the widespread presence of E-box–like motifs in the genome, Pangu may regulate the ovarian aromatase pathway either directly through cyp19a1a promoter regulation or indirectly through upstream regulators such as foxl2 ( 54 ). Future chromatin-based approaches will be required to define the direct genomic targets of Pangu. Cyprinids comprise more than 3000 species, making them one of the largest vertebrate families ( 55 ). With their extraordinary genomic diversity and repeated history of polyploidization events ( 29 ), cyprinids offer a unique framework for studying the evolutionary origin of sex-determining genes and sex chromosomes. Zebrafish ( Danio rerio ), the cyprinid model species, has lost its sex-determining gene during domestication ( 56 ), and no such gene has yet been identified in wild populations. Although sex-specific DNA fragments or candidate sex-linked genes have been reported in other cyprinids, including grass carp ( Ctenopharyngodon idellus ) ( 57 ), bighead carp ( Hypophthalmichthys nobilis ) or silver carp ( Hypophthalmichthys molitrix ) ( 58 ), black carp ( Mylopharyngodon piceus ) ( 59 ), goldfish ( Carassius auratus ) ( 60 ), and blunt snout bream ( Megalobrama amblycephala ) ( 61 ), no definitive master sex-determining gene has been functionally validated in cyprinid fishes to date. The discovery of pangu thus represents the first such case in cyprinids and provides both a framework for identifying sex-determining genes in this family and a key entry point for investigating the evolutionary diversification of cyprinid sex-determination systems. Most sex-determining genes identified so far in fish are derived from the duplication and diversification of homologous genes ( 6 , 62 ). However, pangu shows no homology to known sex-determining or sex-differentiation genes, suggesting that it represents a distinct genetic factor in male determination. The common carp is an allotetraploid species with more than 54,000 genes (table S6A). Whether pangu originated de novo after the emergence of the genus Cyprinus , or was inherited from ancestral lineages but subsequently diverged, remains an open evolutionary question with important implications. The Pangu peptide is characterized by its minimal domain architecture, consisting almost solely of an HLH domain. In vertebrates, basic HLH (bHLH) transcription factors are broadly involved in organ development, cell differentiation, and disease processes ( 63 , 64 ), yet roles in primary sex determination have been rarely reported. A duplicated id2b homolog ( id2bbY ; encoding a 136–amino acid HLH peptide) was proposed as a candidate sex-determining gene in Arapaima gigas , although its function remains unvalidated ( 65 ). In parallel with our study, the duplicated figla-like bHLH gene figlal / figlaY was shown to determine maleness in tilapia ( 66 , 67 ). Together, these findings reinforce the emerging link between HLH/bHLH-domain proteins and vertebrate sex determination. Mechanistically, Pangu represses transcription of cyp19a1a , paralleling the negative regulatory mode of the rainbow trout ( Oncorhynchus mykiss ) sex-determining gene sdy ( 47 ). Intriguingly, male-determining genes in the distantly related class Insecta, including GUY1 in Anopheles stephensi ( 68 ) and Yob in Anopheles gambiae ( 69 , 70 ), as well as the recently identified bHLH transcription factor tesD in amphioxus ( 71 ), a cephalochordate representing the closest extant sister group of vertebrates, also encode short HLH-domain peptides. Although downstream sex-differentiation pathways differ fundamentally among insects, cephalochordates, and teleosts, the recurrent deployment of small HLH peptides as primary sex regulators suggests a notable case of convergent evolution. These findings raise the possibility that small HLH peptides have intrinsic structural or regulatory properties that make them particularly prone to being co-opted as master sex-determining factors. Future studies should elucidate the structural and mechanistic bases by which HLH peptides execute sex-determining functions. MATERIALS AND METHODS Fish materials The XY male Yellow River carp ( C. carpio haematopterus ) used for genome sequencing was obtained from the Henan Fisheries Technology Extension Station (Zhengzhou City, China). It was subsequently crossed with a conspecific female to generate a full-sibling F 1 family for whole-genome resequencing. Yellow River carp (YR) populations with confirmed phenotypic sex were collected during breeding season from Hubei Province (Dayuan Town, Shishou City), Hunan Province (Lixian County, Changde City), Anhui Province (Susong County, Anqing City), and Henan Province (Jinshui District, Zhengzhou City), China, to evaluate the applicability of the identified sex-specific marker. Additional C. carpio strains used for evaluating the functional conservation of pangu were obtained from different sources: Xingguo red carp ( C. c. singuonensis , Xingguo County, Jiangxi Province), Hebao red carp ( C. c. wuyuanensis , Wuyuan County, Jiangxi Province), Koi ( C. c. rubrofuscus , Wuhan City, Hubei Province), German mirror carp ( C. c. carpio , Jiamusi City, Heilongjiang Province), and Sea carp ( C. acutidorsalis , Guangzhou City, Guangdong Province). All common carp strains were maintained at the National Aquatic Biological Resource Center, Institute of Hydrobiology, Chinese Academy of Sciences, in aerated outdoor freshwater ponds under ambient photoperiod and temperature, and fed a commercial diet twice daily. Before sampling, fish were anesthetized in MS-222 (ethyl 3-aminobenzoate methanesulfonate; 50 mg/liter) for 5 to 10 min until loss of equilibrium. All experimental procedures involving animals were conducted in accordance with the guidelines of the Animal Care and Use Committee of the Institute of Hydrobiology, Chinese Academy of Sciences. Whole-genome resequencing and identification of male-specific sequences Genomic DNA was extracted from fin tissue of 101 Yellow River carp individuals, comprising 50 females and 50 males from full-sibling F 1 offspring (sex confirmed by histological examination of gonadal sections) and their female parent, using the Universal Genomic DNA Kit (CWBio, Beijing, China, catalog no. CW2298M) following the manufacturer’s instructions. DNA quality was evaluated using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and agarose gel electrophoresis. Libraries (∼350 bp) were prepared with the TruSeq DNA PCR-Free Kit (Illumina, USA; catalog no. 20015962) and sequenced on the Illumina HiSeq 4000 platform, generating ∼10× coverage per individual (table S9). To identify male-specific sequences, the male genome assembly was first analyzed with RepeatModeler (version 2.0) to identify species-specific repeats, and repetitive elements were masked using RepeatMasker with both RepBase and the de novo repeat libraries. The masked genome was then fragmented into reference sequences with sizes of ≥200 bp using a custom Perl script. Reads from male and female pools were mapped to these fragments using BWA-MEM, and alignments were processed with SAMtools (version 1.9). For each fragment, a normalized female-to-male depth ratio ( R i, norm ) was calculated as R i , norm = ( F i / M i ) × ( M total / F total ) where F i and M i denote the number of reads mapped from female and male pools, respectively, and F total and M total represent the total mapped reads in each pool. Autosomal fragments are expected to yield R i, norm ≈ 1, X-linked fragments ≈ 2, and Y-linked fragments ≈ 0. Candidate male-specific sequences were screened through three criteria: (i) R i, norm &lt; 0.3; (ii) exclusion of sequences with high similarity to the published female carp genome ( 34 ) ( E -value &lt;1 × 10 −10 , query coverage &gt;50%); and (iii) substantially higher read support in males than in females. The DNA fragments meeting these screening criteria were further validated by PCR populations and Sanger sequencing in diverse common carp, with their coverage patterns on genomes examined using Integrative Genomics Viewer (IGV; version 2.11.1). To provide complementary validation of the sex-linked region, whole-genome resequencing data from 50 male and 50 female full-sibling F1 individuals were analyzed using a sliding-window read-depth approach. Clean reads from each individual were aligned to the male Yellow River carp genome assembly using BWA-MEM with default parameters. The resulting SAM files were converted to BAM format, sorted, and indexed using SAMtools v1.9. Genome-wide read depth was calculated for each individual in 50-kb sliding windows and normalized by the corresponding genome-wide average depth to reduce variation caused by differences in sequencing output among samples. Normalized window depth was then compared between males and females. For each 50-kb window, statistical testing was performed to evaluate sex-biased coverage differences, and windows showing significantly elevated coverage in males but absent or markedly reduced coverage in females were considered candidate male-enriched intervals. Adjacent significant windows were merged to define candidate sex-associated genomic regions. The resulting coverage-based signals were compared with the independently identified male-specific read-depth screening results to support the delimitation of the candidate sex-determining region. Gene editing in common carp Because no suitable gRNA target site was found near the start of the pangu coding sequence, candidate gRNAs were designed across both the promoter and coding regions of pangu . In the promoter region, several gRNAs were tested individually by single-site injections, among which only gRNA1 (5′--3′) exhibited detectable activity. Within the coding sequence, multiple gRNAs were similarly screened, and only gRNA2 (5′-GGACGTGTGGAGAGAGAGGA-3′), located in exon 2 and targeting the sequence encoding the C-terminal region of the Pangu protein, was functionally effective. To ensure a complete functional knockout of pangu , two injection strategies were used: gRNA2 alone was injected to induce indel mutations, whereas gRNA1 was coinjected with gRNA2 to produce large deletions encompassing most of the pangu coding region. One-cell–stage embryos of all-male Yellow River carp were microinjected with gRNA-Cas9 ribonucleoprotein complexes (50 ng/μl of each gRNA; 500 ng/μl Cas9 protein; Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA; catalog no. A36498) at an injection volume of approximately 1 nl per embryo. Given that female common carp require approximately 2 to 3 years to reach sexual maturity, whereas males mature within 1 year, to shorten the time required for line establishment, adult P 0 pangu mutants exhibiting testis-like phenotypes were selected and crossed with wild-type females to generate mutant lines. F 1 homozygous mutants were obtained, and the F 2 generation was produced by backcrossing selected F 1 mutant females with wild-type males. Mutations were verified by PCR and Sanger sequencing and tracked across generations using allele-specific primer pairs (table S14). To further assess the functional conservation of pangu in other Cyprinus species, comparable CRISPR-Cas9 editing and breeding strategies were applied to Xingguo red carp and Koi. To examine the epistatic relationship between pangu and cyp19a1a , we generated double mutants carrying disruptions in both genes. As previously reported, the common carp genome contains two cyp19a1a paralogs, designated cyp19a1a-A and cyp19a1a-B ( 49 ). Specifically, two gRNAs targeting the first exon of each paralog— cyp19a1a-A (gRNA-A: 5′-GGAGCCCATAACAGCAGTGA-3′) and cyp19a1a-B (gRNA-B: 5′-GGGGCCCATAACAGCACTGA-3′) (fig. S26A)—were coinjected into embryos produced from crosses between XX pseudo-males ( 43 ) and XY pangu Δ288 females, thereby generating double mutants with concurrent disruptions in both pangu and cyp19a1a . P 0 fish were genotyped using sex-specific and cyp19a1a copy-specific primers, and mutations were confirmed by Sanger sequencing of the target sites. S18B). The fragment was PCR-amplified from fin tissue DNA of the genome-sequenced XY male (primers OE-insert-F/R; table S14) and cloned into the laboratory-preserved pSK-MCS-EGFP-Tol2 backbone. Specifically, the parental pSK-MCS-EGFP-Tol2 vector was digested with Eco RI and Xba I to excise the EGFP cassette, and the 3131-bp fragment was assembled into the vector by homology-based recombination using the pEASY-Basic Seamless Cloning and Assembly Kit (TransGen Biotech, China; catalog no. CU201-02), following the manufacturer’s instructions. The full sequence of final transgenic vector, pSK- pangu -Tol2 is available in GenBank under accession no. PX396239 . The recombinant plasmid was then linearized with Bsa I (New England Biolabs, catalog no. R0535), purified, and mixed with Tol2 transposase mRNA synthesized in vitro using the mMESSAGE T7 Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, catalog no. Statistical analysis All experiments were biologically replicated at least three times unless otherwise indicated. Data are presented as mean ± SD unless stated otherwise.
