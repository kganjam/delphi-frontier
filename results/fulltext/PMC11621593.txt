1 Introduction Interspecies animal hybrids have long been known as a phenomenon occurring at the incipience of species divergence, which can help interrogate drivers of species boundaries. These boundaries are in part defined by the evolution of intrinsic reproductive isolation, involving postzygotic barriers, between diverged species (Dobzhansky 1937 ; Muller 1942 ). This principle started to be outlined 100 years ago by the Haldane rule which states that heterogametic sex is preferentially rare, absent, or sterile in the offspring of two different species (Haldane 1933 ). Decades later the Dobzhansky‐Muller model of incompatibility described that incompatible epistatic interactions between two genomes lead to reduced fitness (reviewed in Chou and Leu 2015 ). In this model, genes that coevolve in each genetically isolated population can no longer interact with each other in hybrids between those populations. The developmental and reproductive outcomes of interspecies hybrid crosses have been reported throughout many taxa (Schwenk, Brede, and Streit 2008 ; Comeault and Matute 2018 ). From these studies and others (Edmands 2002 ; Stelkens, Young, and Seehausen 2010 ), it is evident that defective outcomes of hybrid crosses correlate, at least in part, to the phylogenetic distance between parental species. A gradient of hybrid incompatibility along phylogenetic space is concordant with the Dobzhansky‐Muller model, where molecular divergence among program drivers of biological function accumulates according to evolutionary time. Although this phenomenon has been described many times across the animal kingdom, it is stochastic and our understanding of how and where an incompatibility boundary arises along a given phylogeny remains incomplete. Defining a boundary of incompatibility is complex given the multiple, interconnected biological programs required for successful developmental and reproductive outcomes. Moreover, there is vast variation in the correlation between hybrid incompatibility severity and the degree of parental divergence both within and among taxonomic clades that exhibit pre‐ and postzygotic isolation patterns (Edmands 2002 ; Lemmon and Kirkpatrick 2006 ; Coughlan and Matute 2020 ). Based on studies across the animal taxa, hybrid incompatibility has been observed to arise within a wide timeframe, from partial incompatibility at divergence in the order of tens of thousands of years, as in the case of lake whitefish (Dion‐Côté et al. 2015 ) to complete incompatibility at divergence from over 4 million years (MY to MRCA) among mammals (Fitzpatrick and Fitzpatrick 2004 ), 20 MY in frogs (Prager and Wilson 1975 ), 20–30 MY in teleost fish (Bolnick et al. 2005 ), and up to 65 MY in birds (Alfieri et al. 2024 ). Variation in the timeframe for speciation has been suggested to depend on the driver of diversification being selection versus drift (Schumer et al. 2014 ; Seymour, Räsänen, and Kristjánsson 2019 ), but is also likely influenced by the nature of the underlying biological networks within the various clades. In the present study, we aim to expand the framework for investigating hybrid compatibility boundaries by using a developmental “model subfamily,” Danioninae, that encompasses the cornerstone developmental model species, zebrafish ( Danio rerio; D. rerio , in figures: Dr ) (McCluskey and Postlethwait 2015 ). We systematically outline the relationship between phylogenetic distance and developmental and reproductive outcomes across the Danio and Devario genera using interspecies hybridization of D. rerio with three of its Danio relative species and an outgroup Devario species. These species, in order of evolutionary distance to D. rerio , are orange‐finned danio ( Danio kyathit; D. kyathit, Dk [ca. 6.5 MYA to most recent common ancestor (MRCA)]), pearl danio ( Danio albolineatus; D. albolineatus, Da [ca. 10.5 MYA to MRCA]), celestial pearl danio ( Danio margaritatus; D. margaritatus, Dm [ca. 13.5 MYA to MRCA]), and giant danio ( Devario aequipinnatus ; Dev. aequipinnatus, Dev [ca. 22.5 MYA to MRCA]) (McCluskey and Postlethwait 2015 ; Rüber et al. 2007 ). The Danionin subfamily provides an opportunity for detailed analysis of hybrid incompatibilities because of the well‐established framework of developmental genetic knowledge in D. rerio as well as the stepwise phylogenetic relatedness to available relative species, spanning 6.5–22.5 MY of species divergence. We report that the timing of early developmental transitions is similar across all analyzed Danionin species, indicating conservation in the temporal regulation of early developmental programming, including the putative timing of the mid‐blastula transition (MBT). Early embryonic developmental transitions also appear conserved in interspecies hybrid crosses with D. rerio , although such hybrid embryos can exhibit developmental defects and embryonic inviability. Interspecific hybrids also exhibit considerable growth rate variation during the juvenile transition into adulthood, as well as male sterility after reaching sexual maturity. Altogether the collection of phenotypes highlights progressively increasing deleterious effects on embryonic development, life‐long growth, and reproductive potential that follow increasing phylogenetic distance. 2 Results 2.1 Early Developmental Features Are Conserved Across the Danionin Clade For our studies, we used four relative species that represent progressive phylogenetic divergence from D. rerio : D. kyathit, D. albolineatus, D. margaritatus , and Dev. aequipinnatus (Figure 1 ). We initially assessed the conservation of the early developmental events among these four Danionin species in intraspecies crosses, with D. rerio as a control. Although interspecies hybrids can exhibit asymmetry in developmental compatibility outcomes, depending the direction of the cross (reviewed in Turelli and Moyle 2007 ), we chose to assess incompatibilities in a single direction, with D. rerio as the egg source. This allowed us to take advantage of in vitro fertilization (IVF) methods involving egg extrusion, currently optimized for D. rerio but not other Danionin species, and at the same time focus solely on the relationship between incompatibilities and phylogenetic relatedness. In intraspecies natural crosses for all four species, fertilization rates were consistently above 85% (Figure S1A ). Additionally, survival rates of intraspecies embryos, at 1‐day post fertilization (dpf) were above 90% for all species (Figure S1B ). There was no statistical difference among fertilization and survival rates between these species ( n = 12 samples (four replicates; three clutches per replicate; &gt; 50 embryos per sample)/species; One‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA); p &gt; 0.01). As expected, given their close phylogenetic relatedness, overall morphology at 1 dpf was indistinguishable between relative species, including D. rerio (Figure S1C–G ). Observation of live embryos shows that during the cleavage period (45 min postfertilization [mpf]–2.25 h post‐fertilization [hpf]) the first cleavage (2‐cell) in all fertilized embryos was complete by 45 mpf, which was followed by synchronous cleavages in 15‐min intervals (Figure 2A–E ). Throughout this period, all four species showed the stereotypic cell cleavage arrangement observed in D. rerio , involving a repeated 90‐degree alternation of cell cleavage orientation along a single plane during the initial cycles, followed by a shift of the cleavage plane around the fifth cycle to generate multiple cell layers (Kimmel et al. 1995 ; Olivier et al. 2010 ). Two key stages during the blastula period, high (3.5 hpf), known in D. rerio to correspond to the MBT which includes the bulk of zygotic transcriptional activation (Kane and Kimmel 1993 ), and dome (4.33 hpf), diagnostic of progression to morphogenetic movements during gastrulation, were additionally morphologically and temporally indistinguishable across species. At 3‐3.5 hpf, the time corresponding to the 1000‐cell (1K‐cell) stage (10th cell cycle (Kimmel et al. 1995 )) at the MBT, the rate of cell density gain slows down, consistent with the known increase in cell cycle length and asynchronicity that begins at this developmental transition (Kane and Kimmel 1993 ). Through all recorded stages, pseudo‐quantification values showed no statistical difference between the four species (One‐way ANOVA; p &gt; 0.05). Altogether, our data show that embryos of these four closely related Danionin species, including Dev. aequipinnatus , exhibit conserved morphology and timing of key early embryonic developmental transitions, such as early cellular cleavage, the MBT, and the initiation of gastrulation. 3 Embryonic Development of Interspecies Danionin Hybrids Next, using IVF we analyzed the developmental competence of interspecies hybrids between D. rerio females and males from the three other Danionin species. Fertilization rates were on average above 80% for all intraspecies D. rerio controls and interspecies IVF within the Danio genus, and over 70% for IVF between D. rerio and Dev. aequipinnatus (Figure S2 ), with no statistical differences between test groups ( n = 12 samples (&gt; 250 embryos per sample)/species; One‐way ANOVA; p &gt; 0.05). For D. rerio / D. kyathit and D. rerio / D. albolineatus hybrids, developmental transitions, such as patterns and timing of cell division during the cleavage period and the formation of a dome‐like structure at the initiation of gastrulation, were similar to intraspecific D. rerio controls (Figure 3A–C ; Table S1 ; Kimmel et al. 1995 ). In contrast, a fraction of D. rerio / D. margaritatus and D. rerio / Dev. aequipinnatus hybrid embryos were observed by their morphology to show somewhat delayed developmental timing (see e.g., 256‐cell stage (2.5 hpf) and the dome stage (4.33 hpf)) (Figure 3D,E ; Table S1 ), although we observed no statistical difference between study groups in the pseudo‐quantitative analysis (Figure 3F ; One‐way ANOVA; p &gt; 0.01). Additionally, D. rerio hybrids with the sister clade Devario appear to be fully inviable. 3.1 Variability in Juvenile Growth Rate in Danio Hybrids Mirrors Phylogenetic Divergence As an indicator of developmental competence in hybrids that survived embryogenesis and became swimming larvae, we analyzed growth, as assessed by body length, at the transition from larval to juvenile stages (6 weeks postfertilization [wpf]), at regular intervals during the juvenile period (8 and 12 wpf), and into the adult stage (16 wpf) (Singleman and Holtzman 2014 ), with all hybrid and control crosses under similar husbandry conditions. Using an ImageJ tracing tool (Schindelin et al. 2012 ) on images of growing hybrid and intraspecies control clutches, we determined the average total body lengths (TL) of growing juveniles ( n = 40 for all hybrid and control crosses; Figure 5 ; Figure S3 ). At 6 wpf (Figure 5A ; Figure S3A ), the TL of intraspecies control fry for the range of Danio species were not significantly different ( D. rerio : 11.22 millimeters (mm), D. kyathit : 11.34 mm, D. albolineatus : 11.91 mm, and D. margaritatus : 10.73 mm) (One‐way ANOVA; p &gt; 0.01). In contrast, at the same time point, TLs of time‐matched D. rerio / D. kyathit , D. rerio / D. albolineatus , and D. rerio / D. margaritatus hybrid fry were significantly reduced compared to intraspecific controls, averaging measurements of 8.07 mm, 7.46 mm, and 6.02 mm, respectively (One‐way ANOVA; p &lt; 0.01). D. rerio / D. kyathit and D. rerio / D. margaritatus continued to show statistically significant reductions in TL compared to control D. rerio juveniles at 8 and 12 weeks of development and into adulthood at 16 wpf (Student t ‐tests; p &lt; 0.01; Figure 5B–D ). D. rerio/D. albolineatus continued to show a statistically significant reduction in TL at 8 wpf (Student's t ‐test; p &lt; 0.001) but not at 12 wpf or 16 wpf where there was no statistical reduction (Student's t ‐tests; p &gt; 0.01), although these hybrids did show an increase in TL variance during these later stages (see below). TL variance was larger in all hybrid combinations compared to D. rerio and other intraspecies controls. Moreover, TL variances increased for all hybrid combinations during juvenile growth: for example, variances increased in all hybrid combinations from 6 wpf to 16 wpf ( D. rerio / D. kyathit hybrids: 4.13–7.54; D. rerio / D. albolineatus hybrids: 4.45–25.8; D. rerio / D. margaritatus hybrids : 3.26–15.33 (Levene's F ‐tests; p &lt; 0.01). Similar to D. rerio , control intraspecies juveniles for other Danio species, D. kyathit , D. albolineatus , and D. margaritatus , also exhibit low TL variances throughout their growth into the adult stage ( n = 40/species, Levene's F ‐tests; p &gt; 0.05; Figure S3 ). All three Danio hybrid crosses can reach sexual maturity and appear healthy, as previously indicated (Parichy and Johnson 2001 ), with survival from fertilization to adulthood heavily dependent on phylogenetic divergence (Figure S4 ). Hybrid adults exhibit pigment patterning reminiscent of but not identical to the distinctive horizontal stripes of D. rerio (Figure S5 ). The adult pigmentation for D. rerio / D. albolineatus in our studies was, as reported in previous studies (Parichy and Johnson 2001 ; Quigley et al. 2005 ), largely similar to D. rerio with reduced pigmentation and consistent with the reduced coloration of D. albolineatus . Pigment patterns for D. rerio / D. kyathit and D. rerio / D. margaritatus adults are also similar to D. rerio but in both cases included additional pigment spots on all fins, in particular the ventral fins. Additionally, D. rerio / D. kyathit hybrids exhibit stripes that are more irregular and inconsistent in number compared to the four to five stripes in D. rerio , and D. rerio / D. margaritatus hybrids also exhibit more discontinuous or spotted stripes than D. rerio . 3.2 Danio Hybrids Exhibit Reduced Fertility Most viable hybrid fish appeared morphologically male in all interspecies hybrid combinations (see Table S3 ), consistent with previous reports of skewed sex ratios after hybridization (Runemark et al. 2018 ). Thus, we focused on assessing male fertility in adult hybrids. Attempts to breed such adult hybrid males with D. rerio females through natural pair matings were unsuccessful. We therefore tested hybrid sperm function through IVF of manually extruded D. rerio eggs with sperm from macerated testes (Walker‐Streisinger IVF method via Westerfield et al. 2007 , Baars et al. 2016 ). Fertilization rates with sperm from hybrids were significantly reduced compared to control IVF (using D. rerio sperm): across four IVF experiments per condition– D. rerio / D. kyathit 48% ( n = 322); D. rerio / D. albolineatus 7% ( n = 416); D. rerio / D. margaritatus 0% ( n = 365); D. rerio / D. rerio control 87% ( n = 398) (Figure 6A ). [Color figure can be viewed at wileyonlinelibrary.com ] Tubulin labeling also revealed that interspecies hybrid sperm tail flagella had apparent breaks in their structure, which we assessed as present when observable as a discontinuity in the labeling of at least 0.25 microns (Figure 8E',F' ). Such flagellar breaks are not observed in sperm from intraspecies wild‐type controls for any of the parent species (Figure 8A–D ). The presence of multiple breaks in a single flagellum was observed in all species hybrid combinations (Figure 8J ). A low proportion of sperm from D. rerio/D. kyathit hybrids were observed to have up to only 2 breaks, while D. rerio / D. albolineatus and D. rerio / D. margaritatus hybrid sperm had higher numbers of breaks and in greater proportions. The proportion of sperm with at least one flagellar break increased with phylogenetic distance between species combinations: 48% for D. rerio / D. kyathit hybrids ( n = 159), 81% for D. rerio / D. albolineatus hybrids ( n = 150) and 88% for D. rerio / D. margaritatus hybrids ( n = 142). Flagellum breaks were observed to be distributed across the length of the flagellum, with no obvious enrichment in distal, medial or proximal regions respective to the sperm head (Figure 8K ). A second, more severe defect was observed only in sperm from D. rerio / D. margaritatus hybrids. In this case, there appeared to be no or minimal formation of a sperm tail, with tubulin instead enriched aberrantly around the sperm head nucleus, suggesting defective sperm tail initiation and/or elongation of the flagellum. Thus, hybrid sperm exhibit flagellar formation and integrity defects, whose incidence and severity generally mirror phylogenetic divergence. 3.4 Lack of Developmental Potential of Back‐Crossed F2 Progeny From D. rerio / D. kyathit Hybrids Our studies showed that D. rerio / D. kyathit hybrid males were the only hybrid combination able to produce viable embryos in an IVF‐mediated backcross to D. rerio females (Figure 9A ) Morphologically, these D. rerio / D. rerio*D. kyathit F2 embryos were indistinguishable from D. rerio controls at 1 dpf (Figure 9B,C ; embryos with normal morphology at 24 h, from the number of fertilized eggs: D. rerio / D. kyathit [137, n = 322]; D. rerio / D. albolineatus [0, n = 416]; D. rerio/D. margaritatus [0, n = 365]) and exhibited normal development through larval stages (5 dpf–6 wpf) and into the juvenile stages (6 wpf). However, during subsequent growth through the juvenile period, these F2 fry experienced increasing rates of lethality (Figure 9D ): the percent of surviving fry was reduced to 81% at 8 wpf and 37% at 10 wpf, compared to the initial number at 6 wpf ( n = 121), and none of the developing hybrid‐derived juveniles survived past 16 wpf. In contrast, intraspecies D. rerio controls grown under similar conditions survived without losses during this period ( n = 256). Thus, male hybrids of the more closely related species combination ( D. kyathit / D. rerio ) can produce F2 offspring, yet these do not survive past the juvenile stages. Altogether, our studies show effects on fitness in Danionin hybrids that are consistent with genetic divergence of parental species, including reductions in embryo and juvenile survival, decreases in juvenile body length and increases in juvenile body length variance, decreases in sperm count and viability, and decreases in sperm tail length and increases in sperm tail length variance (Figure S7 ). 4 Discussion We show a decrease in developmental and reproductive success of interspecies hybrids between the zebrafish, Danio rerio , and four other Danionin family species: three species across the Danio genus and one species in the Devario genus. Key indicators of viability and fitness including embryonic competence, juvenile growth rate, and fertility, the latter including sperm viability and flagellar morphology, are affected in Danionin hybrids. Additionally, growth rates become consistently variable in hybrid juveniles compared to the parent species. Collectively, observed defects generally appear to become increasingly more severe in hybrids between more evolutionarily distant parental species. Overall, our studies are consistent with an increase in postzygotic incompatibilities, involving aberrant genetic interactions in hybrids, that follow phylogenetic divergence between the parent species. 4.1 Early Embryonic Development in Intraspecific Danio and Devario Embryos Is Conserved Using D. rerio , D. kyathit , D. albolineatus , D. margaritatus , and Dev. aequipinnatus as representative species, we show that embryonic morphological features and developmental timing are conserved in the Danio and Devario genera of the Danionin lineage. We find that the early stereotypic cell cleavage patterns as well as early embryonic developmental transitions during the maternal‐to‐zygotic transition and into gastrulation are indistinguishable, both by morphology and stage timing, among these relative Danionin species and the well‐described D. rerio . In particular, the observation that a slowdown in cell division temporally coincides in all species suggests that MBT landmarks, including zygotic genome activation, are occurring at a similar timepoint. However, gene expression analysis will be required to confirm similarities in zygotic genome activation programs. Additionally, embryos at 1 dpf exhibit indistinguishable body plans. Our data supports high conservation in embryonic development, and presumably underlying developmental programs, across the analyzed species. 4.2 Appearance of Developmental Incompatibilities in Hybrids With Increased Phylogenetic Distance Among interspecies hybrid progeny, hybrids between D. rerio and the more related species, D. kyathit and D. albolineatus (MRCA to D. rerio at 6.5 and 10.5 MY ago, respectively) have developmental progression and 1 dpf survival rates similar to those of intraspecific embryos from the parent species. In contrast, hybrids between D. rerio and the less related Danio species, D. margaritatus and Dev. aequipinnatus , (MRCA to D. rerio 13.5 and 22.5 MY, respectively), begin to exhibit aberrant developmental progression as early as the late blastula period and initiation of epiboly, with diminished survival rates by 1 dpf. Although a fraction of D. rerio / D. margaritatus hybrids develop into viable embryos, D. rerio / Dev. aequipinnatus hybrids appear to be fully inviable. Our findings confirm and expand on previous studies on Danionin hybrids (References in Table S4 ). Our observations of impaired morphogenesis and embryonic arrest among D. rerio / D. margaritatus and D. rerio / Dev. aequipinnatus hybrids concur with previous reports detailing defects in hybrid embryos between D. rerio and other distantly related species, including Dev. aequipinnatus and Tanichthys albonubes (minnow; ca. 33 MY to MRCA [Rüber et al. 2007 ]) (Parichy and Johnson 2001 ). Additionally, D. rerio and Oryzias latipes (medaka) hybrids (ca. 250 MY to MRCA [Schartl et al. 2013 ]) also invariably lyse within the first 24 h of development (Herberg et al. 2018 ) (Table S4 ). D. rerio hybrids with more phylogenetically distant species exhibit observable decreases in developmental competence. Such a reduction, however, still allows a fraction of viable hybrids to form in the case of the more distantly related D. margaritatus and involves a fully penetrant lethal phenotype in the case of a representative for the sister genus, Dev. aequipinnatus . Thus, hybrid embryo developmental incompatibilities begin to arise in the Danio genus at a phylogenetic distance corresponding to the D. rerio and D. margaritatus divergence at ~13.5 MY (Rüber et al. 2007 ). On the other hand, apparently normal fertilization using IVF indicates that sperm‐egg interactions continue to be fully functional at a phylogenetic distance corresponding to the D. rerio and Dev. aequipinnatus divergence of at least 22.5 MY. Dobzhansky‐Muller incompatibilities in the very early embryo can arise due to maternal‐zygotic interactions, where maternally inherited cytoplasmic components need to activate nuclearly encoded genes. The effects of incompatibilities between maternally inherited mitochondria and the nuclear genome can affect energy production in the early embryo, for example leading to defects in the energy‐intensive processes such as gastrulation (Stackley et al. 2011 ), but can also extend past early development and throughout the organism's lifespan (Rambani et al. 2022 ). Hybridization can also contribute to a mismatch in maternally inherited cytoplasmic trans regulators and nuclearly encoded cis‐regulatory sequences, for example during activation of the zygotic genome (Lee et al. 2013 ; Schulz and Harrison 2019 ). The timely progression into the dome stage for most Danionin hybrid embryos suggests a largely normal onset of zygotic genome activation, since new zygotic transcripts are known to be essential for progression into gastrulation in model species including D. rerio (Kane et al. 1996 ; Tadros and Lipshitz 2009 ; Lee et al. 2013 ). In contrast, early developmental defects in a fraction of D. rerio / D. margaritatus and D. rerio / Dev. aequipinnatus may reflect incompatibilities at the level of early zygotic gene activation (Table S1 ). Additionally, incompatibilities in early hybrid embryos can arise through the derepression of nuclearly encoded transposable elements due to incompatible or absent maternally inherited regulators of transposition (Michalak 2009 ). Developmental incompatibilities can also arise through interactions between species‐specific alleles solely expressed zygotically. Such species‐specific variants may result in absent or aberrant interactions that, possibly compounded by haploinsufficiency for each variant, result in sub‐optimally functional outputs leading to developmental defects. Further studies will be needed to assess the precise cause for the observed incompatibilities. Similarly, here we analyze solely unidirectional incompatibilities in hybrids derived from D. rerio eggs, focusing on their occurrence across phylogenetic space. Further work will be needed to address potentially asymmetric hybrid incompatibilities in reciprocal crosses (reviewed in Turelli and Moyle 2007 ) some of which have already been identified in select pairwise inter‐species Danionin hybrid combinations (Table S4 ). 4.3 Loss of Developmental Robustness With Increased Phylogenetic Difference In addition to the appearance of phenotypes in interspecies hybrids reflecting developmental incompatibilities, we observe a marked increase in phenotypic variability in hybrids. This can be observed in embryonic development leading to aberrant morphology, as in the case of interspecies hybrids between D. rerio with D. margaritatus and Dev. aequipinnatus . Additionally, we observe widely varying growth rates in juvenile Danio species hybrids, in contrast to the narrow range of growth rates observed in intraspecific controls. As with other observed phenotypic effects, size variance during juvenile growth is greater in hybrids between more distantly related species. Despite reduced average growth rates and increased variances, all Danio hybrid combinations exhibit a fraction of individuals with growth rates similar to control parental species. Indeed, the increased variance in growth rates appears to be the result of apparently slower‐growing individuals compared to intraspecies controls. Thus, variation in individual size including instances of apparently normal‐sized individuals is consistent with detrimental consequences on growth caused by additive effects from inter‐species allelic combinations. Of interest, whereas the developmental incompatibility boundary regarding embryonic viability is only observed in hybrids between the more distantly related D. margaritatus , the increase in growth rate variance occurs in hybrid combinations with all tested Danio species, including the more closely related D. kyathit and D. albolineatus . Although we can not rule out a causal relationship in hybrids between early embryonic defects and their later increased growth variance in juveniles, their different observed pattern across phylogenetic space is consistent with detrimental effects of hybrid incompatibilities occurring during juvenile growth itself, independent of early embryonic development. Indeed, zebrafish and other teleosts are known to undergo morphological transitions spanning weeks of growth after embryonic development (reviewed in McMenamin and Parichy 2013 ), each of which could be sensitive to inter‐species allelic interactions. Thus, our results suggest that developmental incompatibilities can manifest throughout the lifespan of an organism, including the period of growth to adulthood, and that robustness in lifelong growth may be more sensitive to interspecies hybrid compatibilities than simple progression through early embryonic developmental transitions. 4.4 Hybrids Exhibit Reproductive Defects Including Loss of Sperm Structural Integrity We find that adult Danio hybrid males exhibit features leading to reduced reproductive capacity, including impaired fertilization potential in vitro and reduced testes size. Reduced testes size has been observed in some hybrid combinations (Torgasheva and Borodin 2016 ; Cutter 2023 ) including fish species (Yoshikawa et al. 2018 ). Observed phenotypes of reduced sperm density and viability are consistent with aberrant gametogenesis reported in hybrid animals (Bullini 1994 ), thought to be triggered by failures of synapsis between homologous chromosomes during meiosis (Flachs et al. 2014 ; Torgasheva and Borodin 2016 ). Our systematic analysis is consistent with and expands on previous findings in interspecies hybrids within Danio species, including D. rerio / D. nigrofasciatus (9 MY to MRCA), D. rerio / D. albolineatus , and D. rerio / D. kerri (10.5 MY to MRCA) (Table S5 ). Of interest, we find that hybrid males between D. rerio and species with less phylogenetic distance, such as D. kyathit , can yield relatively normal sperm and even viable progeny (see below). Analysis of sperm samples from hybrids reveals both a decreased sperm density and the presence of sperm abnormalities, both of which likely contribute to the observed reduction of fertility in these individuals. In sperm cells from hybrids, we additionally observe defects in sperm tail morphology, including a reduction in sperm tail length that follows phylogenetic distance between parental species. Sperm flagellum formation and maintenance depend on bidirectional intraflagellar transport (IFT) of flagellar components between the ciliary base and tip (reviewed in Taschner and Lorentzen 2016 ). It is possible that the observed reduction in hybrid sperm tail length is caused by defects in flagellar component transport and/or the association of core units to generate the flagellar structure. Indeed, the most severe phenotype, observed in D. rerio / D. margaritatus hybrids, involves a defect in flagellum formation itself, with ectopic tubulin aberrantly accumulating in the sperm cell body, consistent with a lack of initiation or early stabilization of the flagellar structure. When the flagellum does form, a second phenotype we observe in Danio hybrids involves the presence of breaks in the sperm tail flagella. Flagellar structures are formed by tubulin cores additionally incorporating hundreds of closely associated protein factors, with the flagellum selected to function to provide structural integrity under dynamic conditions involving sperm motility (Hagedorn et al. 2009 ; Lehti and Sironen 2017 ). It is possible that close interactions within this structure provide protein‐protein interaction surfaces that are sensitive to Dobhansky‐Mueller‐type incompatibilities caused by non‐productive genetic interactions, which upon force‐ and movement‐induced stress leads to the observed flagellar ruptures. Further studies will be required to better understand the underlying cellular and molecular nature of these defects. 4.5 Boundaries of Species Isolation Across the Danionin Lineage All tested hybrids resulted in embryonic to juvenile inviability and/or adult sterility. Hybrids between D. rerio and Dev. aequipinnatus , a representative of its most closely related genus, Devario , invariably failed development during embryogenesis. Hybrids between D. rerio and three other tested Danio species ( D. kyathit , D. albolineatus , and D. margaritatus ) could undergo normal embryogenesis, larval and juvenile development, albeit at reduced fractions, and even develop into apparently healthy adults. All male hybrids for these three Danio species, when tested for the ability to fertilize D. rerio eggs in vitro, exhibited sterility ( D. margaritatus ) or semi‐sterility ( D. kyathit , D. albolineatus ). Among the latter, only hybrids between D. rerio and its most closely related sister species D. kyathit were able to produce F2 backcrossed offspring that survived past the embryonic stages. Such F2 progeny nevertheless exhibit a dramatic loss of viability during the larval and juvenile stages such that, in our experiments, no F2 hybrid progeny reached adulthood. This latter effect is similar to hybrid breakdown observed in F2 crosses or backcrosses from closely related animal and plant species (Dobzhansky 1970 ; Grant 1971 ), thought to occur because of non‐productive genetic interactions as alleles from each species segregate in hybrids and their progeny. Thus, hybrids between D. rerio and other species in Danio and Devario genera appear to be reproductive dead ends, through mechanisms along a continuum ranging from F1 hybrid lethality, itself occurring at developmental stages varying from embryonic to juvenile stages, as well as F1 hybrid sterility and F2 hybrid breakdown (Graphical Abstract). At one end along that continuum, D. rerio F1 hybrids with its least related species, Dev. aequipinnatus , exhibit fully penetrant embryonic lethality. At the other end, D. rerio F1 hybrids with its most closely related species, D. kyathit , can survive to adulthood and produce F2 progeny, which nevertheless are not able to reach adulthood. In between these extreme cases, for species with more intermediate relatedness, D. albolineatus and D. margaritatus , F1 hybrids exhibit a mixture of lethality at embryonic, larval, and juvenile stages, as well as adult sterility. Overall, the range of observed phenotypes for given hybrids closely follows phylogenetic relatedness between the hybridized species. The observed hybrid incompatibilities in Danionins mirror the lack of reports, to our knowledge, of naturally occurring Danionin hybrids, as well as the known infertility of captive hybrids. The pronounced hybrid incompatibilities observed in Danios &amp; Devarios contrasts with apparent lower hybridization barriers in other fish lineages such as those of the genus Amphiprion (anemonefishes), which while exhibiting a similar phylogenetic depth (10–15 MYA to MRCA) exhibit significant natural hybridization (van der Meer et al. 2012 ; Marcionetti, Schmid, and salamin 2022 ). At the same time, recent studies show that D. rerio shares genomic regions with its closest relatives, D. kyathit and D. aesculappi (McCluskey, Batzel, and Postlethwait 2024 ), suggesting it originated from an ancestral hybridization event between these two species and highlighting the potential for closely related species to overcome incompatibility barriers within an evolutionary timescale. It will be of interest to assess the extent of hybrid incompatibilities in additional lineages and how it relates to phylogenetic, ecological and developmental constraints. Our study provides insights into the divergence of developmental mechanisms across the Danionin clade. Phenotypes observed through interspecies hybridization underscore the complexities of postzygotic interactions, leading to variable outcomes along the life continuum and affecting processes such as morphology, viability and growth, and fertility. Our observations both confirm the appearance of developmental incompatibility boundaries within the Danio and Devario genera and suggest that such incompatibilities accumulate with phylogenetic divergence. Further investigation into the genetic and molecular underpinnings of the observed incompatibilities will provide deeper insights into processes shaping developmental competence and reproductive outcomes of interspecies hybrids. 5 Materials and Methods 5.1 Fish Maintenance and Growing Conditions Wild‐type zebrafish ( Danio rerio ) were raised and maintained in standard conditions at 28.5° Celsius (C) (Brand, Granato, and Nüsslein‐Volhard 2002 ). Other Danionin fish species were also raised and maintained under the same conditions (Hansen et al. 2021 ). After embryos inflate their swim bladders and become free‐feeding at day 5 postfertilization, they were fed from day 6 through week 3 of age with daily addition of 100 mL of paramecia ( ab libitum , 100–150 paramecia/mL), subsequently fed during weeks 3–6 of age with 0–8 h posthatching artemia (Brine Shrimp Direct) for 4–6 h until water exchange, and during weeks 6–16 of age with increasing doses of a mixture of fish flakes (TetraMin) and food pellets (GEMMA Micron 300) for 4–6 h until water exchange. To facilitate food type transitions, 1‐week overlap in food type was implemented between diet types, as indicated above. All intra and interspecies conditions were reared similarly, starting with all viable Day 5 hatchlings (up to 85 fry per tank) initially in 0.25 L conditioned water within 1.75‐L tanks. On Days 6–8, 0.5 L of conditioned water was added daily up to the overflow opening corresponding to 1.75 L tanks capacity. Subsequently, water exchanges were implemented with the daily addition to the tank of increasing volumes of conditioned fish water, allowing excess water to flow through a mesh at the overflow opening: remaining of Week 2: 1–2 L; Weeks 3–5: 3–5 L, Weeks 6–16: 6–16 L. Starting at Week 3 with the initial feeding of artemia and through week 16, water exchanges were timed at 4–6 h after feeding to clear excess food and restoring water quality. Fish densities were similar in hybrids and intra‐species controls, except in the case of D. rerio/D. margaritatus hybrids, where due to their greater embryonic lethality the initial number of larvae was lower leading to reduced fish densities. In wild type zebrafish and other fish species, fish density is generally known to negatively correlate with growth rate (e.g. Sen Sarma et al. 2023 ). However, in our studies we did not observe density effects, that is, lower‐density tanks (e.g., containing D. rerio/D. margaritatus hybrids) did not exhibit fish significantly larger than other tanks with higher fish densities. In at least one study, wild‐type zebrafish density positively correlates with growth variance (Sen Sarma et al. 2023 ), potentially due to food competition, yet the increase in variance observed in hybrids in our studies occurs at fish densities that are either below (e.g. containing D. rerio/D. margaritatus hybrids) or similar (other hybrids) to those in intra‐species controls. Thus, our data do not suggest significant density effects in our experiments, with the increase in variance in hybrids also not readily attributable to density. All procedures were conducted according to the University of Wisconsin‐Madison and Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) guidelines (Protocol ID: M006714). 5.2 Embryo Production For intraspecific crosses, male and female pairings of D. rerio and the other relative Danionin species were placed in breeding tanks, separated by mesh dividers, the night before embryo collection. The following morning within 3 h of the start of the light, the fish were allowed in the same compartment to promote natural breeding. Embryos were collected in Petri dishes with E3 embryonic medium (Pelegri and Schulte‐Merker 1999 ) and allowed to develop. All experiments involved mixed clutches of embryos from three breeding pairs for each species. Interspecies hybrid embryos were generated using previously described in vitro fertilization (IVF) methods (Walker and Streisinger 1983 ; Pelegri and Mullins 2011 ), adapted to include other Danionin males. For egg collection, pairs of wild‐type D. rerio fish were set up as above and, at the first sight of egg laying, females were transferred to a separate tank to interrupt the natural mating. For sperm collection, Danionin males were collected and euthanized in 1% (w/v) of buffered tricaine methanesulfonate (MESAB) in conditioned fish water for testis dissection. Testes pairs were extracted, pooled, and homogenized in 200 microliters (µL)/pair of testes in ice‐chilled Hanks' sperm solution (Walker and Streisinger 1983 ; Pelegri and Mullins 2011 ). Females from interrupted crosses were briefly (less than 30 s after gill movement slowed down) immobilized with buffered MESAB, rinsed with conditioned fish water, and placed onto a paper towel to remove excess moisture before being placed into a petri dish. A soft tissue was used to remove any excess moisture around the anal fin and cloaca area. Then mild pressure was applied in the abdomen to release eggs and the fish was returned to conditioned fish water within 1 minute (min) of immobilization. Eggs were fertilized by adding 20 µL of prepared sperm solution onto the eggs for 15 s, using the pipette tip for gentle mixing, and subsequent activation with 1 milliliter (mL) of E3 embryonic medium for 1 min, followed by flooding of the dish with E3. IVF crosses were generated by fertilizing eggs (50–200 per dish) pooled from three separate D. rerio females with sperm of D. rerio (intraspecies control) or other Danionins. Fertilization rates were assessed at 3–5 h postfertilization (hpf) when nondividing, unfertilized embryos were removed from the clutches. At 1‐day postfertilization (dpf), embryos were assessed for defects and categorized into phenotypic groups (see Results). 5.3 Brightfield Microscopy, Time‐Lapse Imaging and Analysis Early embryos (&lt; 5 hpf) were imaged using an Axioplan 2 upright microscope (Zeiss), and late‐stage (&gt; 20 hpf) embryos were imaged on a MZFLIII stereo microscope (Leica). All embryonic imaging was performed under brightfield illumination. One‐cell embryos were collected in Petri dishes and dechorionated using 2 milligrams (mg)/ml pronase (EMD Millipore) in E3 media at room temperature. Embryos were thoroughly rinsed before mounting in 500 µL of 3% methylcellulose in 8‐well µ‐Slides (iBidi). Each experiment began with one 1‐cell D. rerio control embryo and one time‐matched 1‐cell hybrid embryo mounted adjacent to each other in the same well, with both embryos aligned with their animal‐vegetal axis perpendicular to the objective. Before the first cellular division, embryos were placed under 20× magnification (1.4 plan‐apochromat objective), and brightfield images were captured every minute for 4.5 hpf. Embryonic developmental stages were ascertained by a combination of computational cell division tracing by the image analysis software, CellProfiler Analyst (Dao et al. 2016 ), and morphological characterization corresponding to Kimmel et al. 1995 . Timing of the first 10 developmental stages (up to the 1K‐cell stage), was traced using the object tracking functionality of the CellProfiler Analyst package, CellProfiler Tracer (Bray and Carpenter 2015 ). Time‐lapse images were loaded into the CellProfiler Tracer interface, and the following pipeline was utilized to process, trace, and extract data from the images. Step 1: Identify Primary Objects &gt; Step 2: Measure Object Size Shape &gt; Step 3: Measure Object Intensity &gt; Step 4: Track Objects &gt; Step 5: Overlay Outlines &gt; Step 6: Tile &gt; Step 7: Save Images &gt; Step 8: Export To Spreadsheet. In step 1, each image of the time‐lapse was subject to thresholding by an adaptive strategy of the ‘Minimum Cross‐Entropy’ method with default settings (Smoothing scale = 1.3488; Correction factor = 0.75). The “Typical diameter of objects, in pixel units (Min, Max)” range setting was specified as 40–200 pixels. After thresholding, algorithms measured the morphology of each embryo to identify the mass of cells corresponding to the blastoderm apart from the yolk (Step 1). Subsequently, it then measured the grayscale intensity in the identified mass to define cell boundaries (Steps 2–3). After these image‐processing steps, object tracing was conducted computationally to track cell divisions. The “LAP” tracking method was used to track objects by the amount of frame‐to‐frame overlap by object boundary size. When the radii of objects (cells) were 3.0 standard deviations from a previous frame, a cellular division event was recorded as a new cell division cycle. The cells were numbered and colored based on these cellular division events (Step 4). These renderings were then overlayed with the original corresponding brightfield image and saved (Steps 5–6). Data on cellular divisions and event timing was exported into a spreadsheet for subsequent analysis. Timepoints were assigned to cellular division events based on the time between fertilization and the onset of imaging. Late‐blastula stages and later stages have asynchronous cellular divisions that the computational object tracing could not track accurately, thus the developmental timing of stages 1K‐cell through dome was solely determined by morphology and image position in the time‐lapse series. 24 hpf or older embryos were immobilized with 0.1% (w/v) tricaine, mounted in water and imaged using an MZFLIII stereo microscope and an EC3 camera (Leica) at 50× magnification (Leica; Plan APO 1.0× objective, 10×/21B eyepieces, 5× zoom). 5.4 Juvenile Growth Tracing All larval fish (5 dpf) were transferred in clutches of 40 into 2.5 L (L) mouse cage tanks, fed a similar diet two times daily, and maintained at 28.5℃. At the end of each week following embryo generation, an aerial photograph was taken of each tank from ~15 centimeters (cm). Images were analyzed using ImageJ (Schindelin et al. 2012 ). To measure body length (total length (TL), from the anterior of the snout to the posterior end of the tail fin), each image was subject to black‐and‐white thresholding, and a segmented line was drawn from anterior to posterior apexes of each fish. 5.5 Testis Size Analysis The testes were dissected as above and placed on a petri dish. Images of intact testes were acquired using a MZFLIII stereo microscope (Leica) and testes were traced using the ImageJ “Wand (tracing) tool” (Schindelin et al. 2012 ), with black‐and‐white thresholding to define the pixel area within the perimeter. 5.6 Live/Dead Sperm Analysis Dissected testes from three male fish per experimental group were homogenized in 500 µL of ice‐cold Hanks' sperm solution (recipe according to Walker and Streisinger 1983 ; Pelegri and Mullins 2011 ). The sperm solution was diluted 1:10 using Hank's saline and labeled with eosin‐nigrosin (EN) staining (Agarwal et al. 2016 ). Sperm count and viability were assessed across three slides each with 15 µL of smeared sperm solution per experimental group using brightfield imaging (Axioplan 2 upright microscope [Zeiss]) at 40× magnification (1.4 plan‐apochromat objective). Live sperm were scored based on a white or faint pink head, while dead sperm were scored by a dark purple appearance. 5.7 Immunofluorescent Sperm Staining, Imaging, and Analysis Sperm was collected and diluted as described in the previous section and 15 µL of the diluted sperm sample was mounted on a poly‐ l ‐Lysine (Thermo Fisher Scientific, cat. 50‐279‐89) coated slide by gently expelling the solution in a “zigzag” motion to evenly distribute sperm in a monolayer across the slide. A hydrophobic boundary was drawn around the perimeter of the slide using a PAP pen (Electron Microscopy Sciences, cat. 71310) and the sperm was then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde in phosphate buffer saline (PBS) overnight at 4℃ in a humidified chamber. The following day, fixed sperm cells were permeabilized with 0.25% Tween in 1 × PBS for 30 min at room temperature. The slides were briefly washed in PBST (1 × PBS + Tween 0.1%) and then blocked in 10% normal bovine serum albumin (BSA) (Invitrogen) in PBST for 1 h at room temperature. The slides were washed in PBST several times (3 × 10 min/wash) before incubating with mouse anti‐goat α‐tubulin primary antibody solution (1:100 in blocking reagent, Thermo Fisher Scientific) overnight at 4℃. The primary antibody was removed with 3 × 10‐min washes of PBST the following day. The slides were then incubated with goat‐anti‐488 secondary antibody solution (1:1000 in blocking reagent, Thermo Fisher Scientific) for a minimum of 2 h at 35℃. After PBST washes, the sperm was labeled for DNA with DAPI (4′,6‐diamidino‐2‐phenylindole, 1:200 of a 100 µg/mL stock in PBS) for 10 min followed by PBST washes. The slides were subsequently mounted in 10% glycerol media. Sperm was imaged with an Axioplan 2 microscope (Zeiss) using an oil immersion 65×/1.4 plan‐apochromat objective. Widefield (40×/1.4 plan‐apochromat objective) images were processed in ImageJ (Schindelin et al. 2012 ) to adjust brightness and contrast (congruently across experimental groups) as appropriate. 5.8 Backcrossing of Hybrid Males for F2 Generation Natural breeding trials between a D. rerio female and a hybrid male at the start of the daylight cycle were unsuccessful (3 h postpairing, three attempts per cross).
