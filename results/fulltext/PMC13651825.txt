Introduction Sarcopenia is a type of muscle atrophy characterized by progressive decline in muscle mass and muscle function, including strength; it is prevalent in elderly population and various human diseases. The pathophysiology of sarcopenia is multifactorial: age, sex, obesogenic diet, and genetic factors are the strong contributors to muscle degeneration. 1 , 2 Global leaders in this field informed us that more than 2 billion individuals over 60 years old will be affected by 2050. 3 , 4 , 5 Already in 2019, USA was estimated spending annually 40.4 billion dollars ($18.7 billion in men and $21.7 billion in women) on improving adverse outcomes of sarcopenia, including frailty, disability, and death. Due to this global health care and economic burden, it is pivotal to conduct research on skeletal muscles to refine the quality of life of the elderly population. Genetics plays a significant role in lean mass maintenance; however, genetic markers that are responsible for body composition, especially lean mass, fat mass, and sarcopenia, were not appreciated for almost two decades. Importantly, clinical trials are less successful in translating potential drug targets for treating human diseases, such as sarcopenia, with no genetic musings. 5 Genome wide associations (GWASs) have successfully identified multiple loci influencing the heritability of lean mass and body composition in humans. In a bivariate GWAS analysis, the sterol regulatory element binding factor 1 ( SREBF1 ) gene near 17p11.2 region was found to control the bone and muscle phenotypes in a human population. 6 The SREBP1 protein, encoded by SREBF1 gene, is expressed in all tissues, including skeletal muscle. SREBP1, a master regulator of lipogenesis, acts as a transcriptional factor in the biosynthesis of cholesterol and triglycerides and controlling the expression of genes encoding enzymes for fatty acid synthesis (e.g., FASN , ACC1 , and SCD1 ) and myogenesis. 7 Unsaturated fatty acids were reduced in Srebf1 −/− knockout (KO) mice, and supplementation of eicosapentaenoic (EPA) acid protected them from inflammation and restored the regenerating capacity of myofibers. 8 SREBF1 mRNA levels were identified in fast-twitch (type-II/glycolytic) fibers in gluteus muscle. 9 While mammalian studies implicate SREBP1 in skeletal muscle lipid metabolism and insulin sensitivity, it remains unknown whether Srebp1 acts as a fiber-type-specific survival checkpoint in vivo , particularly in non-mammalian vertebrates, and how its loss affects selective degeneration of slow type I-like fibers. We have reported the first teleost zebrafish (ZF) model created using CRISPR-Cas9 technology and found that KO of srebf1 , ensuing alteration of essential lipid mediators (LMs) in skeletal muscle, 10 is associated with a low bone mineral density (BMD) phenotype. We, therefore, asked how lipid accumulation in skeletal muscle alters the amount of fast-to-slow vs. slow-to-fast muscle fibers, and whether it is modulated by Srebp1. Importantly, PPARs (peroxisome proliferator-activated receptors) can inhibit SREBP1 activity, connecting energy sensing and lipid metabolism; this inhibition of SREBP1 is explored for treating metabolic diseases and certain cancers. Since the gene indirectly influences triglyceride synthesis and lipid storage, and SREBF1 has been linked by human GWAS to lean mass, here we asked: what is its role in muscle fiber-type-specific muscle maintenance? Our studies are the first ever in ZF interrogating this question. Our vast combination of functional, genetic, molecular, and biochemical approaches allowed us to us to show a new role of SREBF1 working as a fiber-type-specific survival checkpoint that maintains lipid compositional and cardiolipin homeostasis, and whose disruption qualitatively reprograms LMs to drive slow-to-fast fiber switching and slow-fiber degeneration relevant to sarcopenia and sarcopenia obesity. In this study, we confirmed KO of srebf1 −/− ZF by western blot analysis, showing a significant reduction ( p &lt; 0.002) of matured isoform of Srebp1 protein at 70 kDa in whole cell lysate fraction in comparison to srebf1 wild-type (WT) siblings ( Figures 1 A and 1B) at 7 days post fertilization (dpf). We found that total body weight and lean mass were significantly lower in srebf1 KO as compared to srebf1 WT siblings (0.160 vs. 0.357 g; p = 0.005, and 0.120 vs. 0.319 g; p = 0.0004, respectively ( Figures 2 A–2C)). To study the underlying mechanisms related to lean mass and body composition, skeletal muscle-specific lipidomic profiling of WT siblings and srebf1 KO ZF (total n = 89) was performed to investigate the detrimental effect of lipids in muscle pathology. Small bioactive lipids, typically derived from polyunsaturated fatty acids (arachidonic acid, eicosapentaenoic acid [EPA], and docosahexaenoic acid [DHA]), that are enzymatically produced on demand and act locally via specific receptors before rapid inactivation. Herein, we identified eight key LMs that were significantly altered in the dorsal and caudal skeletal muscles of srebf1 KO compared to the srebf1 WT. Notably, clinically important arachidonoylethanolamide (AEA) was significantly ( p &lt; 0.01) downregulated, while palmitoylethanolamide (PEA) fatty acid level was upregulated ( p &lt; 0.001) in srebf1 KO as compared to srebf1 WT siblings ( Figures 2 D and 2E). Sex-specific analysis further strengthened our findings that AEA and PEA levels remain unaltered within WT and KO compared groups, indicating that this effect is primarily driven by srebf1 in ZF (data not shown). In addition, we showed srebf1 genotype-dependent differences in other LMs and polyunsaturated fatty acid composition ( Figures S1 B–S1E), and their potential role in skeletal muscle maintenance in srebf1 KO ZF is summarized in Table S1 . To further substantiate the effect of Srebp1 on altered lipid metabolism, we decided to measure the expression of its downstream targets involved in de novo lipogenesis. RT-qPCR analysis found significant upregulation of lipogenesis enzymes, such as acetyl co-enzyme ( acc ), fatty acid synthase ( fasn ), and sterol desaturase-1 (a.k.a. scd ) in skeletal muscle of young adult srebf1 KO ZF as compared to their WT siblings ( Figure 2 F; p &lt; 0.0001). In line with the genotype-specific lipidomics and gene expression profiles, Oil Red O (ORO) staining demonstrated elevated ( p &lt; 0.0001) neutral lipids in slow muscle fibers (SMF) (combined right SMF [RSMF] and left SMF [LSMF]) with respect to fast muscle fibers (FMFs) of srebf1 KO ZF ( Figures 4 B and 5 B–5D). In summary, loss of srebf1 induces an imbalance in fatty acid composition and decreases lean mass and body weight in young adult ZF ( Table S1 ; Figure S5 ). Imaging of slow muscle fiber using Lugol contrasting does not alter BMD in srebf1 −/− fish Due to clinical and diagnostic applications of Lugol (iodine) across human diseases, 13 we proposed that Lugol contrasting solution could be ideal in an attempt to image muscle and fat tissues without affecting BMD in the fish model. The homogenous distribution and complete penetration of the contrasting solution rely on size, shape, and length of the organism. By considering earlier staining methods, adult ZF were stained with 20% aqueous Lugol solution for 6 days (data not shown). After fish were stained with 20% aqueous Lugol solution, whole-body BMD was re-measured and compared to non-Lugol-stained values. The whole-body BMD was non-significantly ( p = 0.3) differing between stained as compared to unstained WT fish specimens ( n = 12; Figures S4 A–S4C). A trend of increased BMD was observed in 9 of 12 fish after Lugol staining ( Figure S4 A). These findings support our intent to evaluate the application of Lugol’s solution on musculoskeletal system’s components. We further sought to examine the outcome of Lugol contrasting solution on BMD under genetic and diet (environmental factors). Henceforth, we analyzed bone, muscle, and fat tissues at caudal region (anal fin to tail fin) using srebf1 −/− ZF. The caudal BMD was calculated with an average of n = 13 animals per diet/genotype group and BMD measurement was nominally altered regardless of overfeeding or srebf1 gene KO in ZF. It was noted that the staining potency of iodine decreased with time. In turn, Lugol solution was replaced every 24 h. Importantly, Lugol solution is strongly distinguished between the fast and slow muscle fiber regions of AB, srebf1 +/+ and srebf1 −/− fish groups, while this demarcation was unnoticed in unstained specimens ( Figure S4 di-ii). Overall, this staining method generated an average 500–1000 contrasted μCT images per sample, an ample supply to validate μCT findings of skeletal muscle histology within a single specimen. Our data support a model whereas Srebp1 functions as a fiber-type-specific survival checkpoint that safeguards slow, oxidative fibers against qualitative lipid dysregulation, and chronic metabolic stress (e.g., due to overfeeding and aging). This model is supported by: (1) the selective vulnerability of slow fibers to srebf1 loss despite comparable neutral lipid content, (2) fiber-type-linked remodeling of LM pathways, (3) transcriptional evidence for compensatory and stress-response programs engaged specifically in slow muscle, and (4) the specificity of the up-regulation and down-regulation of fast vs. slow-twitch contractile machinery genes. Observed neutral lipid accumulation is equivalent between SMF WT and SMF KO under overfeeding, and our sophisticated lipidomics profile informs that lipid accumulation alone cannot explain slow fiber degeneration. Therefore, we asked whether an imbalance in fatty acids such as AEA and PEA could alter the early expression of myogenic genes, especially dysregulated fast muscle-fiber-specific genes affecting muscle progenitors in srebf1 KO. Surprisingly, mining of our RNA-seq data revealed the upregulation of slow muscle-associated metabolic gene tnnt1 ( p = 0.0056 and Table 1 ) in KO larvae. This, in concert with elevated expression patterns of myogenic and progenitor markers, underscores the possibility of developmental transition between slow-to-fast vs. fast-to-slow in the absence of srebf1 ( Table 1 ). RNA-seq analysis of srebf1 KO versus WT ZF skeletal muscle identified a broad set of differentially expressed genes, as visualized by the volcano plot ( Figure 7 A), with numerous transcripts passing the false discovery rate threshold for significance. To place these changes into a mechanistic framework, we applied pathway analysis using ingenuity pathway analysis (IPA) software, which revealed an srebf1 -centered transcriptional network in which pax3 , myf5 , myod1 , and myog are tightly connected to srebf1 and to slow-fiber-associated structural genes such as myh4 and tnnt1 , alongside metabolic regulators pparg , ppara a , ppard , mlxipl , and mtor ( Figure 7 B). In parallel, IPA of the skeletal-muscle lipidomics dataset showed that srebf1 deficiency remodels a cluster of endocannabinoids and PUFA-derived mediators—including AEA, 2-AG, OEA, EPA, DHA, PGF2α, PGE2, PGD2, 11,12-EET, 12,13-DiHOME, and PEA—that converge on nitric oxide, cAMP, TRPV1, cytokines (TNF, IL-6), Ca 2+ nodes, AR, and a “formation of myofibrils” function ( Figure 7 C). These combined transcriptomic and lipidomic networks indicate that loss of SREBF1 perturbs both myogenic transcriptional programs and lipid-driven Ca 2+ -dependent signaling pathways that collectively support slow muscle fiber structure ( Figures 7 A–7C). Discussion Skeletal muscle mass, aging, lipids, and muscle fiber type switching Skeletal muscle is the largest organ system in the human body, making up about 50% of body mass, and it plays a critical role in mobility, physiology, blood supply to organs and tissues, and protects vital internal organs from damage. 14 , 15 The heritability of lean mass (skeletal muscle mass) was estimated at 42% 16 and that of grip strength ranged between 40% and 65%. 17 Muscle tissue is comprised of a heterogeneous cell population, in which type-II (fast: glycolytic) and type-I (slow: oxidative) muscle fibers exhibit distinct metabolic and physiological functions to augment muscle performance. 18 , 19 , 20 Despite significant alterations in human body composition, loss of skeletal muscle mass is mild in the third to fourth decades of life, which is exacerbated during the sixth decade due to aging. 21 Recently, sarcopenia was denoted as an equal loss of fast and slow fibers, with reduced size of type-II fibers reported in both men and women. 22 , 23 Normal aging tunes skeletal muscle metabolism toward an increased number of type-I fibers, while mechanical and metabolic loading affect the age-associated transition of fast-to-slow muscle fibers. Conversely, in states of obesity and diabetes, excessive caloric intake promotes slow-to-fast muscle fibers without influencing metabolic demand. 24 Additionally, age-related infiltration of fat impairs the potential of muscle progenitor cells to differentiate into myoblasts, which are critical for maintaining muscle mass and active repair mechanisms. 25 Altogether, we advocate that a deeper understanding of the role of fast and slow muscle fibers in the context of sarcopenia could pave the way for an early diagnosis of the disease and developing therapeutic strategies for the affected populations. 26 SREBF1 and lipid signaling mediators As noted, lipids play a major role in the pathogenesis of sarcopenia. SREBF1 gene via its protein SREBP1 contributes to cholesterol uptake and primarily regulates genes involved in fatty acid and triglyceride synthesis (e.g., FASN , ACC1 , and SCD1 ). It thus indirectly influence s triglyceride synthesis and lipid storage. PPARs can inhibit SREBP1 activity, connecting energy sensing and lipid metabolism. LMs regulate homeostasis by modulating or inhibiting SREBF1 , particularly under conditions of metabolic stress or inflammation in skeletal muscle. DiHOMEs promote fatty acid utilization in muscle, reducing the need for SREBF1 activity. In turn, PEA, HODEs, HOTrEs activate PPARs or specialized pro-resolving mediators (SPMs) pathways. Furthermore, AEA and PEA fatty acids have been implicated as therapeutic molecules for aging, inflammation, spinal cord injury, and neurological diseases. 27 , 28 , 29 , 30 Diet interventions and pharmacological therapies have been suggested to help improve age-related complications of sarcopenia. 31 Usually, lipids and lipid derivatives are dispersed within and between muscle cells; abnormally high amount of lipids and their long-term storage (intramuscular fat) activates fatty acid beta-oxidation and generate reactive oxygen species, which ultimately lead to lipotoxicity. 32 , 33 , 34 Largely, an obesogenic diet, which primarily contains high fat and low fiber content, is suggested as an environmental factor contributing to increased body weight, reduced muscle mass, and impaired muscle contraction due to altered energy metabolism. 35 A G952G variant in SREBF1 gene has affected muscle sterol metabolism in humans. 36 Genetic polymorphisms linked with SREBP1 have been associated with an increased risk of type 2 diabetes and dyslipidemia. Particularly, SREBP1/rs2297508 variant was predicted to regulate the association between dietary cholesterol intake and serum low-density lipoprotein/total cholesterol levels in Chinese preschoolers. 36 Moreover, SREBP1 expression is potentially driving lipid metabolic reprogramming via activation of the HIF-1α/TGF-β pathway. SREBF1 drives de novo fatty acid synthesis, therefore its loss is expected to suppress the lipogenesis signaling pathway and it is also expected that the skeletal muscles of srebf1 KOs will shift toward catabolism and immune modulation pro-inflammatory pathways, relying on circulating fatty acids instead of de novo synthesis . In this study, we confirmed the absence of the mature isoform of Srebp1 protein (70 kDa) in srebf1 KO zebrafish (ZF) larvae at 7 dpf. In young adults, KO of srebf1 leads to accelerated lipogenesis process. We do not view this as a paradox but rather as a biologically coherent compensatory adaptation. Although ACC , FASN , and SCD1 are canonical SREBP1 targets, they are not exclusively controlled by SREBP1. Alternative transcriptional regulators, including ChREBP, LXR, and PPARs, can drive the same genes and may become upregulated or more active when SREBP1 is absent, i.e., redundancy in lipogenic control. Furthermore, compensatory adaptive feedback from endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress, redox changes, and altered lipid composition can activate mTORC1, ChREBP, or other sensors to restore lipogenic gene expression. Finally, systemic metabolic changes (glucose, hormones, and fatty acid availability) caused by srebf1 KO in ZF may indirectly stimulate these genes through alternative regulators. To test if srebf1 affects AEA and PEA and or similar clinically important fatty acids and their impact on body composition and skeletal muscle mass (lean mass), we profiled 69 distinct LMs by targeted customized liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry. Key conclusions of our lipidomics findings are that • In skeletal muscle of the srebf1 KO, SREBF1 -dependent lipogenesis modulation is lost for all mediators that normally suppress it. • We speculate that skeletal muscle PPAR-mediated signaling, fatty acid uptake/oxidation, and anti-inflammatory effects remain mostly intact in the srebf1 KO, since they are independent of SREBF1. • The altered lipid signaling upon srebf1 deletion reflected with elevated expression of key lipid and fatty acid synthesis enzymes, such as acc , fasn , and scd in srebf1 KO with respect to controls. Zebrafish and obesogenic diet Previously, an obesogenic diet fed female (HFD) rats resulted in maternal obesity and accelerated puberty in the offspring, which in turn increased adiposity, decreased muscle mass, and reduced locomotion. 37 PPARγ is a common obesogenic target and KO of this gene in mice had shown weight gain, while treatment with GW4148, a potent activator of PPAR α and PPAR δ , significantly reduced body weight by lowering fat mass. 38 To decipher if srebf1 interacts with diet (environment) and how this gene-diet (G x E) interaction can modulate body composition, lean mass, and lipid content, we performed dietary experimentation. Our overfeeding regime revealed significantly increased total body weight and body length in both WT and srebf1 KO ZF. Body weight outcomes in Figures 2 B and 3 C add a nuance of why overfeeding may differentially affect body weight trajectories in KO vs. WT animals, suggesting the G x E interaction. 15 Increased body weight, BMI, and accumulated fat mass in obesity is responsible for skeletal muscle dysfunction. We then specifically examined gross morphology of fast (FMF) and slow (SMF) muscle fibers in srebf1 KO at different ages. As a result, smaller SMF fiber area and perimeter were found in KO compared to srebf1 WT siblings in middle-aged (17 mpf), but not in young (6 mpf) ZF. Whether the interaction of gene-diet or gene-age or diet-age could impact on SMF (type-I) fibers through lipid accumulation is unknown. 18 , 19 , 20 , 39 SREBP1 regulates myogenesis signaling pathway via muscle protein synthesis, which had been reported in cell culture model. 40 On 5 dpf, the KO larvae exhibited altered organization of slow muscle fibers. Additionally, somite and myofibers length were significantly smaller with a sign of pennation angle alteration in KO larvae, explaining faster swimming capacities under stress (dark) conditions at 7 dpf. It is well appreciated that somite size determines the size of muscles and vertebral structures formed in each segment. With shorter somites, each muscle segment is smaller. Consequently, as development proceeds, total body length is reduced, as the summed length of all segments is decreased. srebf1 loss-of-function model, supported by the confirmed genetic lesion, the marked reduction of the specific Srebp1 band, and coherent downstream transcriptional and metabolic phenotypes. Mechanistic framework We suggest a mechanistic framework connecting Srebp1 loss to reduced body length via the following chain: (1) altered membrane phospholipid composition disrupts membrane-bound receptor activity (TGF-β/myostatin and IGF-1 pathways) governing fiber hypertrophy; (2) SREBP1-regulated PUFA substrate pools determine the balance of pro-inflammatory eicosanoids vs. pro-resolving mediators (resolvins, protectins, and maresins), which regulate satellite cell proliferation, differentiation, and extracellular matrix remodeling; (3) altered LMs (LPA and prostaglandins) regulate fibroblast activation and collagen deposition, affecting connective tissue geometry; and (4) body length reflects integrated outputs of impaired bone longitudinal growth (altered osteoblast lipid signaling) and reduced muscle-tendon unit length (fewer sarcomeres in series due to altered mTORC1/lipid sensing). The unifying hypothesis is that Srebp1 controls PUFA substrate pools for bioactive LM synthesis, and its loss produces shorter body length and altered muscle architecture as coupled phenotypic outputs of the same upstream lipid signaling disruption. In addition, another component of our mechanistic model is that LMs signaling leads to cardiolipin programming changes, which then mediate muscle fiber switching ( Figure 8 ). Body weight and muscle weight decrease due to less tissue overall per segment means smaller muscles and leaner fish. Shorter myofiber also increases the density of myofibers per somite, which could explain the swimming phenotype, faster in the KO in the early muscle fibers development. Furthermore, already at 6 mpf, the muscle-rich mid-trunk region of srebf1 KO demonstrated more neutral lipids with respect to srebf1 WT siblings. Interestingly, in obese KO and WT fish, higher neutral lipids were observed in SMF muscle fibers in contrast to FMF of diet-matched genetic controls. In all, we infer that srebf1 is critical for the structural organization of SMF by controlling the effect of diet in young-to-aged skeletal muscle. There is a need for time to accumulate the lipids and to lose a muscle fight with lipotoxicity. 41 , 42 Micro-CT and histological studies Complementary approaches such as micro-computed tomography (micro-CT) and histology have become the gold standard for identifying evidence for age-related bone and muscle loss diseases, i.e., osteoporosis and sarcopenia. 43 , 44 , 45 Imaging-contrasting solutions (X-ray enhancers) have the potential to penetrate into multiple tissue types and to enhance imaging of muscle and fat tissues. 46 Lugol (iodine) has been suggested as an ideal X-ray enhancer in clinical and diagnostic applications, and chemically iodine has a high affinity to fat and glycoproteins in skeletal muscle. 13 Our Lugol staining method revealed a non-significant effect on BMD as compared to unstained WT fish specimens. Lugol contrasting solution was then applied to the fish ex vivo images to examine the outcome of Lugol contrasting solution on BMD under altered genetics and diet. These encouraging results suggest that Lugol contrasting solution could aid in imaging muscle-related tissues that further perform histology of the same specimen to reveal evidence for osteoporosis and sarcopenia related to fat infiltration. We found that overfeeding resulted in significantly lower SMF mass/density in srebf1 KO compared to srebf1 KO diet-control, but this phenomenon was not true in case of srebf1 WT. At the same time, higher HUs were obtained in srebf1 KO compared to srebf1 WT diet-controls. The higher HUs in srebf1 KO diet-control, indicative of higher glycoprotein and neutral lipid content, support our hypothesis toward accumulated lipids and muscle loss in KO ZF. The higher BMI in both srebf1 WT and srebf1 KO overfed ZF may reflect an increase of stored lipid content besides the presence of glucose and other nutrient contents as one of the major energy sources. When compared to srebf1 WT controls, the aged (24 mpf) srebf1 KO fish had reduced muscle mass. In summary, we show that srebf1 is essential for maintaining muscle mass/density in conditions of altered diet and aging. The volcano plot and RNA-seq-based network demonstrate that srebf1 is embedded within a myogenic transcriptional module ( pax3-myf5-myod1-myog ) that connects to slow-fiber sarcomeric genes and metabolic regulators, supporting the notion that srebf1 participates in maintaining oxidative fiber identity rather than acting solely as a generic lipogenic switch. The lipidomics-based pathway further shows that srebf1 deficiency alters specific endocannabinoids and PUFA-mediator species (AEA, PEA, DiHOMEs, and eicosanoids), which in turn modulate nitric oxide production, TRPV1, cytokine signaling, Ca 2+ handling, and AR-linked myofibril formation, providing a plausible route by which “lipid quality” changes translate into structural and functional defects in slow fibers. We believe that this fiber-type selectivity is among the central discoveries of this study. Fast- and slow-type fibers share the same srebf1 deletion and the same systemic environment; the fact that degeneration occurs exclusively in slow fibers points to intrinsic biochemical properties that make oxidative fibers uniquely dependent on Srebp1-regulated lipid metabolism. We now explicitly articulate the following candidate mechanism: (1) Srebp1 loss creates a high-demand/low-quality fuel mismatch unique to slow fibers that glycolytic fibers never encounter; (2). disruption lipid metabolism and highly specific LMs signaling levels; (3). cardiolipin compositional vulnerability, 47 because slow fibers harbor 3–5× more mitochondria and thus a vastly greater cardiolipin surface area that depends on Srebp1-governed PUFA remodeling for respiratory super complex integrity; and as supported by our RNAseq results, there is disruption of cardiolipin signaling; and (4). this framing elevates above findings from a metabolic phenotype to a fundamental principle of fiber-type identity maintenance with direct relevance to sarcopenia, obesity-related myopathy, sarcopenic obesity, and aging. Conclusion In summary, this study suggests a necessity of AEA and PEA fatty acids and specific LMs homeostasis along with normal functioning of the cardiolipin signaling pathway in the homeostasis of slow muscle fiber metabolism where srebf1 acts as a regulator of lipid storage to an indispensable guardian of lipid compositional homeostasis and slow muscle fiber programming. Lugol-contrasted imaging will aid in preclinical testing and diagnostic applications for muscle and fat composition. Furthermore, this work demonstrates the utility of ZF genetic modeling for skeletal muscle research, with the goal of improving the quality of life for patients prone to sarcopenia and related muscle diseases. We also emphasize the key roles of AEA and PEA, as well as the ratio between n -3/6 polyunsaturated fatty acids for muscle fibers integrity. These skeletal muscle-derived LMs form a network of checks and balances, linking nutrient status, energy metabolism, and inflammation to SREBF1 -controlled lipogenesis. In the absence of SREBF1 , there is an increase in de novo fatty acid synthesis in skeletal muscle, shifting muscle metabolism toward a higher catabolic state as evidenced in the srebf1 KO ZF. Future implications should include functional experiments to test causality, for example, by pharmacologically restoring or blocking AEA/PEA signaling in KO and WT fish and assessing rescue or exacerbation of slow fiber degeneration analysis is warranted using micro-CT and DEXA-based body composition. Limitations of the study The study did not provide direct evidence for the transition between slow-to-fast and fast-to-slow muscle fibers driven by altered fatty acid composition. The genetic role of srebf1 in geriatric/sarcopenia-induced muscle function decline requires further investigation. Furthermore, therapeutic interventions, including dietary essential fatty acid supplementation to restore selective degeneration of slow muscle fibers specifically in obesity and aging have not been explored. Detailed experimental procedures Western blot analysis for SREBP1 Lysates of 7 days post fertilization srebf1 WT and srebf1 KO larvae ( n = 10/genotype) were prepared using radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) lysis buffer (Sigma-Aldrich, Burlington, MA, USA) with protease inhibitor cocktail (Sigma-Aldrich).
