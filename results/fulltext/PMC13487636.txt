Introduction Ploidy variation is a characteristic that has pervasive consequences for fitness variation, adaptation, and evolution ( Chen 2010 ). While many plant and animal species have two sets of chromosomes, there are a substantial number of species that have three or more sets of chromosomes including many vascular plants ( Wood et al. 2009 ), the cyanobacteria Trichodesmium ( Sargent et al. 2016 ), seagrasses ( Edgeloe et al. 2022 ), bivalves ( Taylor and Foighilà 2000 ), echinoderms ( Hugall et al. 2024 ), amphibians and fishes ( Mable et al. 2011 ), and squamates ( Mezzasalma et al. 2023 ). While polyploidy has also been documented in corals (e.g. Acropora species ( Kenyon 1997 ), Acropora palmata larvae ( Baums et al. 2005 ), ancient polyploidy in Acropora ( Mao and Satoh 2019 ), and Pocillopora acuta ( Stephens et al. 2023 ; Chille et al. 2024 )), the mechanisms that give rise to polyploidy and its effects on individual fitness and response to thermal stress in corals remain unknown. The influence of ploidy on fitness is dependent on ecological context and can have positive or negative effects on traits necessitating the comparison of performance under different environments. When polyploidy arises as a response to environmental stress, it can confer a rapid increase in genetic diversity that can facilitate greater success in new environmental niches compared to diploids ( Van de Peer et al. 2021 ; Turcotte et al. 2024 ). Gene redundancy can also mitigate the fitness consequences of deleterious recessive alleles, thus impacting the ability to diversify gene function by altering redundant copies of essential genes ( Spoelhof et al. 2017 ). Environmental stressors, particularly thermal stress, can induce triploidy by disrupting meiosis resulting in diploid gametes or by preventing recently fertilized eggs from extruding the second polar body, thereby retaining two sets of maternal chromosomes (the third being contributed by the sperm) ( Comai 2005 ; Choleva and Janko 2013 ). Furthermore, an increased frequency of unreduced gametes resulting in polyploidy is associated with temperature stress in amphibians and fish ( Mable et al. 2011 ) and is also well documented in plants ( Pécrix et al. 2011 ; De Storme et al. 2012 ). Polyploids occur at increased frequencies in disrupted or harsh environments where polyploidy can beneficially impact the expression of genes and epigenetic remodeling involved in stress and hormone pathways ( Van De Peer et al. 2017 ). Environmental stress may also reduce intrinsic fitness differences between ploidies, facilitating the establishment of polyploids ( Guo et al. 2023 ). Conversely, polyploidy can have detrimental effects on fitness resulting from changes in cellular architecture and genomic instability ( Comai 2005 ). Polyploid cells have larger nuclei and are larger than diploid cells, and in polyploids cell numbers are generally reduced in direct proportion to cell size ( Benfey 1999 ). Consequently, metabolism and respiratory gas exchange rates across cell membranes differ between diploids and polyploids. In fish, these differences often result in better performance of triploids in colder temperatures ( Hermaniuk et al. 2021 ), while in oysters, polyploidy has highly variable impacts (positive, negative, or neutral) on mortality depending on environmental context ( Gagnaire et al. 2006 ; Ibarra et al. 2017 ; Bodenstein et al. 2023 ). Polyploids may be infertile or generate aneuploid gametes, with resulting zygotes varying widely in viability, and among odd-paired polyploids there is greater epigenetic and genomic instability than in even-paired polyploids ( Comai 2005 ). Furthermore, among recently formed species, polyploids experience greater extinction than diploids ( Mayrose et al. 2011 ). Autopolyploidy arises from whole genome duplication within a species, whereas allopolyploidy arises from hybridization between species. Polyploids arising from hybridization (allopolyploidy) tend to experience more severe genetic and genomic alterations than polyploids arising within a species (autopolyploidy), including widespread gene loss, histone modification and DNA methylation changes, and changes in the levels of gene expression and gene silencing ( Comai 2005 ). However, because allopolyploids gain chromosomes from an already diverged species, the acquiring of functionally different genes also presents novel allelic combinations and can result in increased fitness via heterosis, or hybrid vigor ( Chen 2010 ). Furthermore, the changes in gene expression and epigenetic remodeling associated with allopolyploidy is generally expected to result in higher adaptive potential than autopolyploidy, increasing niche space ( Van de Peer et al. 2021 ). For example, hybridization between female grass carp ( Ctenopharyngodon idellus ) and male blunt snout bream ( Megalobrama amblycephala ), both of which are diploid herbivores, generates both diploid and triploid omnivores, with increased growth rates observed in triploids relative to parental species ( He et al. 2013 ). Polyploidy can disrupt self-incompatibility systems, allowing self-fertilization ( Mable 2004 ; Novikova et al. 2023 ) and facilitating parthenogenesis ( Hojsgaard 2018 ). Self-fertilization and asexuality can be ecologically successful in the short term in isolated and relatively stable environments leading to rapid population increases and range expansions ( Peniston et al. 2021 ; Orive et al. 2023 ). In the long term, asexual lineages can face higher rates of extinction due to the accumulation of deleterious mutations and slower rates of adaptive evolution ( Schwander and Crespi 2009 ; Cheptou 2019 ). However, polyploids may initially experience lower levels of inbreeding depression relative to diploids due to an instantaneous increase in heterozygosity, which can facilitate geographical expansion and promote self-fertilization ( Clo and Kolář 2022 ). Furthermore, some triploids are fertile and reproduce via a specialized form of meiosis which produces haploid sperm and diploid eggs (e.g. in frogs ( Stöck et al. 2002 ) and loaches ( Li et al. 2012 )), or diploid sperm and haploid eggs (e.g. in plants ( Norrmann and Quarin 1987 )). Kāneʻohe Bay, Oʻahu, Hawaiʻi, has a long history of anthropogenic impacts but continues to support a healthy coral reef ecosystem ( Bahr et al. 2015 ). Corals in this ecosystem are consistently exposed to higher temperatures and lower pH relative to other sites in Hawaiʻi ( Jury and Toonen 2019 ), leading to improved survivorship under increased temperature conditions relative to corals from nearby sites. Kāneʻohe Bay has also experienced repeated coral bleaching events, including in 2014, 2015, and 2019, with the 2014/15 events representing the most severe thermal anomalies on record in Hawai‘i ( Bahr et al. 2015 , 2017 ; Yadav et al. 2023 ). Suboptimal environmental conditions and repeated acute stress make Kāneʻohe Bay a natural testing ground for mechanisms of coral resilience, including variation in ploidy. Pocillopora acuta in Kāneʻohe Bay is among the most thermally sensitive species but is ecologically persistent despite increasing environmental stress ( Jury and Toonen 2019 ; Yadav et al. 2023 ). This species was recently found to be capable of polyploidy ( Stephens et al. 2022 ), with contemporary populations composed of up to 63% triploid colonies ( Stephens et al. 2023 ). Here, we aim to understand the ecological trends, fitness consequences, and source of ploidy variation in this species. We sequenced a model population of 326 P. acuta colonies collected in 2024 and samples of 55 clonal lineages collected in 2007 and 2010 representing 2,641 colonies from across Kāneʻohe Bay ( Gorospe and Karl 2013 ). First, we assessed how the frequency of triploids changed over time from 2007 to 2024, bracketing the 2014/15 bleaching events to test the hypothesis that thermal stress of the 2014/15 bleaching events could have induced triploidy. Next, we measured bleaching and subsequent mortality of diploids and triploids under an experimental heat stress to test the hypothesis that an increasing prevalence of triploids may be a result of selection for more thermally tolerant individuals. We also measured the growth rate of both ploidies in a common garden in situ nursery to test the hypothesis that a tradeoff may exist between thermal tolerance under stressful conditions and growth under non-stressful conditions. Then we used genomic data to explore relationships between diploids, triploids, and the sister species P. damicornis to determine whether triploidy in this species resulted from intraspecific genome doubling (autopolyploidy) or hybridization with P. damicornis (allopolyploidy). Finally, larval release patterns were documented over five months to determine if triploids were capable of reproduction and if both ploidies released larvae on the same lunar cycle. Materials and methods Coral collection We obtained samples of P. acuta originally collected between 2007 and 2010 by Gorospe and Karl (2013) during extensive sampling of four patch reefs in Kāneʻohe Bay ( Fig. 1 ). These corals were originally used to identify genotypic lineages using microsatellite markers, resolving high clonality in this species. From this sample set, we selected a single representative sample for each of the 55 common clonal lineages (of 81 total), which represented 99% (2,465 of 2,493) of the originally sampled colonies. Libraries generated from these samples begin with the naming scheme PDA. In June 2024, we collected branches (5–10 cm) from 326 P. acuta colonies from 66 sites throughout Kāne‘ohe Bay, Hawai‘i ( Fig. 1 ). Branches were attached to aragonite plugs with RFID tags to ensure accurate tracking of colonies through time, sampled for genomic analysis (&lt;1 cm), and subsequently maintained at an in situ nursery at reef 13 in Kāne‘ohe Bay ( Fig. 1 ). Libraries generated from these samples are labeled with numbers only. For genomic analysis, we also selected four colonies of P. damicornis collected from the Northwest Hawaiian Islands (NWHI; French Frigate Shoals and Pearl and Hermes) in 2010 ( Marti-Puig et al. 2014 ) and one colony of P. damicornis collected from Kāne‘ohe Bay in 2016 ( Johnston et al. 2018 ). Libraries generated from these samples all begin with Pdam_. Samples Pdam_576a, Pdam_JM021, Pdam_628, and Pdam_664 were collected from the NWHI. Sample Pdam_12 was collected from Kāne‘ohe Bay. All samples were stored in salt-saturated DMSO buffer following the preservation technique of Gorospe and Karl (2013) . Genomic library preparation and sequencing We extracted DNA from all samples using the Omega BIO-TEK E-Z 96 Tissue DNA Kits. Two elutions (50 and 100 μL) were collected in HPLC water. DNA from both elutions was combined and concentrated using 3 M sodium acetate. Samples were identified as either P. acuta or P. damicornis using the restriction fragment length polymorphism (RFLP) assay developed by Johnston et al. (2018) . Briefly, the mitochondrial open reading frame region (mtORF) was amplified using PCR and then digested with the Tsp45I restriction enzyme. The colony is identified as P. damicornis if the PCR amplicon is digested. RADseq libraries were constructed using two frequent cutter restriction enzymes, MboI and Sau3AI (New England BioLabs), that cut at the same restriction site, but which have different methylation sensitivities. An in silico digest of the triploid P. acuta genome ( Stephens et al. 2022 ) using these enzymes generated approximately 250,000 150 to 300 bp fragments. After genomic DNA was digested using MboI and Sau3AI, libraries were size selected for fragments between 150 and 300 bp using Omega Mag-Bind TotalPure NGS beads (M1378-01). Following size selection, libraries were prepared using half reaction volumes of the Watchmaker Library DNA prep kit (7K0103-096), IDT xGen stubby adapters (10005924), and IDT xGen unique dual indexes (UDIs—10008052). Equimolar amounts of each library were pooled, mixed, and split across three lanes of Pippin Prep to select for fragments 291–441 bp in length (150–300 bp insert + 141 bp addition of adapters and indexes). We included duplicates of three genotypes identified by Gorospe and Karl to evaluate clonality. The 384 libraries ( n = 58 historical samples; n = 326 contemporary samples) were sequenced on one lane of the Illumina NovaSeq X Plus platform (150 bp paired end sequencing) to attain sequencing depths &gt; 30×. The five P. damicornis libraries generated from samples collected in 2010 and 2016 were sequenced separately on one lane of the Illumina MiSeq platform (150 bp paired end sequencing). Genomic analyses Quality control of all libraries was assessed using MultiQC ( Ewels et al. 2016 ). Demultiplexed reads were mapped to the unphased P. acuta genome generated from a triploid ( Stephens et al. 2022 ) using the BWA-MEM software package ( Li and Durbin 2009 ). Alignments were filtered with samtools ( Li et al. 2009 ) to remove reads with a mapping quality score below 20 and those with secondary alignments (samtools view -b -q 20 -F 0×0100). To determine the ploidy of each library, the nQuire statistical framework ( Weib et al. 2018 ) was used on the filtered bam files. nQuire assesses the quantitative distribution of nucleotide frequencies at variable sites using relative differences in likelihoods and performs best when sequencing depths are ≥20× per site. This program was used by Stephens et al. (2023) to identify diploids and triploids in their P. acuta collection from Kāne‘ohe Bay. Using nQuire, we filtered bam files for mapping quality (-q) and sequencing depth at each site (-c) using four setting combinations (-q 20, -c 20; -q30, -c20; -q 20, -c 30; and -q 30, -c 30) and then used the delta log-likelihood values calculated for each ploidy model from each of these four settings. We assigned a ploidy for each library if the same ploidy had been called for that library for 3/4 or 4/4 setting combinations. However, if a ploidy was called for 2/4 setting combinations for a library, then the ploidy of this library was identified as unknown. The ploidy for the five P. damicornis colonies sequenced separately on the MiSeq platform could not be called confidently because of inadequate depth of sequencing. To identify clonal lineages, genotype likelihoods for each library were estimated using ANGSD v0.940 with following settings requiring a missingness of &lt;20% and a minimum read depth of 10 per site: -domajorminor 1, -gl 2, -domaf 2, -docounts 1, -dopost 1, -dogeno 16, -doGlf 2, -SNP_pval 1e-7, -minInd 310, -nInd 387, -minmapQ 30, -minQ 30, -doBcf 1, -geno_minDepth 10, -dumpCounts 2, -doIBS 1, -doCov 1, and -makeMatrix 1 ( Korneliussen et al. 2014 ). ANGSD only calls genotype likelihoods as diploid. ANGSD was also used to generate an identity by state matrix (-doIBS 1). This analysis does not use an evolutionary model and therefore should not be interpreted as a phylogeny. We then used the “vcf_clone_detect.py” script from https://github.com/pimbongaerts/radseq (retrieved 2025 February 20) to identify clonal lineages using the VCF file generated by ANGSD. A threshold of &gt;97.51% shared SNPs was identified as the clonal threshold based on the distribution of shared SNPs across all pairwise combinations of samples ( Fig. 2 ). Once ploidy and clonal lineages were identified, the GATK toolkit ( McKenna et al. 2010 ) (v4.2.4.1) was used to call diploid and triploid genotypes using the best sequenced library from each clonal lineage. Unlike ANGSD, GATK can call genotypes for different ploidies if the ploidy is known. Within the GATK framework, HaplotypeCaller was used to call variants for diploids (default settings) and triploids (“-ploidy 3”) separately. Because the ploidy of the P. damicornis samples sequenced on the MiSeq platform could not be confidently identified due to lower sequencing depth and there is no evidence of polyploidy in this species yet, their genotypes were called as diploid. After variants were called separately by ploidy, genotyped VCFs were combined into a mixed ploidy VCF using CombineGVCFs and variants were identified using GenotypeGVCFs. To examine shared ancestry between diploids, triploids, and P. damicornis , the mixed ploidy VCF was filtered to keep sites with a minimum read depth of 10 per site, a minimum quality score of 30, and a maximum missingness of 2 libraries per site, while removing sites located within 1,000 bp of each other using BCFtools v1.22. This dataset is referred to as the filtered mixed ploidy VCF and was used to assess genomic relationships using three different programs: (i) Discriminant Analysis of Principal Components (DAPC), which does not use a model of evolution to assess relationships but instead uses k-means clustering; (ii) STRUCTURE, which does use a model of evolution to infer ancestry; and (iii) Statistical Analysis of Mixed Ploidy Populations (StAMPP), which does use a model of evolution to generate genetic distances between individuals. To identify clusters of genetically related individuals using DAPC ( Jombart et al. 2010 ), the filtered mixed ploidy VCF was converted into a genind object using the vcfR2genind function in the R package vcfR ( Knaus and Grünwald 2017 ). In the R package adegenet ( Jombart 2008 ), we then used 6 PCA axes and 19 DA axes to assess relationships after interactively selecting clusters using the find.clusters and optim.a.score functions. Then to assess population structure between unique diploid, triploid, and P. damicornis genotypes, we parallelized STRUCTURE v.2.3.4 ( Pritchard et al. 2000 ), which can be used for assessing relationships in mixed ploidy assemblages, with Structure_threader ( Pina-Martins et al. 2017 ). The filtered mixed ploidy VCF was converted to STRUCTURE format using the “polyVCFtoStructure.py” script from https://github.com/tkchafin/polyrad_scripts/blob/main/polyVCFtoStructure.py (retrieved 2025 July 17). Ten replicates of K = 1 to 15 were run, each with a burnin of 20,000 and 50,000 MCMC generations. The best value of K was determined based on Evanno calculations of Δ K and mean L ( K ) using STRUCTURE HARVESTER ( Earl and vonHoldt 2012 ) and replicate runs of each K were consolidated using CLUMPAK from https://clumpak.tau.ac.il/index.html (accessed 2025 July 31). To quantify genetic distances between unique diploid, triploid, and P. damicornis genotypes, allele depth per site per library was extracted in R v4.3.0 from the filtered mixed ploidy VCF and the program StAMPP ( Pembleton et al. 2013 ) was used to generate a matrix of Nei's D genetic distance between individuals with the stamppNeisD function. With this matrix, we ran a principal coordinates analysis (PCoA) using cmdscale with k = 4. We also generated an unrooted neighbor-joining network using the nj function in the R package ape ( Paradis and Schliep 2019 ). Histone amplification The histone region that was identified in Johnston et al. (2017) and found to differentiate P. grandis from other Pocillopora species in Johnston et al. (2018) was amplified from one colony from each unique genotype to determine if there was sequence variation between diploids, triploids, and colonies with the P. damicornis mtORF sequence. Amplicons were visualized on a 2% agarose gel. Amplicons were sequenced from colonies with unique banding patterns and alignments were analyzed using Geneious Prime v.2025.1.1 bioinformatics software. Growth measurements To assess growth rate, surface area, volume, and buoyant weight were collected on 2024 July 2 and 2024 October 22 for 315 colonies. During this interval, all colonies were maintained in a common garden at an in situ nursery at patch reef 13 in Kāneʻohe Bay ( Fig. 1 ). Surface area and volume were measured from structure-from-motion photogrammetry using a custom seven-camera turntable which creates high overlap and coverage that captures 210 photos per time point ( Miller et al. 2024 ). Six Metashape Ground Control Point markers, of which a minimum of two were visible in every photo, were included to allow for standardized measurements across time. Images were processed using Metashape photogrammetry software (version 2.1.2) to build 3D models before trimming models to remove aragonite plugs. Surface area, volume, and fractal dimension (a measure of structural complexity) were measured using AddTools for Metashape add-on software ( Miller et al. 2024 ). Buoyant weight was measured using a bottom-weighing scale while each fragment was fully submerged in seawater. Temperature was recorded with each weight measurement. Salinity was recorded at the start of each time point measurement. Using the density of aragonite of 2.93 g/cm 3 following ( Jokiel et al. 1978 ) as the skeletal density of P. acuta , the dry weight of each coral fragment at each time point was calculated as: dry weight of object = weight in water / ( 1 – ( density of water / skeletal density ) ) Thermal stress experiment, photosynthetic efficiency, and survival In October 2024, after the final growth measurements were collected, fragments (2–3 cm) were taken from 225 of the largest colonies held at the in situ nursery at reef 13, fixed to aragonite plugs, and allowed to recover for 2 weeks in 200 L tanks in an outdoor flow-through seawater mesocosm before stress testing. Temperature was ramped from 26.3 to 30.9 °C over 18 days and then maintained at 30.9 °C until complete mortality of all samples, which was 73 days after thermal ramp began. Symbiont photosynthetic efficiency (maximum quantum yield; F v / F m ) was measured using pulse-amplitude modulated fluorometry (Walz MAXI Imaging PAM) after a 30-min dark adaptation period the day before the thermal stress test began and then every other day for 4 weeks until coral fragments were visibly bleached. Mortality was recorded daily based on visual inspection. The coral sample was outlined in each image to create an image mask. Image stacks were converted to matrices in R (version 4.4.1) and F m and F 0 values were obtained from the respective matrix/image layer within the masked area (region of interest, ROI). F m values less than 0.048 were set to 0 (per manufacturer documentation). F v / F m was calculated using the formula F v / F m = ( F m − F 0 ) / F m for each pixel within the ROI. Infinite F v / F m values were removed and the mean F v / F m was then calculated for the entire ROI. Samples that died during the four weeks of heat exposure were removed from the experiment and their F v / F m values set to zero in remaining time points to represent the complete collapse of symbiosis. Relative F v / F m values were obtained for each time point by dividing by the initial value (the mean F v / F m value from two pre-exposure time points). Reproductive assay Pocillopora acuta release brooded larvae just after sunrise following the full moon in Hawai‘i ( Schmidt-Roach et al. 2012 ; Huffmyer et al. 2021 ). Several of the largest colonies from each ploidy were monitored for larval release in August, September, and October 2024 and again in June 2025, for 3 to 4 days directly after the full moon. Colonies were retrieved from the in situ nursery the day of, or 1 day after, the full moon and placed in individual plastic bowls (1.5 L) to collect larvae. Each bowl had flow-through seawater emptying into 123 μm mesh larval traps, which ensured separate larval collection from each known maternal colony. The number of larvae released was counted each morning. Larval release was also monitored in May 2025 for 3 days after the new moon to determine if there was any evidence that colonies released larvae at different stages of the lunar cycle. Statistical analyses All analyses were conducted in R (version 4.3.0). We used a Fisher's exact test to determine if the relative abundance of colonies and genotypes of each ploidy differed by year. We used linear mixed models in the R package glmmTMB ( Brooks et al. 2017 ) to evaluate the impact of ploidy on each metric (surface area, volume, buoyant weight) during the growth assay, including a random effect of colony ID nested with genotype to account for repeated measures and non-independence of clonal replicates. Using the same framework but excluding random effects and time, we evaluated baseline initial F v / F m , final fractal dimension, skeletal density (volume/dry weight), and the interaction between growth ((surface area in October − surface area in July)/surface area in July) and survival (days) in the heat stress assay. To evaluate responses during the heat stress experiment, we calculated experimental degree heating weeks (eDHW ( Leggat et al. 2022 )) using 27.4 °C as the maximum monthly mean for Kāneʻohe Bay ( Dilworth et al. 2021 ). Using eDHW rather than time, we explored the influence of ploidy on survivorship using a Coxph model implemented in the package coxme ( Therneau 2024 ) with random effect of colony ID nested within genotype effects to account for repeated measures (frailty) and non-independence of clonal replicates. To analyze symbiont efficiency, eDHWs accumulated at each time point were used as the dose variable in fitting dose response curves to relative F v / F m values using a four-parameter logistic function in the R package drda ( Malyutina et al. 2023 ) with the alpha set to 1, delta set to −1, and the eta and phi parameters allowed to fluctuate freely. Effective dose measurements (ED60) were calculated as the amount of eDHW required for a 40% decline (reduction to 60% of original value) in relative F v / F m . We chose the ED60 threshold (rather than ED50) because some samples did not fall below the 50% threshold within the bounds of our experiment. We used a mixed effects model implemented with the R package lme4 to model the relationship between ED60 values and ploidy, with genotype included as a random effect to account for non-independence of clonal replicates within some genotypes. Results Sequencing The number of reads per library for the 384 libraries sequenced on the NovaSeq X platform (1,591,421 ± 214,632 [mean ± standard deviation] reads/library) was approximately three times greater than the number of reads per library generated for the five libraries sequenced on the MiSeq platform (445,905 ± 132,077 reads/library). Two libraries failed to sequence on the NovaSeq X platform. At a depth of 10×, libraries sequenced on the NovaSeq X platform had a breadth of genome coverage of 7.4% ± 1.84 (mean ± standard deviation) and the libraries sequenced on the MiSeq platform had a breadth of genome coverage of 0.54% ± 0.34. The VCF file produced by ANGSD of the 387 libraries that were used to identify clonal lineages contained 1,006,151 sites. Ploidy identification Using the four parameter settings to identify ploidy in nQuire, we found that for triploids, 174/214 libraries were identified as triploid in 4/4 parameter settings and 26/214 were identified as triploid in 3/4 parameter settings. This resulted in a 93.5% identification call for triploids. For diploids, 146/167 libraries were identified as diploid in 4/4 parameter settings and 12/167 were identified as diploid in 3/4 parameter settings, resulting in a 94.6% identification call for diploids. For 23 libraries (6.3%), nQuire could not identify a majority ploidy call: 14 triploid and 9 diploid. However, all of these libraries were members of a clonal lineage and could thus be assigned a ploidy based on the ploidy of the other members of their respective clonal lineage. The five libraries sequenced on the MiSeq could not be confidently identified as diploid or triploid due to their much lower sequencing depth compared with the libraries sequenced on the NovaSeq. While no clonal lineage contained individuals of mixed ploidy, i.e. all members were either diploid or triploid, the use of flow cytometry or karyotyping would provide an independent validation method for ploidy identification. Genotype and ploidy abundance through time There were always more unique diploid genotypes than triploid genotypes in each of the sampled years ( Fig. 3a ), except in 2018 when Stephens et al. (2023) sampled, but differences between years were not significant. Triploid colonies were more abundant than diploid colonies in Kāne‘ohe Bay in 2018 and 2024 in comparison to 2007 ( P &lt; 0.001 for both comparisons) and 2010 ( P = 0.02 and P = 0.01; respectively; Fig. 3b ). There was no difference in the proportion of colonies sampled of a given ploidy between 2007 and 2010 ( P = 1) or between 2018 and 2024 ( P = 1). Species identification Of the 387 colonies sequenced, 379 hosted the P. acuta mitochondrial genome and 8 hosted the P. damicornis mitochondrial genome ( Fig. 2 ). Four of the colonies hosting the P. damicornis mitochondrial genome formed a clonal lineage (genotype 25, diploid) consisting of two colonies collected in 2007 from Kāne‘ohe Bay by Gorospe and Karl (2013) , one colony collected from the NWHI in 2010, and one colony collected in 2024 from Kāne‘ohe Bay. The other four colonies hosting the P. damicornis mitochondrial genome were each unique genotypes, genotypes 33 to 36, and consisted of three colonies collected from the NWHI in 2010 and one colony from Kāne‘ohe Bay in 2016. Assessment of population structure, shared ancestry, and genetic distance After filtering the mixed ploidy VCF generated by GATK, 1,476 SNPs were used as input for DAPC, STRUCTURE, and StAMPP analyses. Generally, the results from these three analyses agreed well with each other ( Fig. 4 ). DAPC found shared genetic clusters between diploids and triploids but no shared genetic clusters between P. damicornis genotypes of unknown ploidy with diploids or triploids ( Fig. 4a ). The optimal number of ancestral populations following Evanno's ΔK was K = 8 for STRUCTURE ( Fig. 4b ), which indicated shared ancestry between several diploid, triploid, and P. damicornis genotypes. PCoA of Nei's D genetic distance indicated some differentiation between most diploids and triploids. In the PCoA1 vs. PCoA2 contrast, however, there was greater differentiation between individuals of P. damicornis of unknown ploidy than between ploidies of P. acuta , but the PCoA1 vs. PCoA3 contrast then resolved P. damicornis as distinct from P. acuta ( Fig. 4d ). In the neighbor-joining network constructed from Nei's D genetic distance, P. damicornis of unknown ploidy recovered reciprocally monophyletic while there was no clear separation between diploid and triploid P. acuta ( Fig. 4e ). Among other Pocillopora species, this region is 673 bp long and produces a single band ( Cohn et al. 2026 ). In diploid P. acuta , 77% of unique genotypes produced a triple band pattern with bands at approximately 600, 850, and 900 bp (banding pattern 2; Fig. 4c ), but 18% of diploid genotypes produced a single band at approximately 600 bp (banding pattern 1; Fig. 4c ). In triploids, 86% of unique genotypes produced a single band at approximately 600 bp; 7% produced a triple band at 600, 850, and 900 bp (banding pattern 2); and 7% produced a quintuple banding pattern with bands at 500, 550, 650, 850, and 900 bp (banding pattern 5). Among the four genotypes of P. damicornis , 50% produced a band at approximately 500, 550, and 650 bp (banding pattern 3) and 50% produced a quintuple banding pattern with bands at 500, 550, 650, 850, and 900 bp (banding pattern 5). Despite observed differences in histone banding patterns, there were no fixed nucleotide differences that identified ploidy in P. acuta , nor differences between colonies hosting the P. acuta or P. damicornis mitochondrial genomes. Impacts of ploidy on baseline growth, structure, and photosynthesis Triploids grew significantly faster than diploid corals over the 112-day growth assay ( Fig. 5a to c ). On average, percent change in surface area was +70.6% for triploids and +53.8% for diploids ( P &lt; 0.001; Fig. 5a ), percent change in volume was +59.4% for triploids and +49.0% for diploids ( P &lt; 0.001; Fig. 5b ), and percent change in dry weight was +80.4% for triploids and +65.1% for diploids ( P &lt; 0.001; Fig. 5c ). After 112 days of growth, there was no difference in fractal dimension between ploidies ( P = 0.361; Fig. 5d ), nor was there a significant difference in skeletal density between ploidies ( P = 0.687; Fig. 5e ). Before heat exposure, there was no significant difference in symbiont photosynthetic efficiency between ploidies ( P = 0.31). Response to thermal stress While stress testing 225 colony fragments from 89 diploid and 136 triploid colonies (24 unique genotypes: 14 diploid and 10 triploid; Figure S2 ), triploids experienced significantly faster mortality than diploids over the 73-day thermal ramp ( P = 0.008; Fig. 5f and g ). Triploids experienced mean 50% mortality at 15 eDHW, whereas diploids experienced mean 50% mortality at 22.5 eDHW, a difference of 7.5 eDHW. Using 219 colonies from which we had both growth rate data and survival in the heat stress treatment, we found that there was a positive but non-significant relationship between growth rate and thermal tolerance in diploids but a positive significant relationship in triploids ( P = 0.392 and P = 0.046, respectively; Fig. 5h ). ED60, the eDHW required for a 40% decline in relative F v / F m , did not differ between ploidies ( P = 0.281; Figure S1 ). Discussion We found that triploidy in this population did not originate from the thermal stress associated with the 2014/15 bleaching events because 57% of the triploid genotypes identified in the 2024 sampling were also present prior to these events in 2007. Triploid P. acuta colonies increased in Kāne‘ohe Bay from 2007 (45% of colonies sampled) to 2018 (63% of colonies sampled) and have remained the dominant ploidy in the bay since (61% of colonies sampled in 2024). However, triploids are significantly more thermally sensitive than diploids, so if the increase in triploid colonies over time is due to selection, it is likely to occur indirectly via other traits associated with ploidy rather than direct selection on thermal tolerance. Diploid and triploid genotypes are highly clonal and both ploidies release larvae after the full moon. Under ambient conditions, triploids grew significantly faster than diploids, which might allow them to reach reproductive maturity faster than diploids. It is not known if triploid colonies of P. acuta are capable of sexual reproduction, or even the frequency of sexual reproduction in diploids, or if triploids only reproduce via parthenogenesis, like triploids of many other species ( Otto and Whitton 2000 ; Taylor and Foighilà 2000 ; Lv et al. 2025 ). However, genomic analyses indicate that sexual recombination between sister species P. acuta and P. damicornis likely led to the formation of allotriploids in Hawai‘i. The increase of triploid colonies in Kāne‘ohe Bay was not driven by an increase in the number of new unique triploid genotypes or a decrease in the number of unique diploid genotypes, rather this change was a result of an increase or decrease in the number of ramets within certain genotypes. Four triploid genotypes experienced a greater than 2% increase in the number of colonies sampled from 2007 to 2024. Conversely, three diploid genotypes experienced a greater than 2% decrease in the number of colonies sampled, with genotype 18 experiencing a 13% decline. Triploids experienced 50% mortality 7.5 eDHW before diploids, despite no difference in symbiont photochemical efficiency between ploidies before and under heat stress ( P. acuta hosts only Cladocopium pacificum in Kāne‘ohe Bay ( Núñez-Pons et al. 2023 )). This finding suggests that metabolic differences between P. acuta ploidies likely exist, which might be driven in part by differences in cell size. Polyploids typically have larger cell sizes than diploids, which may increase nutrient storage but also the risk of structural instability ( Pacey et al. 2022 ). Larger cell size may also increase the length of time for signals to travel, impacting stress responses, and increased genomic load can lead to longer DNA replication times and reduced metabolism ( te Beest et al. 2012 ). In frogs, polyploidy results in organ allometry and under increased temperatures, higher energy expenditure ( Chen et al. 2025 ). There does appear to be a metabolic tradeoff in P. acuta because triploids have a faster growth rate under benign conditions but are metabolically more compromised than diploids under heat stress. Changes in gene expression may not increase linearly with ploidy, such that certain genes are more sensitive to ploidy changes than others ( Yahya et al. 2022 ). Interestingly, we found that, under ambient conditions, the triploids that grew faster were also those that survived significantly longer in the heat stress treatment. Furthermore, the distinct histone banding patterns between most diploid and most triploid P. acuta colonies, and colonies hosting the P. damicornis mitochondrial genome, indicates that histone modification is likely the result of ploidy differences, as is often the case in other polyploids, and may have unique impacts on transcriptional regulation ( Song and Chen 2015 ). The factors contributing to an increase in triploid colonies over time are likely to be complex and may include dynamics such as increased energy resulting from size-dependent photosynthetic production ( Milosavljevic et al. 2024 ), epigenetic remodeling differences ( Song and Chen 2015 ), a longer reproductive lifespan, increased heterotrophic capacity, increased larval and/or juvenile survival, and/or a reduction in energy diverted to sexual reproduction ( Stelzer 2015 ). In agreement with previous studies, we found that P. acuta is highly clonal in Kāne‘ohe Bay ( Gorospe and Karl 2013 ; Gorospe et al. 2015 ; Stephens et al. 2023 ), with only two genotypes (5.6%) presenting no evidence of clonal ramets across years. While triploids did release larvae, fewer triploid colonies did so in comparison to diploids over the 4 months observed: 46% of triploid colonies vs. 89% of diploid colonies. However, we only monitored a small portion of the population, and even though larvae were released, larval settlement is not guaranteed. A more comprehensive examination of reproductive trends in this population should be undertaken to determine the relationships between fecundity, sexuality of larvae generated (asexual vs. sexually derived), and settlement success, with ploidy. Reduced clonality of P. acuta in the Philippines is associated with higher energy environments ( Torres et al. 2020 ); on the Great Barrier Reef, clonality in P. acuta was only 5.7% ( Torda et al. 2013a ); and in Thailand, clonality never exceeded 25% across nine sampled locations ( Fiesinger et al. 2023 ). Clonality in Kāne‘ohe Bay is much higher than in these other locations. Across sampling years, we found that 94.6% of genotypes were clonal lineages. We also found that 57% of genotypes were sampled in 2007 and again 17 years later in 2024, indicating that most clonal lineages in Kāne‘ohe Bay have multi-decadal lifespans, likely a consequence of prolific clonal reproduction. Only five genotypes were new to the 2024 sampling that were not detected in 2007, three diploids and two triploids. The capacity to self-fertilize or release clonal larvae very likely contributed to the establishment of early generation triploids in this system as it has in other systems ( Yamauchi et al. 2004 ; Siopa et al. 2020 ). Although P. acuta is highly clonal, there is evidence that this species does generate sexually derived larvae ( Combosch and Vollmer 2013 ; Torda et al. 2013b ; Smith et al. 2019 ), and concurrent larvae and sperm release have been observed in Kāne‘ohe Bay. Schmidt-Roach et al. (2014a) found that, of five colonies monitored in Kāne‘ohe Bay over 4 days following the full moon, all released larvae over multiple days but only three released sperm, with sperm release occurring on just a single day. Interestingly, Jokiel et al. (1985) found that two morphologies of (what was then identified as) P. damicornis were present in Kāne‘ohe Bay, Type Y and Type B corals, with Type Y corals releasing larvae on the full moon and Type B corals releasing larvae on the new moon; these types could correspond to what are now identified as P. acuta and P. damicornis . While P. damicornis is very rare in Kāne‘ohe Bay today ( Johnston et al. 2018 and this study), this species may have been more abundant 40 years ago. Alternatively, Type Y and B corals could represent diploid and triploid P. acuta . However, because our data suggest that there are not differences in the timing of larval release between ploidies, this scenario is unlikely. Now that genomic sequencing allows for the identification of both species and ploidy, reproduction in these species will hopefully become clearer and reveal patterns that to date have remained so elusive. Asexual pathways in animals can be complex and generate both genetically identical (GI) and non-genetically identical (NGI) offspring. In animals, these pathways can involve mitotic parthenogenesis (somatic budding or apomixis—both GI), meiotic parthenogenesis (fusion of egg or polar body cells generated from same individual—GI and NGI), and gynogenesis (sperm presence triggers egg development but no fusion occurs—GI) ( Neiman et al. 2014 ). How asexual reproduction proceeds in P. acuta is unknown, but many asexual plant and animal taxa are products of hybridization, and the common association between obligate asexuality and hybridization suggests that hybridization plays a fundamental role in transitions to obligate asexual reproduction ( Avise 2008 ; Choleva et al. 2012 ; Van Drunen and Husband 2019 ). Following the evolution of asexuality, polyploidization might then occur via the fusion or fertilization of unreduced egg cells ( White 1973 ). The complex ancestry observed between P. acuta diploids and triploids and P. damicornis in our DAPC, STRUCTURE, and Nei's D genetic distance analyses indicates that triploids may be the result of allopolyploidy via hybridization between P. acuta and P. damicornis. The finding of a diploid genotype (genotype 25) hosting the P. damicornis mitochondrial genome, but which does not form a monophyletic clade with the other P. damicornis genotypes, provides further evidence of gene flow between these two species. However, we cannot determine whether hybridization is a contemporary process yielding mixed ploidy in the same generation, or if historical hybridization between P. acuta and P. damicornis produced asexual diploids with triploids originating more recently from the fertilization of diploid eggs. Hybridization resulting in mixed ploidy populations has been frequently observed in plants ( Kolář et al. 2017 ) but also exists in animals, including some vertebrates ( Choleva et al. 2012 ; Choleva and Janko 2013 ; He et al. 2013 ). The contemporary extreme rarity of P. damicornis in Kāne‘ohe Bay suggests that present day interspecific fertilization of gametes is not likely. Given that paternal inheritance of mitochondrial genomes is very rare ( Munasinghe and Ågren 2023 ), hybridization between P. acuta and P. damicornis likely proceeded from the fertilization of haploid or diploid eggs of P. acuta with haploid sperm of P. damicornis , hence why all triploid colonies carry the mitochondrial genome from P. acuta . However, there may be rare instances of P. damicornis egg fertilization with the sperm of P. acuta because we did find one diploid P. acuta genotype that carried the mitochondrial genome of P. damicornis (genotype 25). Furthermore, the ploidy status of P. damicornis is currently not known but will be paramount to further understanding the cause and consequences of polyploidy in this system. Alternatively, following triploidization, diploidization may subsequently occur in some P. acuta lineages. Nothing is known about the diploidization process in corals, but in angiosperms, this process can occur in as few as 10 to 40 generations, while in Sequoia , this process likely takes millions of years ( Li et al. 2021 ). Whether this strategy promotes long-term persistence of triploid P. acuta colonies will depend on multiple factors including, but not limited to, the ability of triploids to recruit genetic variation from their diploid relatives, their propensity of asexual reproduction coupled with occasional sexual reproduction, and their rate of ecological diversification ( Levin and Soltis 2018 ). The geographic extent of triploid formation of P. acuta is worth further investigating. Are triploids only formed in the Hawaiian Islands, or do they also occur in other locations where P. acuta and P. damicornis are sympatric such as Australia, New Caledonia, and the South China Sea ( Oury et al. 2023 )? If not, what are the reproductive, ecological, and/or genomic barriers that prevent hybridization and triploid formation in these locations? Within both the currently recognized P. acuta and P. damicornis species, there is extensive genetic subdivision, indicating that these species are likely species complexes ( Oury et al. 2023 ). Despite multiple species revisions of Pocillopora ( Pinzón et al. 2013 ; Schmidt-Roach et al. 2014b ; Gélin et al. 2017 ; Johnston et al. 2017 ; Oury et al. 2023 ), including new species descriptions ( Johnston and Burgess 2023 ; Oury et al. 2025 ), there continues to be unexpected findings in this genus. The recent discovery of a mixed ploidy assemblage (diploids and triploids) in the beadlet anemone Actinia equina , with triploids resulting from hybridization between Actinia species ( Wilding et al. 2025 ), indicates that the polyploidy observed in P. acuta may not be unique in anthozoans. Furthermore, given the evidence for hybridization in the coral genus Acropora ( Vollmer and Palumbi 2002 ; Bridge et al. 2023 ; Furukawa et al. 2025 ; Rassmussen et al. 2025 ) and previous evidence of polyploidy in Acropora ( Kenyon 1997 ; Mao and Satoh 2019 ), the potential for hybridization as a promoter of polyploidy in anthozoans should be further examined.
