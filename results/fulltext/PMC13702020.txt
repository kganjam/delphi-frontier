1. Introduction The Nile tilapia ( Oreochromis niloticus ) is a globally significant aquaculture species, listed by the Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) as being important for freshwater production. As the world’s third most farmed fish, with an annual yield exceeding 4.5 million tons, it generates considerable economic value worldwide [ 1 , 2 ]. However, parasitic diseases pose a significant threat to the health and sustainability of the industry. These infections can cause mass fry mortality, stunted growth and direct physical damage, thereby reducing market quality and causing substantial economic losses for producers [ 3 ]. Monogeneans are small, hermaphroditic platyhelminths that exhibit strong host specificity and primarily parasitize the gills, skin and fins of teleost fish [ 4 , 5 , 6 ]. They usually attach via chitinous anchors, causing mechanical damage that leads to bleeding, ulceration and tissue necrosis; severe infections can be fatal [ 7 , 8 ]. These parasites feed on epithelial cells, mucus and blood from the host. In tilapia, Cichlidarus cichlidarum (synonym: Gyrodactylus cichlidarum ) and Cichlidogyrus spp. (cichlid gill flukes) are particularly prevalent and harmful [ 9 , 10 ]. Studies confirm that the anchoring and migration of C. cichlidarum significantly damage the skin and gill tissues of hosts, inducing local inflammation. Infected fish exhibit behavioral signs of stress, alongside a significant upregulation of immune-related genes aimed at clearing the parasite [ 10 ]. The skin mucus of fish constitutes the primary interface with the aquatic environment, providing a multifaceted defense through physical, biochemical, and physiological mechanisms [ 11 ]. Fish cutaneous mucus is secreted by mucous cells extensively distributed in the epidermal tissue and on the gill surface. Its primary components are mucins and fibrous materials. Upon release from mucous cells, these secretions hydrate and expand upon contact with water, forming a gelatinous matrix that coats the free surface of the epithelium and constitutes a natural physical and immunological barrier. This gelatinous matrix, composed of water and mucins, forms a physical barrier that hinders pathogen adhesion, while its continuous secretion and shedding help to clear microbes from the epithelial surface [ 11 ]. Fish epidermal mucus contains numerous innate immune components, such as glycoproteins, lysozyme, complement proteins, lectins, ApeC-containing proteins, C-reactive proteins, flavoenzymes, proteolytic enzymes, and antimicrobial peptides, as well as immunoglobulins [ 12 , 13 ]. Studies have demonstrated that the innate immune factors in fish skin mucus can effectively kill parasites. For instance, the skin mucus of Siganus oramin has been shown to possess significant lethal effects against Cryptocaryon irritans and Ichthyophthirius multifiliis , with treated parasites exhibiting ciliate shedding, swelling, rupture of the outer membrane, and leakage of internal contents [ 14 ]. Similarly, the skin mucus of Amphiprion clarkii demonstrated strong antiparasitic activity against C. irritans theronts in a dose- and time-dependent manner. Microscopic observation revealed that treated theronts rapidly ceased swimming, followed by cilia shedding, cell rounding, membrane rupture, and content leakage [ 15 ]. Patel reported that the epidermal mucus of Clarias batrachus exhibits antipathogenic activity, with its mucus extract demonstrating significant inhibitory effects against Escherichia coli and Staphylococcus aureus [ 16 ]. To counteract host surface defense mechanisms, parasites employ specific molecules within their excretory/secretory proteins (ESPs) to achieve bidirectional regulation of host physiological and immune responses, thereby enhancing their own survival [ 17 ]. For example, certain parasite-derived adhesion proteins bind specifically to host epithelial receptors, facilitating attachment [ 18 ], whereas other secreted factors degrade defensive barriers in the host to facilitate tissue invasion and nutrient acquisition. Secretory proteins from the labial glands of salmon lice ( Lepeophtheirus salmonis ) interfere with the host’s inflammatory responses and inhibit coagulation, thereby promoting the establishment of the parasite [ 19 ]. The surface mucus of fish contains various humoral immune components, including complement, lysozyme, and antibodies, which can directly attack the parasite’s tegument [ 20 ]. In response to this immune pressure, monogeneans utilize their ESPs, including stefins [ 21 ], serpins [ 22 ], and kunitz domain proteins [ 23 ] to counteract host defenses. These inhibitors are considered key tools employed by parasites to evade host immunity by modulating host immune signaling pathways, thereby facilitating survival at the infection site. In vitro studies have demonstrated that a recombinant kunitz-type protein from Eudiplozoon nipponicum (rEnKT1) compromises both hemostasis and complement activation through the inhibition of Factor Xa, plasmin, and plasma kallikrein, consequently promoting the survival of E. nipponicum [ 17 , 23 ]. We hypothesize that the protein composition of fish skin mucus varies across developmental stages and in response to parasitic challenges, reflecting distinct modes of mucosal immune responses and, to some extent, elucidating the mechanisms underlying parasite–host mucosal interactions. In this study, we employed parasitic challenge experiments coupled with liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC-MS) to characterize the proteomic profiles of skin mucus in juvenile and adult O. niloticus , as well as in O. niloticus following C. cichlidarum infection. Our objective is to provide foundational data elucidating the developmental characteristics of fish mucosal immunity and the mechanisms underlying parasite–host mucosal interactions, thereby identifying novel targets for the development of biomarkers and vaccines against fish parasitic diseases. 2. Materials and Methods 2.1. Fish and Parasites All the experimental tilapias were purchased from Tilapia Breeding Center of Guangdong province (Guangzhou, China). The fish were acclimated in a laboratory recirculating aquaculture system at a temperature of 25 °C. During the acclimation period, they were examined to ensure they were free of parasite infection. Ultraviolet (UV) light was used to sterilize the circulating water system to avoid bacterial infection, and the filter material was cleaned periodically. Cichlidarus cichlidarum for infection experiments was taken from a laboratory-maintained population, which had previously been established on the host O. niloticus [ 10 , 23 ]. The monogenean parasite C. cichlidarum was propagated on tilapia (body length: 3–4 cm) and subsequently collected for experimental infection. Tilapias (body weight: 1.8 ± 0.6 g; body length: 3.7 ± 0.5 cm, values are shown as means ± standard deviation) for parasite maintenance were cultured in the indoor experimental fish tank with recirculating freshwater at 25 °C without feeding. Subsequently, they were injected with 50–100 µL Dexamethasone Sodium Phosphate Injection-BL-DM11 (BOLAI YAOYE, Jiujiang, China) to allow a higher intensity of C. cichlidarum when they were infected with 5–10 individuals of C. cichlidarum . The parasite burden of fish was individually examined under a stereomicroscope (SZX16, Olympus, Tokyo, Japan)after 10 s anesthesia in solution of 500 mg/l ethyl 3-aminobenzoate methanesulfonate salt (A5040, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA). Subsequently, fins infested with C. cichlidarum were excised to harvest C. cichlidarum for the infection experiment [ 24 ]. Prior to the infection experiment, the healthy tilapias were examined under a stereomicroscope to confirm the absence of parasites and physical abnormalities. Five selected fish (body weight: 1.0 ± 0.6 g; body length: 3.7 ± 0.6 cm) were individually housed in 350 mL plastic containers for a 72 h acclimatization period, during which the water was changed daily. For infection, each fish was exposed to fin fragments carrying C. cichlidarum at a dose of approximately 100 parasites per fish. After 24 h post-infection, the fish were anesthetized with ethyl 3-aminobenzoate methanesulfonate and examined under a stereomicroscope to determine the parasite load. The intensity of parasites per fish were 52, 58, 52, 40, and 75, respectively. Subsequently, skin mucus was collected from these infected fish to serve as samples for the infected group. 2.2. Mucus Collection Mucus samples were collected from five healthy juvenile tilapias (body weight: 1.0 ± 0.6 g; body length: 3.7 ± 0.6 cm), five infected tilapias (body weight: 1.0 ± 0.6 g; body length: 3.7 ± 0.6 cm), and three adult tilapias (body weight: 365.6 ± 62.6 g; body length: 27.0 ± 1.4 cm). The body surface of each fish was wiped with sterile absorbent cotton (HQH776, LEAGUE, Jiaozuo, China), which was then immersed in a 1.5 mL centrifuge tube containing Phosphate-Buffered Saline (PBS; pH 7.2–7.4, 8 mM Na 2 HPO 4 , 2 mM NaH 2 PO 4 , and 137 mM NaCl). Mucus samples from each group were pooled into one tube per group. After brief vortexing, the cotton was compacted using forceps, and the tubes were centrifuged at 8000× g for 5 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was then transferred to a new tube. Total protein concentration was determined using the Bicinchoninic Acid Assay (BCA), strictly following the manufacturer’s instructions for the BCA Protein Assay Kit (T9300A, Takara, Dalian, China). Subsequently, proteins were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE). Due to the limited volume of skin mucus secreted by individual juvenile and infected tilapia, which yielded insufficient protein for individual LC-MS/MS analysis, samples from individuals within each experimental group were pooled. Prior to pooling, individual samples were assessed via SDS-PAGE to ensure biological consistency and similar protein profiles across individuals within the same group. Each pooled sample contained approximately 100 μg of total protein. 2.3. Mass Spectrometry The gel fragments containing target proteins (approximately 1 mm 3 ) were destained until colorless, washed with ultrapure water, dehydrated with acetonitrile until they turned white, and air-dried. The reduction process entailed the addition of 10 mM dithiothreitol (DTT) (incubated at 56 °C for 1 h), followed by alkylation with 55 mM IAM in the dark for 45 min. Following a series of washes and dehydrations, the gel pieces were then rehydrated in a trypsin solution (0.01 μg/μL in 25 mM ammonium bicarbonate (NH 4 HCO 3 )) on ice for a period of 30 min. This was followed by the addition of buffer in order to cover the pieces, and the incubation of the samples was carried out overnight at 37 °C. Peptides were extracted with acetonitrile, and the resulting upper layer was collected by means of centrifugation and lyophilization. The lyophilized peptide samples were then reconstituted in mobile phase A (2% acetonitrile (ACN), 0.1% formic acid (FA)), clarified by centrifugation, and separated using an UltiMate 3000 ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography (UHPLC) system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, San Jose, CA, USA). The samples were initially loaded onto a trap column for the purpose of enrichment and desalting. Thereafter, separation occurred on a self-packed C18 column (75 μm inner diameter, 3 μm particle size, 25 cm length) at a flow rate of 300 nL/min with an effective gradient: 0–5 min, 5% B; 5–45 min, 5–25% B; 45–50 min, 25–35% B; 50–52 min, 35–80% B; 52–54 min, 80% B; 54–60 min, 5% B (B: 98% ACN, 0.1% FA). The LC system was connected to a Q-Exactive HF X (Thermo Fisher Scientific, San Jose, CA, USA) mass spectrometer via a nano electrospray ionization (ESI) source (voltage: 1.9 kV). The data were acquired in data-dependent acquisition (DDA) mode. Full-scan MS spectra (350–1500 m/z) were acquired at a resolution of 60,000, followed by higher-energy collisional dissociation (HCD) fragmentation of the top 30 precursor ions (charge 2+–6+, intensity threshold &gt; 10,000) with MS/MS resolution of 15,000. The dynamic exclusion parameter was set to 30 s, and the automatic gain control (AGC) targets were set to 3E6 for MS and 1E5 for tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS). 2.4. Protein Identification The protein identification workflow began with the analysis of tandem mass spectrometry data using Mascot v2.3.02. The raw data were converted into peak lists and then searched against the protein database restricted to Oreochromis niloticus (GCA_001858045.3, https://asia.ensembl.org/Oreochromis_niloticus/Info/Index ; accessed on 1 July 2026), and the data from infected tilapia group was also searched in the C. cichlidarum database (National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) BioProject: PRJNA1229812). The search results were then filtered and subjected to quality control based on several established metrics ( Table 1 ). All results were processed using Percolator [ 25 ] and filtered at a 1% spectrum-level false discovery rate (FDR; PSM-level FDR ≤ 0.01). The unique spectrum distribution, representing the non-redundant spectra from the identified peptides, was then assessed in order to evaluate the depth of the data acquisition. Identification confidence was gauged by the number of unique peptides—sequences that map uniquely to a single protein—with multiple unique peptides per protein increasing reliability. Protein sequence coverage, indicating the percentage of the protein sequence covered by identified peptides, reflected the comprehensiveness of the identification process. For quantitative analysis, peptide extracted ion chromatograms (XICs) were generated with BGI’s in-house software, and peptide peak areas were calculated. Protein abundances were then estimated using the intensity-based absolute quantification (iBAQ) algorithm, which sums the peptide peak areas for each protein and divides them by the number of theoretically observable peptides for that protein, yielding iBAQ values [ 26 ]. Finally, the filtered protein list was functionally annotated using gene ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analyses. 3. Results 3.1. The Proteins in Tilapia Mucus Identified by MS A total of 2771, 2276, and 2858 proteins were identified in the juvenile, adult, and infected groups, corresponding to 16,279 (out of 65,490 total spectra), 11,158 (out of 65,715 total spectra), and 15,411 (out of 66,280 total spectra) matched spectra, respectively ( Table 2 ). The quality metrics demonstrated consistency across the groups. The distribution of unique spectra was comparable among groups, with a median of 2 and a mode of 1. The average protein sequence coverage was 5% for all groups. The ratios of multi-peptide to single-peptide identifications were also comparable, with values of 1.56 in juveniles (1688 out of 2771 proteins), 1.34 in adults (1305 out of 2276 proteins), and 1.47 in infected fish (1699 out of 2858 proteins). 3.2. Proteomic Profiling of Skin Mucus in Tilapia A total of 7905 proteins were identified in the skin mucus of tilapia, comprising 2771 in juveniles, 2276 in adults, and 2858 in infected tilapia ( Figure 1 A, Table S1 ). Among all the identified proteins, 785 were specifically detected in juvenile tilapia, 511 in adult tilapia, and 801 in infected tilapia ( Figure 1 B, Table S2 ). Furthermore, 1192 proteins ( Table S2 ) were found to be common across all three groups ( Figure 1 B), and these proteins were enriched in functional categories including protein synthesis, primary energy metabolism, and antioxidant defense, as revealed by functional enrichment analysis. Analysis by gene ontology (GO) of the 1192 common proteins revealed that abundant proteins are associated with GO terms related to protein biosynthetic process, translation, ribonucleoprotein complex, intracellular protein-containing complex, structural molecule activity and oxidoreductase activity ( Figure 1 C). KEGG pathway analysis demonstrated that the common proteins were enriched in pathways such as carbon metabolism, Tight junction, Focal adhesion, MAPK signaling, NOD-like receptor signaling and C-type lectin receptor signaling pathways ( Figure 1 D). Among the common proteins, those with higher abundance include MHC class II antigen alpha, cystatin, serpin, transmembrane p24 trafficking protein, galectin, peroxiredoxin, C-type lectin, thioredoxin, complement C3, fibrinogen C-terminal domain-containing protein, endoplasmic reticulum protein 44 and prolyl endopeptidase, which contribute to promoting inflammation, counteracting oxidative stress, and safe-guarding immune cells from oxidative damage ( Table 3 ). 3.3. Compositional Differences of the Skin Mucus Proteome in Tilapia Across Developmental Stages A comparative analysis was performed on the proteins identified from juvenile and adult tilapia groups: 1328 proteins were found to be unique to the juvenile group, whereas 833 proteins were uniquely detected in the adult group ( Table S3 ). GO enrichment analysis demonstrated that the 1328 proteins specific to juvenile tilapia were primarily involved in the organophosphate metabolic process, actin cytoskeleton organization, organelle envelope, mitochondrial envelope, oxidoreductase activity, cytoskeletal protein binding and hydrolase activity ( Figure 2 A); and KEGG pathway enrichment analysis revealed these proteins were overrepresented in Staphylococcus aureus infection, Phagosome, cell adhesion molecule (CAM) interaction, complement and coagulation cascades, and PI3K-Akt signaling pathways ( Figure 2 B). In comparison, the 833 proteins specific to adult tilapia were predominantly enriched in macromolecule catabolic process, RNA processing, intracellular protein-containing complex, ribonucleoprotein complex, cytoskeletal protein binding and actin binding ( Figure 2 C); and KEGG pathway enrichment analysis revealed that enrichment of these proteins in MAPK signaling, Tight junction, Focal adhesion, NOD-like receptor signaling, Chemokine signaling, Leukocyte transendothelial migration and Toll-like receptor signaling pathways ( Figure 2 D). 3.4. C. cichlidarum Induced Reprogramming of the Skin Mucus Proteome in Juvenile Tilapia A comparative analysis was performed on the proteins identified from juvenile and infected tilapia groups. 1123 proteins ( Table S4 ) were uniquely detected in the infected group, and 220 proteins ( Table S5 ) exhibited markedly increased abundance in the infected group compared with the juvenile group, as determined by iBAQ-based quantification (log 2 fold change &gt; 1). The unique and upregulated proteins mainly include the immunoglobulin M (IgM) variable region of the heavy-chain (VH) region, complement C3a, complement C9, ubiquitin-conjugating enzyme, integrin α2, interferon-induced protein 35, calreticulin, apoptotic protease-activating factor 1, proteasome subunit β, vacuolar Protein Sorting 35 (VPS35) endosomal protein-sorting factor, paxillin, fibronectin type-III, MHC II β, granzyme K, interstitial collagenase, selectin E, metalloendopeptidase, lectin mannose-binding 2, sestrin 2, C-type lectin, cathepsin B, gelsolin, keratin and serpin ( Table 4 ). These proteins are mainly involved in RNA biosynthetic process, protein biosynthetic process, ribonucleoprotein complex, adenosine triphosphate (ATP)-dependent activity, catalytic activity and structural molecule GO terms ( Figure 3 A); and in phagosome, NOD-like receptor signaling, C-type lectin receptor signaling, Focal adhesion, PI3K-Akt signaling, carbon metabolism, MAPK signaling, chemokine signaling, and complement and coagulation cascades pathways ( Figure 3 B). 3.5. C. cichlidarum-Derived Proteins in the Skin Mucus of Infected Tilapia Database-specific protein identification revealed that 64 proteins from the skin mucus of infected tilapia were uniquely assigned to the C. cichlidarum database ( Table S6 ). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis revealed these proteins were overrepresented in Tight junction, Rap1 signaling, Focal adhesion, proteasome, Adrenergic signaling in cardiomyocytes, Hippo signaling and PI3K-Akt signaling pathways ( Figure 4 ). The enrichment of these pathways reflects the strategies employed by C. cichlidarum to adapt to the host environment and sustain its survival and proliferation. From the 64 C. cichlidarum -derived proteins, 17 proteins are potentially involved in immune evasion or host–parasite interactions, mainly including myosin, actin (cytoplasmic), calmodulin, heat shock cognate protein (HSC70), tubulin, thioredoxin peroxidase, and serine/threonine-protein phosphatase ( Table 5 ). 4. Discussion The skin mucus of teleost fish acts as a primary physicochemical barrier against pathogens and contains various immune components, including immunoglobulins and enzymes. This study utilized comparative proteomics to analyze skin mucus from juvenile, adult and infected tilapias, thereby elucidating the dynamic regulation of this mucosal barrier during host ontogeny and parasitic challenge. The 1192 proteins common to all three experimental groups constitute the core mucosal proteome, underpinning essential physiological functions required for developmental stability and environmental adaptation. Tilapia mucus functions as both a physical barrier and a dynamic biochemical interface, integrating redox homeostasis, innate immune recognition, and antigen presentation, thereby counteracting environmental stressors and pathogen invasion. Enrichment of proteins associated with protein biosynthetic processes, translation, and ribonucleoprotein complexes was observed. This profile suggests a high potential for protein synthesis, which may support the continuous renewal of the mucus layer through the secretion of nascent mucins and antimicrobial peptides [ 90 ]. Additionally, the presence of proteins involved in Tight junction and Focal adhesion pathways suggests the presence of structural components necessary for epithelial barrier integrity. These components are known to maintain tissue mechanical stability and restrict paracellular pathogen penetration in teleosts, potentially preventing secondary bacterial infections during parasitic infestation [ 91 ]. Maintaining the integrity of these junctional structures is particularly critical during parasitic infection, as barrier disruption can facilitate the penetration of secondary pathogens into deeper tissues [ 92 ]. The enrichment of NOD-like receptor and C-type lectin receptor (CLR) signaling pathways, along with the high abundance of C-type lectins, suggests that tilapia mucus possesses the molecular machinery for pathogen recognition. C-type lectins are known to recognize surface carbohydrates and can initiate downstream signaling, contributing to antibacterial defense [ 45 , 93 ]. Similarly, the high abundance of Complement C3 implies the capacity for complement-mediated immune responses, such as opsonization and pathogen lysis, although functional activation status requires further biochemical verification [ 34 ]. Moreover, the detection of MHC class II antigen alpha in skin mucus is not exclusive to tilapia; this molecule was also identified in the skin mucus proteome of Anguilla anguilla infected with Anguillid herpesvirus 1 [ 94 ]. This cross-species presence suggests that fish skin mucus universally possesses the molecular basis for local antigen presentation. While MHC class II is typically expressed by professional antigen-presenting cells, its presence in the mucus may derive from infiltrating immune cells or, potentially, from epithelial cells under specific conditions, thus facilitating an interface with adaptive immunity [ 35 ]. The high abundance of peroxiredoxin and thioredoxin, alongside the enrichment of GO term oxidoreductase activity, suggests the existence of a robust antioxidant defense network within the mucus. This observation is consistent with findings from proteomic and peptidomic studies of epidermal mucus in two Chilean aquaculture species, Seriola lalandi and Seriolella violacea , in which abundant antioxidant enzymes mitigate oxidative stress induced by elevated seawater oxygen tension and pathogen-triggered reactive oxygen species (ROS) bursts [ 95 , 96 ]. By scavenging ROS, these enzymes protect immune cells from oxidative injury and sustain their functional competence [ 44 , 46 ]. This antioxidant mechanism is particularly critical during parasitic infection, as host–parasite interactions often trigger intense inflammatory responses and ROS production; excessive oxidative stress can subsequently cause tissue damage and immunosuppression [ 97 ]. Therefore, the elevated abundance of these proteins may represent an adaptive response to mitigate oxidative injury in tilapia mucus, potentially helping to maintain immune effector functions within a regulated range. Additionally, the high abundance of cystatin and serpin suggests the tight regulation of protease activity by the mucosa. During infection, parasites such as Ichthyophthirius multifiliis secrete proteases that degrade the mucosal barrier, thereby facilitating their invasion into host tissues [ 98 ]. The presence of cystatin and serpin in the mucus suggests a host strategy to inhibit these exogenous proteases and modulate endogenous proteases, thereby potentially preventing tissue damage caused by excessive inflammation. This balance is critical for limiting parasite invasion and mitigating pathological damage. Additionally, the presence of galectin and fibrinogen C-terminal domain-containing proteins (FReDs) contributes to the immune effector repertoire of mucus. Galectin participates in cell adhesion and immune regulation, whereas FReDs are implicated in wound healing and inflammatory responses [ 29 , 42 ]. Proteins showing compositional differences in the skin mucus between juvenile and adult tilapia suggest an ontogenetic shift in the mucosal immune system, moving from metabolically driven nonspecific innate defenses toward receptor-mediated specific immunity, while maintaining barrier integrity. This finding not only elucidates the dynamic remodeling of teleost skin as a primary immune barrier but also offers evidence for the evolutionary conservation of the mucosal immune system across vertebrates [ 99 ]. In juvenile fish, skin mucus proteins were enriched in GO terms associated with organophosphate metabolic processes, mitochondrial envelope components, oxidoreductase activity, and hydrolase activity, as well as in KEGG pathways associated with Staphylococcus aureus infection, Phagosome, complement and coagulation cascades, and the PI3K-Akt signaling pathway. This enrichment pattern implies a dual reliance on high metabolic energy and basal innate immunity to sustain rapid somatic development. The elevated abundance of proteins associated with the mitochondrial envelope and organophosphate metabolism suggests that mucosal epithelial cells in juvenile fish are highly metabolically active, which may support the energy demands for mucus secretion, rapid epithelial turnover, and antimicrobial factor synthesis [ 45 ]. The abundance of oxidoreductases and hydrolases may contribute to a constitutive chemical barrier, potentially providing broad-spectrum and immediate antimicrobial protection by generating ROS or directly degrading pathogen cell walls [ 96 ]. This pathogen recognition-independent defense mechanism may be particularly important for juvenile fish, given their immature immune systems and limited adaptive immune memory. Furthermore, the pronounced enrichment of complement and coagulation cascades highlights the potential importance of humoral immunity in juvenile fish. As a key effector mechanism of innate immunity, the complement system facilitates phagocytic clearance of pathogens via opsonization [ 34 ]. The enrichment of the phagosome pathway further suggests a reliance of juvenile fish on phagocyte-mediated, nonspecific clearance mechanisms against environmental microbial challenges [ 100 ]. Moreover, the enrichment of the PI3K-Akt pathway, beyond its canonical roles in cell survival and proliferation, may be involved in maintaining epithelial integrity and promoting wound healing, which could be advantageous for juvenile fish vulnerable to physical injury [ 45 ]. Additionally, cell adhesion molecule (CAM) interactions may facilitate rapid epithelial cell migration and tissue repair [ 96 ]. In contrast, the skin mucus proteins of adult fish were enriched in GO terms related to macromolecule catabolic, RNA processing, and ribonucleoprotein complexes, as well as in KEGG pathways associated with MAPK signaling, Tight junction, Focal adhesion, NOD-like receptor signaling, chemokine signaling, leukocyte transendothelial migration, and Toll-like receptor signaling. This enrichment pattern suggests a transition toward a more complex and regulated immune strategy. The enrichment of TLR and NOD-like receptor signaling pathways indicates the presence of a mature pattern recognition receptor (PRR) network in the adult skin mucosa, facilitating the recognition of pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) and the initiation of inducible immune responses [ 101 , 102 ]. This receptor-based defense mechanism may allow adult fish to mount more targeted immune responses, potentially limiting tissue damage caused by excessive inflammation [ 103 ]. As a key downstream signaling hub, the MAPK pathway transduces PRR signals to regulate gene expression, thereby coordinating the release of inflammatory mediators [ 104 ]. Furthermore, enrichment of the Tight junction and Focal adhesion pathways suggests that adult fish possess molecular adaptations for maintaining physical barrier integrity. With increasing body size and prolonged environmental exposure, preserving epithelial Tight junction is critical for resisting pathogen invasion and sustaining homeostasis [ 105 ]. The enrichment of chemokine signaling and leukocyte transendothelial migration pathways suggests a robust immune cell recruitment mechanism in the adult skin mucosa, potentially facilitating the mobilization of circulating immune cells, such as lymphocytes and macrophages, to infection sites [ 106 ]. Together with humoral immunity, this cell-mediated immune response may contribute to a more comprehensive defense system in adult fish. Notably, the presence of proteins linked to TCR and BCR signaling pathways in adults suggests a greater involvement of adaptive immune mechanisms at the mucosal surface compared to juveniles. In teleosts, immunoglobulin T (IgT, also known as immunoglobulin Z (IgZ) in some species) serves as the antibody isotype specifically mediating mucosal immunity. Through convergent evolution, IgT has acquired functional properties analogous to those of mammalian immunoglobulin A (IgA), including the regulation of mucosal microbiota homeostasis and defense against pathogen invasion [ 107 ]. IgT is predominantly secreted by mucosal-associated B cells. Enrichment of BCR signaling components suggests that the adult skin mucosa harbors a functional adaptive immune compartment capable of antigen recognition, although further functional assays are needed to confirm antigen-specific activation [ 108 ]. In contrast, the lower enrichment of this pathway in juvenile fish suggests that the immune system may rely primarily on nonspecific innate defenses, including the complement system and phagocytosis, rather than established adaptive memory [ 45 ]. This developmental disparity aligns with the broader paradigm of vertebrate immune ontogeny, wherein innate immunity confers immediate post-hatching protection, whereas adaptive immunity necessitates gene rearrangement, clonal selection, and functional maturation. Proteomic analysis of skin mucus from tilapia following C. cichlidarum infection revealed that 1343 proteins exhibited markedly increased abundance, with enriched pathways involved in inflammatory response, cytoskeletal reorganization, complement activation, and antigen presentation. These changes may collectively contribute to a multi-layered defense network against ectoparasite infestation [ 45 ]. The concurrent upregulation of CLR signaling components and mannose-binding lectin 2 (MBL2) is consistent with the recognition of parasite surface glycans [ 43 , 44 ]. As pattern recognition receptors, C-type lectins specifically recognize and bind carbohydrate structures on pathogen surfaces [ 45 ], which may initiate downstream immune signaling and activate complement and coagulation cascades, as evidenced by the markedly increased abundance of complement components such as C3a and C9 [ 75 , 109 ]. C3a, a potent anaphylatoxin, recruits immune cells to sites of infection, whereas C9, a core component of the membrane attack complex (MAC), has the potential to mediate pathogen lysis [ 78 ]. Furthermore, enrichment of the NOD-like receptor signaling pathway suggests the potential detection of parasite-associated molecular patterns by intracellular sensors, which may lead to inflammasome assembly and pro-inflammatory cytokine release [ 110 ]. Although the skin mucosa is generally considered a primary effector site of innate immunity, the present study detected upregulation of the IgM heavy-chain VH region and MHC class II β-chain in the skin mucus proteome of infected tilapia, indicating active adaptive immune participation at the mucosal surface [ 111 ]. IgM, the predominant immunoglobulin in teleost serum and mucosa, showed upregulation in its VH region, suggestive of a specific antibody response to parasite antigens [ 73 ]. The upregulation of MHC class II molecules and IgM heavy-chain VH regions is associated with potential enhancements in antigen presentation and mucosal antibody responses. These findings imply the involvement of local antigen-presenting cells in processing parasite antigens, a process that potentially contributes to the initiation of adaptive immune memory [ 36 ]. This interplay between innate and adaptive immune components may contribute to host defense against parasitic infections. The increased abundance of proteins associated with PI3K-Akt and MAPK signaling pathways suggests their potential involvement in supporting immune cell functions and metabolic demands during infection [ 112 , 113 ]. Concurrently, elevated expression of the Focal adhesion pathway and its associated proteins—including integrin α2, paxillin, and fibronectin type III—implicates enhanced epidermal cell–extracellular matrix interactions [ 56 , 58 , 63 ]. Such cytoskeletal modifications may contribute to the maintenance of epithelial barrier integrity, wound healing, and immune cell migration [ 114 ]. Given that parasite attachment can damage the epithelium, integrin α2-associated signaling may participate in subsequent tissue repair processes, representing a potential host defense mechanism [ 63 ]. The enrichment of the phagosome pathway, along with the elevated abundance of ubiquitin-conjugating enzymes, proteasome subunit β, and the endosomal sorting factor VPS35, suggests that the host may enhance intracellular degradation mechanisms to clear phagocytosed parasite debris and damaged cellular components [ 60 ]. The ubiquitin–proteasome system tags and degrades abnormal or foreign proteins, thereby generating peptide fragments for antigen presentation [ 62 ]. VPS35, a core component of the retromer complex, mediates endosomal cargo sorting and recycling, ensuring proper localization of immune receptors and signaling molecules [ 55 ]. In addition, upregulation of cathepsin B and granzyme K suggests the potential activation of lysosome-mediated killing and cytotoxic responses, which may contribute to the degradation of parasite structural proteins or the induction of apoptosis in infected cells [ 50 , 70 ]. Detection of interferon-induced protein 35 and Sestrin 2 indicates host responsiveness to oxidative stress and inflammation induced by parasitic infection [ 48 , 79 ]. Sestrin 2, a stress-responsive protein, regulates AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) and mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) signaling to balance energy metabolism and autophagy [ 79 ]. The enrichment of the carbon metabolism pathway suggests that metabolic reprogramming may occur to meet the elevated energy demands of immune cells during infection [ 115 ]. Increased abundance of keratin and serpin suggests a potential strengthening of the epithelial barrier, which may help limit further pathogen invasion and mitigate tissue damage caused by excessive protease activity [ 41 , 67 ]. Thus, skin mucus proteins with markedly increased abundance following infection may serve as candidate biomarkers for the non-invasive diagnosis of gyrodactyliasis. Notably, several C. cichlidarum -derived proteins were identified in the skin mucus of infected tilapia. Based on previous studies in other parasites, these proteins are inferred to be involved in host invasion or immune evasion [ 81 , 116 ]. For example, as a member of the HSP (Heat Shock Protein) 70 family, Schistosoma mansoni HSC (heat shock cognate protein) 70 has been shown in recent studies to interact with immune receptors of the snail host Biomphalaria glabrata , potentially suppressing stress signaling and facilitating immune evasion [ 82 , 83 , 117 ]. This interaction can suppress host stress signaling and immune defenses or dampen pro-inflammatory immune responses, thereby facilitating immune evasion [ 118 ]. Serine/threonine kinases and phosphatases jointly regulate critical cellular processes, including cell-cycle progression, transcription, metabolism, cytoskeletal dynamics, motility, apoptosis, and differentiation [ 85 , 86 ], and they have been demonstrated to be key regulators of parasite growth, development, reproduction, and immune modulation. In Schistosoma japonicum , the catalytic subunit of serine/threonine protein phosphatase 1 (PP1) is essential for worm growth, pairing stability, and reproductive development [ 85 ]. Similarly, serine/threonine protein phosphatase of Eimeria tenella (EtSTP) shows high expression during developmental stages and directly regulates the invasion process [ 119 ]. In Haemonchus contortus , Ehsan et al. identified HcSTP-1 as a key effector molecule mediating host immunosuppression by directly binding to host immune cells, thereby inhibiting their proliferation and antigen presentation [ 120 ]. Calmodulin, a ubiquitous and highly conserved eukaryotic Ca 2+ sensor, orchestrates cell-cycle progression, signal transduction, and motility by triggering the activation of downstream kinases and phosphatases following calcium binding [ 80 , 121 ]. Calcium signaling is a key driver of parasite movement and host cell invasion. For example, the calcium-binding protein TgpCaBP in Toxoplasma gondii is essential for its motility, invasion, and secretion [ 80 ]. In Schistosoma mansoni , the calcium-binding protein SmE16 is highly expressed at the host–parasite interface and responds to calcium signals [ 84 ]. Thioredoxin peroxidase (Prx/TPx) serves as a core antioxidant enzyme that not only scavenges H 2 O 2 through a thioredoxin (Trx)-dependent catalytic cycle but also participates in the regulation of intracellular redox signaling [ 122 ]. At the host–parasite interface, thioredoxin peroxidase functions as a key immune evasion strategy; for instance, Schistosoma japonicum Prx-1 effectively clears ROS generated by both parasite metabolism and host immune cells, thereby providing direct protection against oxidative damage [ 123 ]. Furthermore, these proteins function as dual-purpose immunogens that modulate host immune responses, Toxoplasma gondii peroxiredoxin 3 (TgPrx3) is recognized by the host immune system and stimulates the production of the pro-inflammatory cytokine IL-12, thereby exhibiting both antigenic and immunomodulatory properties [ 124 ]. In summary, these proteins play a crucial role in the host invasion and survival of parasites, and they may serve as promising targets for drug and vaccine development. Constrained by multiple factors, sufficient mucus protein samples were not available to establish biological replicates at the outset of this study. Consequently, only a single pooled sample per treatment group was subjected to LC–MS analysis, representing a major limitation of this study. In the absence of biological replication, we could not determine whether the observed differences in protein abundances across treatment groups were statistically significant. Thus, we restricted our analysis to simple comparisons of total protein abundances among groups. Therefore, our primary focus was on comparing protein composition across treatment groups under consistent analytical sensitivity—specifically, identifying proteins detected universally across all groups versus those detected exclusively in specific groups. It is important to note that these results are influenced by inter-individual variability and entail a non-negligible false-positive rate. Accordingly, the findings should be regarded as preliminary and hypothesis-generating. Guided by these observations, we will undertake rigorous quantitative and functional studies on selected proteins derived from both the host fish skin mucus and the parasite. 5. Conclusions The interaction between parasitic invasion and host immune defense represents a dynamic interplay, with skin mucus serving as the primary interface where this molecular crosstalk initiates. This study reveals that the core proteome of Oreochromis niloticus skin mucus is primarily associated with maintaining physical barrier integrity, responding to oxidative stress, and facilitating pathogen recognition. Compared to juveniles, adult tilapias exhibit a more diverse repertoire of pathogen recognition receptors (PRRs) and proteins linked to adaptive immunity in their skin mucus. Following Cichlidarus cichlidarum infection, there was a marked increase in the abundance of proteins involved in catalytic activity, phagosome formation, C-type lectin receptor signaling, PI3K-Akt signaling, chemokine signaling, and the complement and coagulation cascades. Furthermore, we identified several parasite-derived proteins in the mucus of infected fish, including HSC70, serine/threonine protein phosphatase, modulation, and thioredoxin peroxidase, which may play critical roles in parasite invasion and immune evasion. The identification of these proteins further opens new possibilities for targeted interventions against monogenean parasites, which remain a significant threat to global tilapia production.
