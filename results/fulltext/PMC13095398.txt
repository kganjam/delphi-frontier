Ninety years of research on hybrid incompatibilities Evolutionary biologists have long been fascinated by the immense diversity of species and the mechanisms through which they form [ 1 ]. While many distinct mechanisms contribute to reproductive barriers between emerging species, including pre-fertilization barriers (e.g. [ 2 ]), or sexual and ecological selection on hybrids [ 3 – 6 ], there has been special interest in understanding genetic barriers (see Glossary) that manifest in hybrid populations and prevent successful reproduction, perhaps because these barriers are viewed as “irreversible” when they are sufficiently strong [ 7 ]. Early work in evolutionary biology predicted that genetic barriers between species would arise via distinct genetic changes in each lineage [ 8 – 10 ]. The “Dobzhansky-Müller” (DMI) model of hybrid incompatibility predicts that neutral or adaptive substitutions that accumulate between diverging species may interact improperly in hybrids ( Fig. 1 ), leading to reduced viability and fertility [ 7 ]. While the general predictions of this model have been well supported by decades of genetic crosses in myriad species, only in recent years have the genes underlying these interactions and the mechanisms through which they evolve come into focus, aided by rapid technological advances. Work in the first decade of the 21st century focused on classical lab models with exceptional genetic tools including Drosophila, Arabidopsis , and Saccharomyces (reviewed in [ 7 ]), but advances in genomic tools for non-model species have enabled the discovery of hybrid incompatibilities in diverse taxa. Here, we review how the past 15 years of speciation research has led to a richer understanding of the potential mechanisms through which new species evolve, and deepened our knowledge of how incompatible alleles accumulate. In addition, our review sheds light on how incompatibilities act in naturally hybridizing species and highlights key knowledge gaps in the field. What we knew about “Speciation Genes” and what we know today In his seminal 2010 paper “The molecular evolutionary basis of species formation”, Presgraves [ 7 ] described all known genes involved in hybrid incompatibilities and outlined the first clues about how these incompatibilities arise based on empirical data (for theoretical predictions see [ 9 – 11 ]). Here, we revisit this work with a particular emphasis on incompatibility genes that have been identified since 2010, building on a number of studies that have focused on different aspects of this vast literature [ 7 , 12 – 19 ]. We focus our search on hybrid incompatibilities that act “intrinsically,” meaning that these incompatibilities cause hybrid dysfunction regardless of the environment (but see [ 3 ]). Using both broad and targeted literature searches, we are able to identify 100 incompatibilities where at least one of the genes involved has been precisely identified ( Table 1 ; see table legend for a description of our methodology). This large dataset allows us to begin to explore broad patterns in the data, while keeping in mind the many factors that impact DMI discovery and characterization. One of the most striking differences when comparing our catalog of incompatibilities to the genes reported in Presgraves 2010 is a major expansion in the species in which hybrid incompatibilities have been identified ( Fig. 2 ). Early work necessarily relied on species with exceptionally powerful genetic toolkits. While Drosophila and Arabidopsis continue to be overrepresented among organisms with precisely mapped hybrid incompatibilities, there has been substantial progress in mapping incompatibilities in less traditional models over the last decade. This advance is particularly important in addressing past criticisms of work in model systems, where some researchers noted that deep divergence between lineages could result in the identification of intrinsic incompatibilities that evolved long after speciation [ 20 – 22 ]. Table 1 includes 27 genera (13 of which include domesticated lineages; Fig. 2A ), as opposed to the 7 genera with mapped incompatibilities known in 2010 (3 of which included domesticated lineages and were excluded from Presgraves’s analysis). However, certain groups are notably underrepresented, such as vertebrates, where only seven incompatibilities have been mapped in any species, and conifers in which no incompatibilities genes have been identified ( Table 1 ). These phylogenetic trends are also highlighted in other recent reviews [ 16 , 18 , 19 , 23 ]. Similarly, Table 1 covers a wider breadth of molecular mechanisms and phenotypes. Genes involved in molecular processes from meiotic recombination to developmental patterning to adult pigmentation have been shown to cause hybrid incompatibility ( Table 1 ). These diverse molecular functions are consistent with predictions of theoretical models that any interacting pair of genes could become involved in hybrid incompatibilities [ 24 ]. Despite this diversity, Table 1 features several instances where related genes have been implicated in incompatibility across different species. For example, researchers have found repeated involvement of RPP genes in hybrid necrosis in plants ( Table 1 ). These observations raise the exciting possibility that the rate at which hybrid incompatibilities evolve could differ across genes or pathways. However, it is also likely that these observations are interconnected, with researchers more likely to prioritize investigating incompatibilities that are known in other systems. Moreover, biases may stem from the systems in which incompatibility is most heavily studied– such as crop plants, which have undergone domestication. Genic mechanisms that underlie incompatibilities An expanded knowledge of the genes involved in hybrid incompatibilities allows us to revisit hypotheses outlined in Presgraves 2010 about the molecular mechanisms that result in the evolution of hybrid incompatibilities. In the majority of cases where hybrid incompatibilities have been precisely mapped ( Table 1 ), researchers have identified protein coding genes as the causal factors. Rapid evolution at the amino acid sequence level that disrupts protein-protein interactions appears to be the molecular cause of many of the known hybrid incompatibilities (e.g. [ 25 – 28 ]), and there are some documented cases of amino acid substitutions altering RNA-protein interactions [ 29 ]. In other cases, both evolved changes in expression and amino acid changes underlie hybrid incompatibility phenotypes. For example, in hybrids between swordtail fish species dysfunctional interactions between Xmrk and its repressor can cause melanoma [ 30 ]. Follow up work using cell culture experiments showed that both overexpression of the Xmrk repressor cd97 and amino acid changes in its sequence contribute to melanoma phenotypes in cell culture [ 31 ]. In several cases in Table 1 , the causative variant is structural. This is the case for so-called “ presence-absence variants ”, where duplication and reciprocal loss of a gene makes it possible for hybrids to inherit no functional copies [ 30 , 32 – 35 ]. Even with the massive progress reflected in Table 1 , there are still relatively few studies that have successfully identified which mutations or regulatory changes lead to incompatibility. This prevents us from confidently assessing the relative frequencies of different types of mutations contributing to genetic barriers. Interrogating these patterns is a high priority research area. An expanded knowledge of the mutations underlying incompatible interactions is not only important for our understanding of what types of evolutionary changes are more likely to lead to reproductive isolation, but can also greatly inform modeling efforts investigating the accumulation of incompatibility alleles (i.e. via the snowball effect or other processes; [ 36 ]) and determining how alleles interact at a molecular level to cause hybrid dysfunction. Non-genic components of speciation In addition to major progress in identifying new protein-coding genes involved in hybrid incompatibilities, research over the past decade has dramatically expanded our understanding of the genetic causes of DMIs which are not controlled by gene-gene interactions [ 18 , 37 ]. These mechanisms include structural changes in the genome that cause meiotic dysfunction, issues with inheritance of epigenetic modifications, or global perturbations to the gene regulatory landscape that cause hybrid dysfunction. Our earliest understanding of the genetic basis of hybrid sterility came from broad scale differences in genome structure [ 38 ]. Karyotype differences contribute to reproductive isolation between many species, and are among the best understood incompatibilities in species that are not genetically tractable (e.g. muntjac deer; [ 39 ]). Karyotype differences generally lead to hybrid sterility when hybrids are unable to properly sort their chromosomes during meiosis. Similarly, extremely high levels of genetic divergence between chromosomes can impact success in crossing over during meiosis ([ 40 , 41 ]; Box 1 ). Structural changes, such as translocations, also play a crucial role in hybrid sterility due to failed pairing and meiosis in many plant lineages [ 42 ] and have been linked to hybrid incompatibility through a number of mechanisms. See [ 43 – 45 ] for several excellent reviews on this topic. In addition to structural factors, other types of non-genic elements have been implicated in hybrid incompatibilities. Several families of transposable elements (TEs) have been linked to hybrid dysfunction in Drosophila [ 46 – 49 ]. For example, the copy number of P-elements significantly influences the frequency of hybrid dysgenesis [ 49 , 50 ]. Hybridization could also lead to genome-wide transposable element (TE) deregulation, called “genomic shock” [ 51 ]. Associations between general TE misregulation and hybrid dysfunction have been observed in Drosophila [ 49 , 52 ] and Caenorhabditis elegans [ 53 ]. Hybrid-specific misregulation of TEs has been reported in diverse taxa [ 54 – 58 ]. However, others have found limited evidence of TE misregulation in hybrids [ 59 , 60 ] or identified misregulation but with no clear impacts on hybrid fitness [ 61 ]. Other non-genic elements such as satellite DNA (long tandem repeats found in heterochromatin regions) and non-coding RNAs can play a role in hybrid incompatibilities. In hybrids between Drosophila melanogaster and D. simulans , the mh allele from D. simulans , which typically regulates satellite DNA, interferes with the function of satellite DNA 359bp inherited from D. melanogaster , leading to disrupted genome integrity and female infertility [ 62 ]. Satellite DNA is often highly differentiated even between closely related species, although this does not always result in an incompatibility [ 63 ]. Non-coding RNAs play diverse mechanistic roles, including regulating gene expression, chromatin remodeling, and suppressing transposable elements, among others [ 64 ], and have been implicated in several hybrid incompatibilities. For example, seeds produced by crosses of multiple Capsella species are inviable due to a lack of maternally deposited siRNAs in the endosperm, which leads to abnormal gene regulation and ultimately developmental failure [ 65 ]. Some of the genes that are targeted by siRNAs have previously been identified as incompatibility genes in other systems (such as PHE1 in Arabidopsis ; [ 66 ]), and a similar process may also lead to hybrid seed failure in Solanum [ 67 ] and Oryza [ 68 ]. Since non-coding RNAs tend to evolve rapidly but retain their functional importance [ 69 ], they may fall into a class of elements that are mechanistically likely to become involved in hybrid incompatibilities. Together, this work highlights the immense diversity of mechanisms through which hybrid incompatibilities can evolve. While the importance of non-genic hybrid incompatibilities has been appreciated since the inception of the field [ 70 , 71 ], and was discussed by Presgraves in 2010, newly mapped non-genic incompatibilities are emerging as important mechanisms underlying incompatibility in the decade since, expanding the simple model originally proposed by both Dobzhansky and Müller. Genetic architecture of speciation and its consequences for evolutionary outcomes While understanding genetic interactions and their breakdown in hybrids is an interesting question in its own right, the increase in mapped incompatibilities allows us to begin to evaluate questions about both their mechanistic drivers and their evolutionary consequences. Here, we connect what we have learned from newly identified hybrid incompatibilities to classic evolutionary theory. Symmetry and Complex Incompatibilities Classic theoretical work made two major predictions about the architecture of incompatibilities. First, under a model of neutral evolution, researchers predicted that incompatibilities would be “asymmetrical,” meaning that only one of the two mismatched two-locus genotype combinations is expected to have reduced fitness [ 80 ]. Note that similar outcomes can arise if substitutions leading to incompatibilities are adaptive within the parental lineages, but the dynamics of selection on hybrids are somewhat distinct (see [ 81 ]). Although some incompatibilities fit this asymmetrical model (e.g. Overdrive ; [ 82 ]), in many empirical cases, hybrid incompatibilities act “symmetrically,” meaning that both mismatched two-locus genotypes experience reduced fitness. Symmetrical incompatibility can arise through coevolution driving multiple substitutions in interacting genes [ 83 , 84 ]. Although these differences in genetic architecture may seem subtle, they can have profound impacts on how genetic incompatibilities act after hybridization. With asymmetrical incompatibilities , hybridization tends to lead to a loss of genetic isolation between species as a result of the compatible genotype combination spreading [ 85 , 86 ]. Moreover, this result is expected even if overall hybrid fitness is low due to many asymmetrical incompatibilities across the genome [ 84 ]. By contrast, symmetrical incompatibilities act as strong barriers to hybridization because all heterospecific two locus genotype combinations experience selection. Similarly, theoretical models predicted that hybrid incompatibilities are likely to be “complex”, meaning that they are expected to involve more than two interacting genes [ 24 , 87 ]. The intuition behind these theoretical models is that complex incompatibilities can evolve through more mutational paths that avoid low-fitness genotypic combinations. However, complex genetic interactions are notoriously difficult to detect and incompatibilities involving three or more genes are extremely rare in the empirical literature. Despite this, progress has been made in identifying [ 88 , 89 ] and mapping [ 27 , 90 ] complex incompatibilities, primarily in model organisms where large screens are possible. In some cases, complexity has been added to previously mapped incompatibilities. Bladen and colleagues (2024) recently uncovered additional complexity in the Hmr-Lhr-gfzf incompatibility in Drosophila [ 91 , 92 ], mapping a novel locus in D. sechellia known as Sechellia aversion to hybrid rescue ( Satyr ). Similarly, work by Moran et al. (2024) identified a novel example of a complex hybrid incompatibility in Xiphophorus ( Fig. 4 ). F2 hybrids carrying X. birchmanni nuclear ancestry at ndufa13 and ndufs5 and X. malinche mitochondrial ancestry are inviable. While this interaction initially appears to be the product of two simple incompatibilities with the mitochondrial genomes, Moran and colleagues found that harboring even one mismatched ndufs5 allele sensitizes F2 fish to the ndufa13 incompatibility. This is a subtle three-way interaction that was only detectable because it was possible to generate nearly 1,000 hybrids in the laboratory. This highlights the difficulty of addressing this question based on current studies: while the fact that few complex incompatibilities have been identified in any species could hint that they are less common than theoretical models predict, it is also likely that the technical issues impacting their detection obscure their importance. Since it is challenging for even large experiments to have sufficient power to detect complex hybrid incompatibilities, progress in this area will likely require the development of new computational or experimental tools ([ 93 ]; See Box 2 ). Snowball Theory The rate at which two diverging lineages become fully reproductively isolated depends on how quickly they accumulate hybrid incompatibilities. While classic theoretical work predicts the presence of a “snowball” effect, where the number of genetic incompatibilities grows non-linearly with genome-wide genetic divergence between lineages [ 24 ], only a handful of studies have evaluated this empirically [ 106 – 108 ]. More recent work has suggested a snowball effect might not be expected under certain models of speciation [ 109 , 110 ] or certain models of gene interaction [ 36 ]. For example, a gene at the center of a highly connected gene network may be more prone to incompatible interactions, whereas modular interactions may reduce the opportunity for incompatibilities [ 36 ]. Moreover, to our knowledge, no similar theoretical work has been performed for the strength of selection on hybrid incompatibilities, which is arguably an equally important factor for understanding the emergence of new species. In other words, we do not know whether the strength of selection on the average incompatibility is expected to increase with divergence between lineages. As a result, we are still very much in the dark about how the genetic architecture of incompatibilities scales with genetic divergence, with crucial implications for how quickly new species are expected to become isolated. The evolutionary forces that drive speciation Almost since the inception of the field, biologists have searched for common evolutionary mechanisms that drive the emergence of barriers to hybridization. Even in the decades when empirical work on the genetic mechanisms of reproductive isolation was limited, theoretical and narrative predictions about the potential drivers of this process flourished and were heatedly debated [ 111 – 114 ]. With dozens more empirical cases in hand ( Table 1 ), we can begin to evaluate some of these predictions. “Classic” models for the evolution of incompatibility Much of the classic speciation theory supposes that incompatibility loci fix as a result of genetic drift (although some models allow for adaptive substitutions; [ 115 ]). However, only a handful of incompatibility alleles to date clearly support this “neutral” model. Several incompatibilities caused by gene duplication are consistent with a model of neutral evolution [ 32 , 33 , 116 , 117 ]. In contrast, some of the best studied genes involved in hybrid incompatibilities exhibit elevated rates of molecular evolution, such as Prdm9 , which is one of the most rapidly-evolving genes in many vertebrate genomes [ 118 , 119 ]. The importance of rapid evolution as a driver of hybrid incompatibilities has been apparent for decades [ 24 ], as rapidly evolving genes create greater opportunity for incompatible allelic combinations (assuming that these genes perform essential functions). In many cases, rapidly evolving incompatibility genes are likely evolving via evolutionary arms races such as intragenomic conflicts and host-pathogen co-evolution [ 7 , 120 ]. Evidence for the importance of these evolutionary forces has only strengthened with 15 additional years of research, with “selfish genetic elements” and “host-pathogen coevolution” being the two most common mechanisms proposed by authors as drivers of the evolution of the incompatibilities listed in Table 1 ( Fig. 2B ). Given its clear importance, there have been many excellent and in-depth reviews on the role of genetic conflict in driving hybrid incompatibility and other genomic processes [ 23 , 121 – 123 ]. However, despite empirical evidence of the importance of these processes, to our knowledge, they have yet to be integrated into theoretical models of hybrid incompatibilities, presenting an important (and addressable) knowledge gap for the field. Another classic model for the evolution of hybrid incompatibilities is the evolution of incompatibility as a byproduct of substitutions driven by divergent ecological selection [ 115 ]. This model has a long and contentious history in speciation biology [ 124 , 125 ]. The most direct evidence that ecological divergence can lead to the accumulation of hybrid incompatibilities comes from experimental evolution in yeast and Drosophila [ 126 – 129 ]. However, empirical evidence of this process in nature is scarce and the degree to which adaptation drives the accumulation of intrinsic incompatibilities in nature is still poorly understood. Perhaps one of the most compelling examples comes from recent work on an ancient balanced polymorphism in yeast underlying galactose versus glucose metabolism, in which hybrids suffer reduced growth relative to both parental strains (although we note that reduced hybrid growth may be restricted to galactose environments; [ 130 ]. Other potential examples include strong hybrid incompatibilities between closely related populations adapted to different environments [ 131 , 132 ], and hybrid breakdown associated with dysfunctional metabolism [ 133 , 134 ]. Nonetheless, since we lack knowledge of the precise genes or mechanisms involved in these cases, it remains challenging to distinguish whether ecological divergence is directly responsible for the accumulation of incompatibility alleles versus scenarios of hitchhiking [ 42 , 135 , 136 ]. Moreover, ecological divergence may be quite important in driving the evolution of more subtle dysfunctional hybrid phenotypes, such as those involving physiological, cognitive, or growth related traits [ 137 , 138 ]. Gene networks, complexity, and developmental mechanisms Insights from systems biology have led to new models for how incompatibilities might arise since Presgraves 2010. In particular, developmental systems drift describes observations inspired by gene regulatory networks, where evolving biological systems can remain functionally ‘equivalent’ but have diverged in their underlying structure [ 93 , 139 , 140 ]. This divergence may occur via compensatory evolution or through neutral substitutions that maintain optimal trait expression, but nonetheless alter the underlying genetic basis. Theoretical work on this topic supports the inference that even biological systems under strong stabilizing selection can rapidly evolve hybrid incompatibilities [ 141 ], and that this outcome is particularly likely in models of complex gene regulatory networks with functional redundancy. Importantly, the developmental systems drift model does not require any form of adaptive divergence or genetic conflict within parental lineages for hybrids to experience strong selection. Studies over the past decade have highlighted the prevalence of gene expression misregulation in hybrids [ 140 , 142 , 143 ] and divergence in the genetic architecture of seemingly identical phenotypes among related species [ 21 ], both of which are predicted under a systems drift model (we note that gene misregulation does not necessarily provide direct evidence for the presence of hybrid incompatibilities; [ 142 ]). More direct evidence has come from new empirical studies that have documented hybrid incompatibilities arising in conserved developmental pathways. In a pair of papers, Chang et al. [ 21 , 22 ] show that two highly conserved transcription factors that play the same developmental role across Drosophila species cause severe developmental incompatibilities in hybrids ( Fig. 5 ) These results provide exciting empirical evidence for developmental systems drift in action, and its link to developmental dysfunction in hybrids. Underappreciated evolutionary mechanisms: balancing selection and past introgression Recent work has revealed evolutionary mechanisms that can lead to the emergence of hybrid incompatibilities that were not predicted by previous conceptual or theoretical models: balancing selection and introgression . Balancing selection has been shown to maintain incompatible alleles within a species in several systems, sometimes over millions of years of evolution. Such mechanisms may underlie some hybrid necrosis phenotypes in plants: NB-LRR proteins that are activated in immune responses to pathogens often harbor high levels of polymorphism, strong signals of selection, and alternative alleles at intermediate frequency, supporting a role of balancing selection ([ 122 , 144 – 148 ]; Table 1 ). Balancing selection for immunity traits may be important in maintaining allelic diversity at such NLR genes, but in other cases NLR allelic diversity has been linked to tradeoffs between immune function and adaptation to abiotic pressures [ 149 , 150 ]. Similarly, ancient balancing selection in yeasts has maintained a polymorphism in the ability to grow rapidly in galactose-rich environments (as opposed to glucose-rich environments), which causes growth defects within hybrids between strains (although the strength of this incompatibility varies across environments; [ 130 ]). These intraspecific incompatibilities can ultimately lead to the evolution of interspecific incompatibilities via the differential loss of ancient balanced polymorphisms, for example through mating system shifts [ 144 ]. Beyond these specific examples, a large body of work has highlighted the importance of polymorphic hybrid incompatibilities [ 151 ]. These observations could be consistent with an underappreciated role of balancing selection in the maintenance of hybrid incompatibilities, or simply indicate that these variants are on their way to fixation or loss via natural selection or genetic drift. Historically, researchers have predicted that hybridization between species should erode genetic incompatibilities. Although much theory and some empirical work support this hypothesis [ 152 – 154 ], a growing body of work suggests that hybridization can lead to complex patterns of reproductive isolation and potentially move incompatible alleles between species. For example, recent work in Xiphophorus found that alleles involved in an incompatibility between X. malinche and X. birchmanni have introgressed from X. malinche into a third species, X. cortezi . Crosses between X. birchmanni and X. cortezi suggest that these introgressed alleles could be causing a phenotypically similar incompatibility in this species pair [ 155 ]. Similarly in Mimulus , patterns of organelle capture from the outcrossing M. cardinalis into selfing M. parishii may have facilitated cytoplasmic male sterility between M. parishii and a third species– M. lewisii [ 156 ]. Lastly, horizontal gene transfer of a toxin-antidote system among distantly related Caenorhabditis species has seemingly facilitated ongoing incompatibility within C. briggsae [ 53 ]. Together, this highlights the potential for past hybridization and gene transfer events to impact the present-day distribution of hybrid incompatibilities between species and adds substantial complexity to our understanding of the evolution of hybrid incompatibilities. We speculate that in the case of both balancing selection and introgression, the increased genetic divergence between interacting genes (either driven by ancient balancing selection or movement of genes from a divergent lineage) may be contributing to incompatibility. While the frequency of ancient balancing selection is unclear, it is now apparent that introgression is ubiquitous in many if not most species groups [ 157 , 158 ]. We thus predict that this mechanism may be common beyond the highlighted case studies, with major implications for our understanding of the evolution of reproductive isolation. Determining when introgression leads to the maintenance, erasure, or transfer of incompatibility alleles will require significant strides in both theoretical and empirical work. Understanding hybridization in natural systems will also be crucial for studies considering links between introgression and the spread of hybrid incompatibilities. Reproductive isolation in the wild Research into the genetics of hybrid incompatibilities is often motivated by a desire to understand genetic barriers that are important in speciation. However, the field still has a limited understanding of the genetic basis of hybrid incompatibilities in young, naturally hybridizing systems. This gap in our understanding of how hybrid incompatibilities are uncovered and experience selection in nature is a major shortcoming of the current literature. Because the vast majority of hybrid incompatibilities have been identified in species that do not naturally hybridize ( Table 1 ), it is impossible to evaluate their action in natural populations, and in some cases it is difficult to determine whether incompatibilities evolved before or after speciation [ 20 ]. The few exceptions - including mice, Mimulus , and swordtails - have yielded mixed results. Recently, Frayer and Payseur reported that most loci involved in reproductive incompatibility in mice do not prevent gene flow in natural hybrids [ 159 ]. By contrast, work in swordtails has indicated strong selection against mitonuclear incompatibilities and hybrid melanoma in wild populations, often resulting in changes in ancestry around these loci [ 160 , 161 ]. In hybrids between Mimulus guttatus and M. nasutus , some alleles show reductions in introgression in nature, while others do not [ 86 , 162 , 163 ]. An additional complexity is the growing realization that the strength of reproductive isolation is highly polymorphic in nature. While patterns of local ancestry in replicated hybrid zones are very consistent in some species [ 161 ], in other species pairs local ancestry patterns are highly variable [ 164 ]. Some of these patterns are likely driven by variation in the strength of selection due to extrinsic or demographic factors, but they could also reflect the outcomes of polymorphism in the underlying loci involved in hybrid incompatibilities. Indeed, many of the genes in Table 1 are polymorphic within their respective species. Just as asymmetric incompatibilities may be more likely to be removed by strong selection against hybrids (see section “ Symmetry and Complex Incompatibilities ”), an incompatible allele that is polymorphic within a population may also be easily removed by selection, unless there is selection to maintain this variation (see above). More broadly, we highlight that the way that incompatibility alleles act in naturally hybridizing species may be more complex than has long been anticipated [ 152 , 153 , 165 , 166 ]. Expanding our understanding of genetic incompatibilities in naturally hybridizing species and determining how frequently and under what conditions they play a role in preventing genetic exchange between species in nature should be a major priority for future work. Evolutionary idiosyncrasies: patterns, processes and reading the phylogenetic tea leaves With a rapid increase in the number of mapped hybrid incompatibilities, we have an opportunity to ask whether the mechanisms that promote the evolution of incompatibilities are shared across the branches of the tree of life. At first glance, it appears that the evolutionary mechanisms that underlie incompatibilities may vary across kingdoms, with coevolution with satellite DNA being especially common in Drosophila and host-pathogen coevolution being remarkably common in plants, to name a few patterns that immediately emerge from our analysis ( Fig. 2B ). Furthermore, several cases where the same genes repeatedly become involved in hybrid incompatibilities may be driven by these common evolutionary pressures. For example, almost all incidences of hybrid necrosis across diverse plant species involve nucleotide-binding domain and leucine-rich repeat (NLR) genes [ 122 ]. It is important to note, however, that these discoveries do not occur in isolation. Each newly mapped incompatibility spurs research into the consequences of particular genetic mechanisms, especially in closely related species. This makes unraveling phylogenetic patterns particularly challenging. However, we can look to examples where a particular mechanism has been investigated across diverse taxa. As one example, researchers have intensively studied evidence for TE misregulation in hybrids, motivated by compelling evidence of the links between TE misregulation and hybrid dysgenesis in Drosophila . While studies in several systems have found evidence for changes in TE regulation in hybrids, few have found evidence that this is linked to lower viability or fertility in hybrids [ 49 ], suggesting that this mechanism may be somewhat lineage specific. By contrast, cytonuclear incompatibilities appear to be quite common across taxa and may represent a common evolutionary mechanism for the emergence of incompatibilities. Moreover, the convergent evolution of genomic imprinting in mammals and angiosperms could explain the seemingly parallel patterns of parent-of-origin growth defects underlying early onset hybrid inviability in these taxa [ 167 ]. Overall, the variance in mechanisms across systems highlights how little is known in general about the degree to which the evolutionary drivers of hybrid incompatibilities are shared versus lineage specific. Concluding Remarks Despite substantial progress in the past 15 years in identifying the genes underlying hybrid incompatibilities and the mechanisms through which they evolve, many outstanding questions remain (see Outstanding Questions ). With dozens of newly mapped hybrid incompatibilities, we find that several mechanisms previously synthesized by Presgraves (2010) and others [ 12 , 13 ], such as intragenomic conflicts, remain an important force in the evolution of hybrid incompatibilities. However, we also highlight new evolutionary scenarios that may play fundamental roles in the evolution of incompatibilities, including developmental systems drift, balancing selection, and introgression. Although we are now amassing some empirical examples of these processes, the relative importance of these evolutionary drivers remains unknown. In Box 2 , we highlight new and promising approaches to begin to pursue these fundamental questions. Moreover, there is an urgent need to revisit classic theoretical models of how hybrid incompatibilities evolve in light of current empirical results and newer models for the evolution of hybrid incompatibility. Recombination, Sequence Divergence, and Isolation Successful meiosis requires that a precursor cell accurately sorts one copy of each of its chromosomes into the future gametes. Pairing of homologous chromosomes is a crucial step in this process. If the paired chromosomes are too dissimilar, “anti-recombination” mechanisms can prevent crossing over and halt segregation, generally initiated by the action of mismatch repair proteins [ 72 ]. While this mechanism typically prevents rare errors where non-homologous chromosomes pair during meiosis, a similar process may also come into play in hybrids. Specifically, if the two chromosomes that need to pair come from deeply diverged species, this may trigger anti-recombination pathways in nearly every meiosis, ultimately resulting in hybrid sterility. This mechanism of reproductive isolation has been observed in yeast ( Fig. 3 ). Early studies in yeast showed that the mismatch-repair system plays a key role in several instances of hybrid sterility between species [ 72 ] and between divergent lineages [ 73 ]. Hybrids of Saccharomyces cerevisiae and S. paradoxus , which exhibit ~12% sequence divergence, experience dysfunctional chromosomal segregation and high rates of aneuploidy [ 74 ]. Among S. paradoxus strains, even relatively low levels of sequence divergence (1.4%) result in increased rates of spore inviability due to activation of anti-recombination mechanisms [ 74 ]. The suppression of mismatch-repair during meiosis rescues hybrid fertility, confirming the role of anti-recombination mechanisms in reproductive isolation between these species. While several mismatch repair genes have been implicated in this process (e.g. MHS2 and SGS1 ), the underlying genetic divergence between the sequences plays a key role in meiotic failure and hybrid sterility [ 41 ]. Meiotic problems impacting chromosome pairing tend to be observed in species with high to extraordinary levels of genetic divergence at the nucleotide level, and the degree to which similar mechanisms may impact fertility in species with less extreme genetic divergence is unclear. That said, sequence divergence at the binding sites of Prdm9 , which specifies the locations of meiotic double strand breaks in mammals and some other vertebrates, also drives hybrid sterility in mice through distinct mechanisms [ 75 , 76 ]. This suggests that there are multiple ways in which recombination interacts with sequence divergence to impact successful meiosis. While the mechanisms involved in Prdm9- mediated hybrid sterility may be somewhat unique to its function and evolutionary dynamics [ 77 ], other direct and indirect regulators of crossover locations and overall recombination rates have been identified within and between species [ 78 , 79 ]. This suggests that future work may uncover additional links between recombination and hybrid sterility. Box 2: Promising new computational and experimental approaches A major barrier to progress in research on hybrid incompatibilities is the high cost and labor of identifying causative genes. As one example, the interacting partner of the Xmrk melanoma gene in hybrids between X. maculatus and X. hellerii took ~30 years to be identified [ 94 ]. Some recent experimental work has taken advantage of a combination of natural hybrids and admixture mapping approaches with lab-generated hybrids to combine the precision of mapping in the lab with the shorter ancestry tracts found in late generation hybrids [ 27 , 30 ]. However, few systems in which we can genetically map incompatibilities in the lab also have active hybrid zones, precluding this possibility for many research groups. We propose that an exciting possibility could come from adapting methods from other fields. For example, researchers focused on mapping the interactome have developed high-throughput and sensitive approaches to detect epistasis in cell lines [ 95 ]. Since it is increasingly possible to generate cell lines from non-model species [ 96 , 97 ], this approach could be accessible to many researchers, and could even be combined with a reciprocal hemizygosity test in F1 cell lines [ 98 ]. We note, however, that it would only allow researchers to assay a limited number of phenotypes. In cases where phenotypes associated with incompatibilities are known, other approaches such as targeted or single cell RNAseq, have allowed researchers to identify genes that are expressed or coexpressed in cell types of interest [ 99 , 100 ]. More generally, these approaches can allow researchers to infer gene interactions within regulatory networks in non-model species and identify disrupted interactions in hybrids, resulting in new tools for pinpointing hybrid incompatibilities. In addition to these experimental challenges, scans for hybrid incompatibilities notoriously suffer from low power because of the immense number of statistical tests required [ 101 ], but methodological advances have been slow. Most methods, including those developed by our groups, are underpowered and have high false positive rates (e.g. [ 102 ]). Researchers have used several effective approaches to improve power, such as first identifying segregation distortion in controlled crosses, and then performing scans for loci that interact with the distorter [ 27 ]. However, in a recent study we found that even with ~1800 hybrids, we only had power to detect segregation distorters that reduced survival by at least 30% [ 103 ], highlighting the likely presence of many biologically relevant incompatibilities that fall below the detection thresholds of most studies. Applications of new approaches from human genetics such as network-informed mapping [ 104 ] or machine learning approaches to identify signals in genetic data that have not been the focus of population genetic models could further improve power [ 105 ]. Progress in either experimental or computational tools could fuel major shifts in the field. Outstanding Questions How many hybrid incompatibilities distinguish closely related species and how quickly do they accumulate over evolutionary time? Are there dominant mechanisms through which hybrid incompatibilities evolve? Do these mechanisms differ between taxonomic groups? How complex are most hybrid incompatibilities? How many genes and/or components are involved? How do gene networks and the strength of interactions between genes influence the evolution of hybrid incompatibilities, particularly in models of developmental systems drift? How important is balancing selection in the maintenance of polymorphic hybrid incompatibilities? How often does introgression move incompatible genes between groups of related species? How important are hybrid incompatibilities in shaping the evolution of natural hybrid populations? Highlights The origin of species has long fascinated biologists, but determining the genes underlying intrinsic hybrid incompatibilities has only recently become possible in non-model organisms. We compiled all known incompatibility genes, many of which have been precisely mapped only in the last few years. Collectively, this review underscores that a variety of genetic and evolutionary mechanisms can underpin hybrid incompatibilities, including genic and non-genic interactions. There is growing evidence for the importance of intragenomic conflict in driving the evolution of hybrid incompatibilities, but also new evidence for the role of evolutionary processes such as developmental systems drift, balancing selection, and introgression. Finally, we highlight a growing need for new computational and theoretical advances to aid in identifying incompatibilities and determining how they evolve.
