Introduction Triploid rainbow trout ( Oncorhynchus mykiss ) is a polyploid fish species artificially developed through chromosome set manipulation techniques. From both genetic and aquaculture production perspectives, triploid rainbow trout generally demonstrate significant advantages compared to their diploid counterparts. Specifically, triploid rainbow trout exhibit genetically mediated growth advantages, characterized by significantly accelerated growth rates compared to diploid conspecifics [ 1 – 3 ]. This accelerated growth enables a reduced production cycle, facilitating compliance with market specifications and improving farming efficiency. Furthermore, triploid rainbow trout exhibit superior fillet quality, characterized by fine muscle texture, desirable organoleptic properties, and enhanced nutritional composition, thereby strengthening their commercial competitiveness [ 4 ]. Triploid rainbow trout, developed through genomic manipulation techniques, represents a commercially valuable aquaculture species with particular suitability for cultivation in the Xizang Autonomous Region. As a superior cold-water fish, this artificially induced polyploid exhibits arrested gonadal development coupled with advantageous traits including accelerated growth performance, increased body size, and enhanced fillet coloration [ 5 ]. Upon reaching sexual maturity in diploid rainbow trout, a significant growth retardation is observed compared to their triploid counterparts. Furthermore, triploid variants demonstrate additional advantages in disease resistance and muscle tissue quality [ 6 – 8 ]. The geographical configuration of China's Tibet Autonomous Region is characterized by high-altitude terrain, dense river networks, and extensive lacustrine systems [ 9 ], complemented by substantial cold-water resources that create favorable conditions for cold-water pisciculture [ 10 ]. In recent years, the rainbow trout aquaculture industry in China's Tibet Autonomous Region has achieved remarkable development. Notably, the introduction of advanced technologies and management practices to Shannan and Nyingchi cities has driven breakthroughs in the industrialization of high-altitude fisheries [ 11 ]. Global research on stocking density and growth characteristics of triploid rainbow trout reveals significant findings. Studies conducted in the mountainous regions of northeastern China indicate that at densities of 500 individuals/m 2 and 700 individuals/m 2 , juvenile rainbow trout cultured at the higher density demonstrate enhanced growth performance. Specifically, these fish exhibit a higher specific growth rate in total length coupled with accelerated body weight gain [ 12 ]. However, in the plateau regions of China's Tibet Autonomous Region, growth performance indices of female rainbow trout fry exhibit density-dependent variations. Final body weight, daily weight gain, specific growth rate, and net weight gain all demonstrate significant negative correlations with increasing stocking density [ 13 ]. Furthermore, the flesh quality and nutritional profile of triploid rainbow trout, characterized by elevated lipid content and beneficial fatty acid composition, have attracted significant scientific interest owing to their exceptional suitability for human consumption [ 6 , 14 ]. Research conducted in Washington State, USA, has demonstrated that triploid rainbow trout maintain locomotor behavior patterns comparable to diploid conspecifics in lacustrine environments, though reduced capture and migration rates observed in certain water bodies contribute to safeguarding the genetic integrity of wild fish populations [ 15 ]. Stocking density represents a crucial management parameter in aquaculture systems, exerting profound influences on the growth performance of cultivated aquatic species [ 16 – 21 ]. The gut microbiota is modulated by multiple determinants, including host genetics, environmental parameters, and dietary composition [ 22 – 25 ], and consequently regulates pivotal physiological processes in fish hosts, encompassing nutrient metabolism, dietary utilization, and disease resilience [ 26 – 28 ]. Nevertheless, the mechanistic understanding of how stocking density gradients and interspecific interactions collectively modulate these determinants remains incomplete [ 29 ]. These interactions critically govern community diversity, taxonomic dominance, and the structural organization of both generalist and commensal microbial populations within the intestinal ecosystems of aquatic species. Currently, no systematic investigation has addressed the effects of stocking density on growth performance and intestinal microbiota dynamics in triploid rainbow trout during early developmental stages under high-altitude conditions. This study aims to investigate the multidimensional effects of stocking density on triploid rainbow trout ( Oncorhynchus mykiss) under high-altitude conditions, with a specific focus on growth performance, histopathological manifestations, and intestinal microbiota (diversity and stability). The objective is to elucidate the correlation between stocking density and these physiological and ecological parameters, thereby establishing a theoretical foundation for optimized aquaculture management. The central hypothesis posits the existence of an optimal density threshold: at this critical point, juvenile fish would demonstrate maximized growth performance, absence of pathological tissue alterations, and maintained microbial homeostasis; whereas suboptimal densities would result in either inefficient resource utilization (understocking) or intensified competition and ecological dysbiosis (overstocking). Materials and Methods Stocking Density Setting The experiment was conducted at the Yarlung Zangbo River Fishery Resources Breeding Base (29.638593°N, 91.030090°E) in Lhasa, Tibet Autonomous Region, China. This location exhibits a topographical gradient descending east-westward within a plateau temperate semi-arid monsoonal climate zone, characterized by annual insolation exceeding 3,000 h at 3,650 m altitude with a mean atmospheric pressure of 652.0 hPa. A total of 1,800 triploid rainbow trout fry were supplied by the Institute of Aquatic Sciences, Tibet Academy of Agricultural and Animal Husbandry Sciences. Based on literature review and preliminary trials, three stocking density treatments were established: Low-Density (LD) at 100 individuals/barrel, Medium-Density (MD) at 200 individuals/barrel, and High-Density (HD) at 300 individuals/barrel, with biomass units standardized per experimental unit. There are 3 replicates in the LD treatment, 3 replicates in the MD treatment, and 3 replicates in the HD treatment, totaling 9 experimental treatments. All experimental groups were maintained in circular culture barrels for a 60-day trial period. The initial biometric parameters of the fish cohort were as follows: mean body weight 2.26 ± 0.38 g, total length 62.16 ± 3.95 mm, and standard length 51.77 ± 3.73 mm (mean ± SD). The experimental specimens exhibited healthy physiological status and homogeneous size distribution, with detailed morphological characteristics summarized in Table 1 . Experimental fish were randomly stocked in circular flow-through tanks. (0.45 2 m × 3.14 × 0.45 m -0.15 2 m × 0.45 m × 3.14 ≈ 0.25 m 3 ; tank diameter = 0.45 m, depth = 0.45 m.) The mean daily ration administered during the trial was 6.19 g, delivered through 2–3 daily feedings (morning, afternoon, and evening). Specifically, three feeding sessions per day were maintained during the first month, reduced to two sessions daily in the subsequent month. The total daily feed allocation represented 2%-3% of the total fish biomass. The formulated diet consisted of high-quality commercial pellets (Feed Type B3, Guangdong Yuequn Marine Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China) with the following nutritional composition: crude protein ≥ 50%, crude fat ≥ 8%, lysine ≥ 3%, crude fiber ≤ 5%, crude ash ≤ 16%, moisture ≤ 10%, and total phosphorus ≥ 1.5%, suitable for both freshwater and marine fish larvae. Water quality parameters were monitored following standardized protocols: dissolved oxygen was quantified using a HACH portable meter (HQ1130, USA); water temperature was recorded with a digital thermometer (RC-4HC, Jiangsu Jingchuang Electric Co., Ltd., China); pH measurements were obtained via a benchtop pH meter (PHS-3C, Nanbei Instrument Co., Ltd., China); and ammonia nitrogen and nitrite concentrations were determined using commercial test kits (Beijing Sampu Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd., China). Daily records of feeding adjustments and mortality rates were systematically maintained for all experimental groups (Fig. 1 ). Ammonia nitrogen, nitrite, and pH were measured twice a week, with target values as follows: ammonia nitrogen and nitrite &lt; 0.02 mg/L, pH between 7.6 and 7.8, dissolved oxygen &gt; 5 mg/L, and temperature between 12.2 °C and 13.3 °C. The aquaculture experiment was commenced on March 18, 2024, and terminated on May 16, 2024, spanning a total duration of 60 days. Collection and Processing of Intestinal and Water Samples After 60 days, a 500 mL water sample was collected from a depth of 0.3 m in the flow-through tank, promptly placed in an ice-packed foam container, and transported to the laboratory under cold storage within 30 min. For the water samples, three replicates were established for each treatment group (LD, MD, and HD), resulting in a total of nine sampling units. Water samples were filtered through PVDF membranes (0.22 μm pore size, GenClone Biotechnology Co., Ltd.), and the filtrates were collected in sterile centrifuge tubes with triple replicates per treatment. For intestinal sampling, three triploid rainbow trout were randomly selected from each stocking density group. Following anesthesia with tricaine methanesulfonate (MS-222, 100 mg/L, Beijing Green Hengxing Biotechnology Co., Ltd.), hindgut segments were aseptically dissected using sterile surgical instruments. Intestinal contents were expressed into 1 mL sterile centrifuge tubes, with samples pooled from three individuals per replicate, and subsequently preserved at -80 °C for downstream analysis. Following sample pooling procedures, intestinal specimens from the LD, MD, and HD treatment groups were analyzed in triplicate, yielding a total of nine analytical replicates. 16S rRNA High-Throughput Sequencing Analysis PCR amplification targeted the V3-V4 regions of the bacterial 16S rRNA gene using primers 338F: 5'-ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCA-3' and 806R: 5'-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3' [ 30 ]. Total genomic DNA was extracted from water and hindgut content samples using the TGuide S96 magnetic soil/fecal DNA kit (Tiangen Biotechnology Co., Ltd.), and the PCR products were analyzed using agarose gel electrophoresis, purified with the Omega DNA purification kit (Omega Inc., Norcross, GA, USA), and sequenced on the Illumina Novaseq 6000 platform (2 × 250 bp). Sequencing was outsourced to Beijing Baimaike Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Qingdao, China). For each tissue type (liver, gill, and intestine), three fish per replicate were analyzed across the low-density (LD), medium-density (MD), and high-density (HD) groups. This design resulted in a total of 27 samples per tissue and 81 samples overall. For histological examination, liver, gill, and intestinal tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and processed according to conventional histopathological protocols. Tissue sections were examined using a Nikon Eclipse Ci-L microscope under bright-field illumination at various magnifications (100 × , 200 × , and 400 ×). Digital images were acquired with Media Cybernetics Image Pro Plus 6.0 software for subsequent morphological analysis. Digital histological sections were examined using a Nikon Eclipse Ci-L microscope. Comprehensive morphological assessment was conducted at multiple magnification levels (100 × , 200 × , 400 ×), with fundamental pathological features in hepatic and branchial tissues systematically documented to characterize inter-group histological variations. Intestinal muscularis thickness was quantified using Image Pro Plus 6.0 software, employing quintuple measurements per histological section to ensure metrological reliability. Data Processing and Statistical Analysis All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.2.1 ( https://cran.r-project.org , accessed 20 July 2024). Microbial community analysis based on operational taxonomic units (OTUs) was conducted with the dada2 package ( https://benjjneb.github.io/dada2/ , accessed 22 July 2024). Growth performance parameters and water physicochemical characteristics were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) implemented in SPSS Statistics version 21.0 (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA). Statistical analyses were performed using Fisher's Least Significant Difference (LSD) post-hoc test, with all datasets satisfying the prerequisite assumptions of homoscedasticity and normal distribution. Alpha diversity indices (including Shannon–Wiener diversity, Chao1, ACE, Simpson dominance, and Fisher indices) and principal component analysis (PCA) were computed using the "vegan" package (v2.6–4) in R. Microbial community abundance patterns were visualized through heatmap generation using customized R scripts ( https://blog.csdn.net/ByteNinja/article/details/132518709 , accessed 23 July 2024). Gut microbiota co-occurrence networks were constructed based on Spearman's rank correlation coefficients and visualized using Gephi software (v0.10.1). All graphical representations were generated using OriginPro 2020 (Academic Edition). Results Effects of Different Stocking Densities on Growth Performance Indicators of Triploid Rainbow Trout During the 60-day experimental period, stocking density exerted significant influences on growth performance and survival rates of triploid rainbow trout fry (Table 1 ). At the 15-day sampling interval, the medium-density (MD) treatment group demonstrated the highest mean body weight (3.21 g), closely followed by the high-density (HD) group (3.18 g), while the low-density (LD) treatment yielded the lowest mean weight (3.12 g) (Table 1 ). By day 30, the low-density (LD) treatment demonstrated significantly greater mean body weight compared to the high-density (HD) group ( P &lt; 0.001). Two distinct growth phases were identified: the periods of 15–30 days and 45–60 days (Fig. 2 ) exhibited the most rapid weight gain, whereas the highest mortality rates occurred during the 30–45-day interval, with the HD treatment showing the greatest cumulative mortality. Regarding survival rates, the LD treatment achieved the highest value (84.6%), significantly exceeding the HD group (72.67%; P &lt; 0.036). For the survival rate, the degrees of freedom (df) were 2 between groups and 1 for both the contrast and deviation results. The LD treatment demonstrated significantly superior performance in final body weight, weight gain rate, specific growth rate, and feed conversion ratio compared to both MD and HD treatments ( P &lt; 0.05) (Table 1 ). These findings indicate that stocking density exerts a substantial influence on growth performance and survival rates in triploid rainbow trout fry. Discussion Impact of Stocking Density on Growth Indicators in Triploid Rainbow Trout Stocking density represents a critical management factor in aquaculture systems, directly modulating growth performance, survival rates, and production yield in intensively cultured fish species [ 31 – 34 ]. Empirical evidence consistently demonstrates an inverse relationship between stocking density and growth metrics, with the detrimental effects of high-density conditions exhibiting progressive intensification [ 35 ]. In this experiment, from the third sampling onward, the growth performance of the low-density (LD) treatment was significantly better than that of the medium-density (MD) and high-density (HD) treatments (Fig. 2 ), a trend that persisted throughout the study. This is similar to the results of density experiments on Ictalurus punctatus and Pelteobagrus fulvidraco [ 36 , 37 ]. Weight and Standard Length increased with culture period, but significantly decreased with stocking density. The experimental densities exerted statistically significant effects on final biomass, weight gain rate, specific growth rate, and feed conversion efficiency in triploid rainbow trout fry. These results corroborate established aquaculture principles wherein optimized stocking densities promote significant enhancement in piscine specific growth rates [ 38 , 39 ]. No significant intergroup differences were detected initially; however, after 15 days, the medium-density (MD) treatment demonstrated significantly greater biomass accumulation compared to both high-density (HD) and low-density (LD) groups. This growth divergence may be attributed to density-dependent stress responses that modulate feeding behavior and nutrient utilization efficiency [ 40 ]. By day 30, significant divergence in body mass had emerged between the LD treatment and both the MD and HD groups. This growth differential likely reflects intensified resource competition under elevated stocking densities, where spatial constraints, dissolved oxygen limitation, and trophic competition collectively elevate metabolic demands while suppressing anabolic processes [ 41 ]. Elevated stocking densities resulted in statistically significant reductions in both specific growth rate (SGR) and survival percentage among individual specimens. The findings of this study are consistent with the conclusions from studies on prawns , Channa striata , and Siganus guttatus under different density conditions [ 42 – 44 ]. This investigation establishes that stocking density significantly modulates the histological architecture of branchial and hepatic tissues in triploid rainbow trout fry. The low-density (LD) treatment manifested only minor morphological adaptations, including gill filament thickening and epithelial hyperplasia, without inflammatory pathology. Conversely, medium- and high-density (MD/HD) regimens induced severe histopathological alterations characterized by branchial epithelial lifting, cytoplasmic vacuolization, substantial leukocyte infiltration, and pronounced hepatocellular steatosis. The intestinal muscularis maintained structural integrity across all experimental groups, demonstrating that altitude-adjusted stocking densities within the tested range do not compromise intestinal wall organization in triploid rainbow trout fry [ 45 ]. These findings provide an important histological basis for optimizing stocking densities in high-altitude aquaculture to enhance animal welfare and production efficiency. Effect of Stocking Density on the Characteristics of Gut and Aquatic Microbial Communities in Triploid Rainbow Trout In aquaculture systems, stocking density serves as a pivotal environmental determinant that concurrently modulates water quality parameters and gut microbiota composition, thereby directly influencing aquatic animal health and physiological homeostasis [ 46 , 47 ]. Microbial community analysis of triploid rainbow trout fry reared under different stocking densities identified 27,838 operational taxonomic units (OTUs) in intestinal samples (Fig. 6 ). Treatment-specific OTU distribution revealed 8,516, 9,695, and 9,627 unique OTUs in HD, MD, and LD groups, respectively, demonstrating reduced microbial richness in high-density conditions. Aquatic microbiota analysis detected 8,955 total OTUs, with HD, MD, and LD treatments containing 3,462, 3,362, and 2,131 unique OTUs, respectively. Notably, aquatic microbial richness exhibited a positive correlation with increasing stocking density [ 48 ]. The intestinal and aquatic microbiota exhibited divergent response patterns to stocking density gradients in alpha diversity metrics. Specifically, intestinal microbial communities showed elevated Shannon and PD-whole-tree indices under high-density conditions (HD &gt; MD &gt; LD), suggesting that density-driven microenvironmental modifications may enhance host-environment microbial exchange and promote phylogenetic diversification within the enteric ecosystem (Fig. 4 ) [ 47 ]. In contrast, the ACE and Chao1 richness estimators failed to demonstrate consistent density-dependent patterns, revealing that the relationship between stocking density and taxonomic richness involves complex ecological mechanisms beyond simple linear correlations. This finding is consistent with the results of the investigation into habitat-driven richness and diversity in East African woodlands [ 49 ]. Conversely, aquatic microbiota demonstrated distinct alpha diversity patterns, with Shannon and PD-whole-tree indices peaking in both HD and LD treatments, while ACE and Chao1 indices exhibited an inverse relationship with stocking density (LD &gt; MD &gt; HD). This configuration suggests that elevated stocking densities may promote the dominance of specific bacterial taxa while reducing overall species richness in aquatic environments. These findings align with documented microbial shifts in penaeid aquaculture systems, where density-dependent conditions similarly drive dominant taxa proliferation alongside richness reduction [ 50 ]. The relative abundance of Proteobacteria, the dominant microbial phylum, demonstrated a positive correlation with stocking density, increasing from 19% (LD) to 25% (MD) and reaching 30% in HD treatments. High-density aquaculture can induce intestinal microbiota dysbiosis in fish, characterized by a significant increase in the relative abundance of Proteobacteria, which may elevate pathogen susceptibility and compromise host health [ 51 – 53 ]. Similar patterns of microbial dysbiosis have also been documented in spotted seals [ 54 ]. These changes indicate significant differences in the gut microbiota of triploid rainbow trout under different stocking densities [ 47 , 50 , 55 ]. Under low-altitude aquaculture conditions, stocking density significantly affects the fatty acid composition in various tissues of rainbow trout ( Oncorhynchus mykiss ) [ 56 ]. Moreover, transcriptome sequencing results under low-altitude conditions also revealed significant differential expression patterns [ 57 ]. Although the present study did not elaborate on specific changes in nutritional metabolism indicators or transcriptomic data, integration of existing research reveals that stocking density exerts regulatory effects on fish health in both high- and low-altitude aquaculture environments. This suggests that the impact of stocking density on fish health demonstrates environmental universality and is not confined to specific altitude conditions. Principal component analysis (PCA) was employed to evaluate density-induced variations in both intestinal and aquatic microbial communities of triploid rainbow trout fry (Fig. 5 ). The analysis revealed conserved structural organization of gut microbiota across all density regimens [ 28 ]. With substantial niche overlap and stable competitive interactions persisting across density gradients. In contrast, aquatic microbial communities exhibited significant compositional divergence among LD, MD, and HD treatments, demonstrating heightened sensitivity to density fluctuations. The structural stability of intestinal microbiota likely reflects host-mediated regulation through physiological homeostasis and immune modulation [ 14 , 15 , 18 ]. Whereas aquatic microbiota exhibited greater susceptibility to density variations. Mass spectrometry analysis revealed treatment-specific metabolic profiles: LD samples formed distinct clusters in both ionization modes, suggesting unique microbial/metabolite signatures. Conversely, HD samples demonstrated spectral convergence across modes, indicating metabolic homogeneity. MD treatments displayed intermediate characteristics with positive-ion mode clustering, reflecting enhanced metabolic diversity potentially associated with density-modulated microbial activity. Network Analysis of Gut and Water Microbial Community Composition at Different Stocking Densities Integrated analysis of zootechnical parameters reveals a positive correlation between stocking density and Pseudomonas prevalence. As an opportunistic pathogen, this genus demonstrates the capacity to induce enteric infections in teleosts, subsequently compromising host viability through disease progression and mortality escalation [ 16 , 55 ]. Pseudomonas proliferation induces gut microbiota dysbiosis in teleosts, compromising digestive efficiency and nutrient assimilation (Fig. 7 ) [ 56 , 57 ]. These pathological alterations subsequently undermine intestinal integrity and growth performance through disrupted microbial-enteric axis signaling [ 58 ]. Furthermore, empirical evidence indicates that microbiota alterations in cultured rainbow trout exhibit infection severity-dependent dynamics , potentially associated with the pathogenesis of inflammatory enteritis in triploid specimens during early developmental stages. Clinical investigations have demonstrated elevated Pseudomonas abundance in the intestinal microbiota of inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) patients, where it functions as a microbial destabilizer that disrupts community ecology and potentiates disease pathogenesis [ 23 , 26 ]. Consequently, Pseudomonas overcolonization may initiate a pathological cascade involving intestinal inflammation exacerbation, epithelial barrier dysfunction, and ultimately compromised enteric health in triploid rainbow trout fry [ 27 ]. The observed phylogenetic convergence between LD and HD treatments suggests density-mediated microbial selection, providing insights into ecological adaptation mechanisms in engineered aquaculture ecosystems. Aquatic microbial networks in LD treatments demonstrated enhanced complexity, evidenced by elevated node connectivity and modularity coefficients (Fig. 8 ) [ 2 ]. These findings establish that stocking density critically regulates intestinal homeostasis in triploid rainbow trout fry, with LD and MD regimens maintaining superior microbial ecosystem stability (Fig. 8 ). Conclusion This study establishes a dose–response relationship between stocking density and intestinal health in triploid rainbow trout fry. Experimental evidence demonstrates that low-density cultivation regimes (as implemented in the LD treatment) represent the optimal strategy for rearing this species in high-altitude environments. This finding has direct applicability to commercial aquaculture operations in elevated regions such as the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau. Specifically, low-density cultivation significantly mitigates histopathological damage in branchial and hepatic tissues—notably preventing the severe epithelial lifting and steatosis observed in medium- and high-density groups—while maintaining microbial homeostasis. This integrated approach reduces disease susceptibility and enhances survival probability. The research underscores the critical importance of microbial equilibrium in aquaculture management. Monitoring key taxa such as Pseudomonas —which demonstrated density-dependent proliferation in this study—provides a practical bioindicator for health assessment. Real-time surveillance of such microbial signatures enables proactive density adjustment before pathological manifestations occur. Future investigations should focus on elucidating the dualistic nature of Pseudomonas in teleost nutrition and disease etiology. Determining its context-dependent functional shift between commensal and pathogenic states under density stress will inform targeted interventions, including microbial modulators and precision density management protocols. Through multivariate analysis of growth performance and survival metrics, this work advances our understanding of host-microbiota dynamics in triploid rainbow trout while establishing a foundation for evidence-based aquaculture guidelines tailored to altitudinal challenges. Ultimately, these findings emphasize the ecological significance of density-mediated microbial stability in aquatic animal health. The proposed low-to-medium density framework provides a scientific basis for optimizing production efficiency while safeguarding piscine welfare, thereby minimizing economic losses associated with density-induced stress in high-altitude aquaculture systems.
