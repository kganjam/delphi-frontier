Introduction The Qiandongnan Xiaoxiang chicken, a native chicken breed endemic to Qiandongnan Miao and Dong Autonomous Prefecture, Guizhou Province, China, is renowned for its tender meat and unique flavor. Nevertheless, its slow growth rate and prolonged reproductive cycle have severely restricted industrial breeding and large-scale application of this germplasm resource [ 1 ]. As the major site for nutrient absorption within the digestive tract [ 2 ], small intestinal length (SIL) exerts a pivotal function in modulating the growth performance of poultry [ 3 ]. Inter-individual differences in SIL alter the intestinal absorptive area, the activity of digestive enzymes and the community structure of gut microbiota, which further affects the growth performance and feed conversion ratio of chickens [ 4 – 6 ]. Although the correlation between SIL and growth traits is intricate, numerous previous researches have confirmed a positive correlation between increased SIL and elevated body weight gain [ 7 – 10 ]. Accordingly, dissecting the genetic architecture governing the SIL phenotype carries important theoretical and practical values for establishing marker-assisted selection schemes to cultivate chicken populations with superior SIL characteristics, ultimately improving the growth velocity and feed utilization efficiency of Xiaoxiang chickens. Previous studies have uncovered multiple genomic regions and candidate genes linked to the SIL trait in livestock and poultry via genome-wide association studies (GWAS). In pigs, numerous quantitative trait loci (QTLs) and candidate genes (e.g., SOX6 , APOA4 , SIDT2 , TAGLN , and TMPRSS13 ) have been verified to regulate SIL, and this trait has been proven to be significantly correlated with body weight [ 9 , 11 ]. In poultry research, functional QTLs governing the length of different intestinal segments (primarily located on chicken chromosomes GGA11 and GGA14) and associated SNPs have also been identified, among which GNB1L and G0S2 are considered key candidate genes modulating SIL [ 12 – 14 ]. Further candidate gene-based analyses have confirmed that INS and other genes are closely associated with intestinal length development [ 15 ]. In addition to genetic factors, non-genetic factors including sex, breed and nutritional status also exert significant effects on poultry SIL. In general, female ducks possess longer intestines than their male counterparts [ 16 ]. Moreover, fast-growing broilers exhibit longer intestinal tracts than laying hens and wild ducks [ 8 , 10 ]. Nutritional intervention is also an effective approach to regulate intestinal morphological development. For example, existing studies have demonstrated that inactivated Bacillus subtilis (a probiotic), metabolites of Lactobacillus plantarum (a postbiotic), and their combined application can effectively facilitate intestinal elongation and enhance animal growth performance [ 17 , 18 ]. Gut microbiota and their metabolites act as core mediators that transmit nutritional signals to remodel intestinal morphology. Even under standardized feeding conditions, individuals of the same poultry breed show considerable variations in SIL, which is partially attributable to divergences in gut microbial community composition and serum metabolic profiles. Gut microbiota and their metabolites function as core signaling mediators to convey nutritional cues and remodel intestinal morphology, ultimately altering SIL phenotypes in chickens. Nevertheless, the genetic machinery governing chicken SIL has not been systematically deciphered, especially the genetic crosstalk between host genome and gut microbial metabolism. Accordingly, this study conducted a GWAS using 194 Qiandongnan Xiaoxiang chickens to mine SIL-associated genetic variants and pivotal microbial taxa. The candidate genes screened in the present work furnish robust molecular evidence for marker-assisted genetic improvement of growth traits in local chicken resources, meanwhile laying a framework for subsequent dissection of the genotype–microbiome–metabolism regulatory network governing intestinal development. Materials and methods Experimental animals and phenotypes The experimental animals used in this study were Qiandongnan Xiaoxiang chickens, which were raised in the Experimental Animal House of the College of Animal Science, Guizhou University between April and September 2024. A total of 194 chickens were sampled, including 108 roosters and 86 hens; all chickens underwent identical incubation procedures and were subjected to unified feed formulas, feeding regimens and daily management protocols. The detailed rearing scheme was set as follows: chickens were kept in one unified housing room at 1–4 weeks of age and transferred to another feeding room at 5–10 weeks of age. The cage stocking density was 16 birds per cage during the 0–4 weeks period and 8 birds per cage for 5–10 weeks, and all chickens were reared in mixed-sex groups. A 16-hour daily lighting regime was adopted, with the ambient temperature maintained at 15 °C to 35 °C. Commercial compound feeds (purchased from Guiyang Hengchen Feed Co., Ltd.) were supplied at 09:00 and 17:00 every day. For feed provision: broiler starter feed 510 was applied for chickens aged 1–5 weeks; a transitional mixed diet containing feed 510 and feed 511 was used in the 6th week; broiler grower feed 511 was provided from week 7 to week 10 to guarantee unified feeding standards for all experimental individuals. Feed and clean drinking water were provided ad libitum throughout the whole experimental period. All experimental chickens were reared up to 10 weeks of age. An 8‑h feed withdrawal was implemented for all individuals before slaughter. Following fasting treatment, the live body weight of each chicken was measured individually as the pre‑slaughter weight, and wing vein blood was harvested for subsequent genomic DNA extraction. All experimental chickens were humanely euthanized via cervical dislocation in accordance with institutional animal ethics guidelines, followed by immediate dissection. The intact whole small intestine was isolated and fully excised from the abdominal cavity, spanning from the duodenal root at the gastric pylorus to the ileocecal junction anchored by the ileocecal ligament. The intestinal tract was gently squeezed longitudinally and flushed with warm physiological saline to clear residual chyme, mucus and intestinal digesta, while physical damage to the intestinal wall was strictly avoided. The cleaned small intestine was laid out naturally on a sterile platform without forced tension. Total intestinal length was quantified with a flexible soft ruler: the measurement started at the proximal end of the duodenal bulb near the pyloric sphincter and terminated at the ileocecal ligament connecting the terminal ileum and cecum, and the measured SIL values were documented accordingly. All length determinations were completed by a single skilled operator with uniform mild traction applied during measurement. Every intestinal specimen was measured in duplicate, and the mean value of the two replicates was adopted for statistical analysis. Genome-wide association study Extraction of genomic DNA All samples used for DNA extraction were derived from anticoagulated blood collected from the wing vein of 10-week-old experimental chickens. The experimental procedure was as follows: Approximately 2 mL of blood was collected using a disposable syringe and stored at − 80 °C until further use. A total of 200 µL of anticoagulated whole blood was used for DNA extraction with a Universal Genomic DNA Isolation Kit (Magnetic Bead Method; Majorbio Bio-Pharm Technology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China).The extraction protocol was performed strictly according to the instructions of the manufacturer: 15µL of Proteinase K solution, 200µL of lysis buffer, and 20µL of RNase A solution (10 mg/mL) were added sequentially, followed by vortexing to ensure thorough mixing. The DNA concentration was determined using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). DNA samples that met quality requirements were stored at 4 °C for subsequent experiments. DNA quality control Concentration and purity of the genomic DNA were assessed using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Samples with an OD₂₆₀/OD₂₈₀ ratio of 1.8–2.0 and OD₂₆₀/OD₂₃₀ ratio ≥ 2.0 were selected for whole-genome resequencing. Sequencing data preprocessing Sequencing libraries were constructed using the TruSeq Nano DNA Library Prep Kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). Briefly, approximately 2µg of genomic DNA from each individual was randomly sheared into short fragments and purified. The 3’ ends of the fragmented DNA were repaired, an A-tail was added, and the fragments were ligated with paired-end Y-shaped adapters. PCR enrichment was then performed for library amplification, and the concentration was quantified using a Qubit 4 Fluorometer. Paired-end high-throughput sequencing was conducted on the Illumina NovaSeq Xplus™ platform, with an average whole-genome sequencing depth of approximately 10×. Clean reads from all individuals were aligned to the Gallus gallus reference genome using BWA-MEME software. SNPs were called from the aligned sequence data following the Best Practices pipeline implemented in GATK software [ 19 ]. A total of 21.18 million SNPs were identified in the chicken population, with an average genotype call rate of 95.35%. Functional annotation of the identified SNPs was performed using SNPEff software [ 20 ] combined with gene annotation information from the reference genome, generating complete and systematic functional annotation results. Heritability estimation Prior to genome-wide association study (GWAS), a genomic relationship matrix (GRM) was constructed for the studied chicken population using Genome-wide Complex Trait Analysis (GCTA) software. Based on the preprocessed phenotypic data for small intestine length, heritability estimates were obtained using the available genotypic data and the constructed genomic relationship matrix. Specifically, the restricted maximum likelihood (REML) module implemented in GCTA was used to estimate the heritability of small intestine length. Statistical‑analysis models Three representative statistical models were employed to perform genome-wide association analysis, namely the general linear model (GLM), mixed linear model (MLM), and Fixed and Random Model Circulating Probability Unification (FarmCPU) model. Each model was used to account for confounding factors such as population stratification and genetic relatedness at different levels, thereby improving the reliability and robustness of association results. All three models used the rMVP software version 1.0.6 [ 21 ]. Population stratification analysis Population stratification may induce false-positive associations in GWAS and distort analytical results. To evaluate the potential influence of population stratification in the present study, quantile‑quantile (Q‑Q) plots were constructed to examine the stratification level of the target trait. The degree of stratification was determined by comparing deviations between the observed SNP association distribution and the expected null distribution for non‑associated loci. All Q-Q plot analyses and visualizations were conducted using R software. Metabolomics and 16S rRNA sequencing methods Metabolomic data preprocessing and identification Blood samples (2 mL each) were collected from the wing vein of all chickens at 10 weeks of age. All blood samples were placed in blood collection tubes and kept at room temperature to allow serum separation. The separated serum was transferred into enzyme-free and sterile centrifuge tubes and stored at − 80 °C until use. All serum samples were transported on dry ice to Shanghai Zhongke New Life Biotechnology Co., Ltd. for untargeted metabolomics detection. The serum samples were thawed at 4 °C, and an appropriate volume of each sample was transferred into enzyme-free and sterile centrifuge tubes. For metabolomic data preprocessing, raw mass spectrometry (MS) data were converted to MzXML format using ProteoWizard MSConvert and subsequently imported into the XCMS software. Peak picking was performed with the following parameters: centWave m/z tolerance of 10 ppm, peak width ranging from 10 to 60 s, and a prefilter setting of c (10, 100). Peak grouping was conducted with bw = 5, mzwid = 0.025, and minfrac = 0.5. The CAMERA (Collection of Algorithms of MEtabolite pRofile Annotation) package was used for the annotation of isotopes and adducts. Only variables with non-zero measurements in more than 50% of samples in at least one group were retained. Metabolite identification was achieved by matching the accurate m/z values (mass error &lt; 10 ppm) and MS/MS spectra against an in-house database with authentic standard references. Amplicon sequencing of cecal microbiota based on the 16S rRNA gene Total DNA was extracted from cecal content samples using the OMEGA Mag-bind soil DNA kit, and the purity and concentration of the extracted DNA were evaluated. The amplification system was constructed using specific barcode-containing primers and high-fidelity DNA polymerase. Referencing the preliminary electrophoresis quantification results, the amplified products were quantitatively detected using a Microplate reader (Bio Tek, FLx800). Sequencing libraries were prepared using the Illumina TruSeq Nano DNA LT Library Prep Kit. Library quality was assessed with an Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 and Promega Quanti Fluor prior to high-throughput sequencing. Raw sequencing data were processed by removing primer sequences using cutadapt software. Sequence filtering was performed via the DADA2 pipeline in the QIIME 2 (v2022.8) environment, and sequences with a total abundance lower than 10 across all samples were discarded to obtain valid sequences and ASV feature tables. Representative sequences were taxonomically annotated against the Silva database (version 138) using the classify-sklearn classifier. Two-part model for screening key microbiota and metabolites The two-part model was applied to analyze the correlation between the abundance of cecal microbiota and the levels of different cytokines. This model integrates binary and quantitative analyses. The binary model was used to determine whether microbiota and metabolites in samples exerted effects on small intestine length. Based on the unweighted Z method, a meta-analysis was performed by combining results from the binary and quantitative models to obtain meta P -values. In this study, the final P -value was defined as the minimum value among those derived from binary analysis, quantitative analysis and meta-analysis, and the corresponding Z-scores were calculated according to the principles of Z-distribution [ 22 ]. Furthermore, the correlation analysis between the screened metabolites and microbiota was performed using the Lianchuan Bio Cloud Platform ( https://www.omicstudio.cn ). GWAS was performed using microbiota abundance and metabolite abundance as phenotypic traits, following the aforementioned GWAS procedures. A total of 21,184,357 SNPs and 2,940,186 InDels were retained for subsequent analyses. According to the Bonferroni correction formula, the genome-wide significance threshold was set at P &lt; 3.66 × 10 − 9 . Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) analysis An exploratory transcriptional analysis experiment was performed using an independent chicken population. A total of 25 seven-day-old male chicks were randomly selected and slaughtered to record SIL and body weight. According to SIL ranking, individuals with extreme phenotypes were classified into the low-SIL group and high-SIL group, with six birds preliminarily screened for each group. Subsequently, three birds were randomly selected from each group, generating six experimental chickens in total. Ileal tissues were harvested from all selected individuals for total RNA extraction and qPCR analysis, with four technical replicates set for each sample. It should be noted that this cross-age comparison only serves as exploratory correlative screening rather than definitive causal validation for SIL-related genes identified in the 70-day GWAS cohort. Total RNA was isolated from six ileal samples (three from each group) with the Eastep ® Super Total RNA Extraction Kit, followed by reverse transcription using the NotionScript cDNA First-Strand Synthesis Kit (with dsDNase). Primers for the three target genes were designed via NCBI Primer-BLAST and synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai). Relative gene expression was determined using the 2^(-ΔΔCt) method, with primer details provided in the Table 1 . Population stratification evaluation and GWAS for small intestine length Three representative statistical models, namely GLM, MLM, and FarmCPU, were employed to conduct GWAS in the present study. Visualization analysis of Q-Q plots based on these models demonstrated high consistency between theoretical and observed P -value distributions across all individuals. The genomic inflation factors (λ values) were 0.976 (FarmCPU), 0.976 (GLM), and 0.981 (MLM) (Fig. 2 D and F). All genomic inflation factor (λGC) values were markedly lower than the standard threshold of 1.05 (λGC &gt; 1.05 is considered indicative of substantial population stratification). This result suggests minimal population stratification across the study cohort and supports the credibility and robustness of subsequent GWAS findings. A Manhattan plot of M203T29_2; B Manhattan plot of M243T62; C Manhattan plot of M177T39_1; D Manhattan plot of M133T381 Discussion The small intestine serves as a vital organ for nutrient absorption and profoundly governs the growth performance of chickens. Previous studies have confirmed that SIL is tightly correlated with chicken growth traits. Notably, SIL exhibits moderate heritability, indicating that this trait is strongly modulated by host genetic factors. Nevertheless, the genetic architecture underlying SIL phenotypic variation remains poorly characterized in indigenous chickens. In this study, we performed a GWAS on a Chinese indigenous chicken breed to screen key candidate genes responsible for SIL variation. Phenotypic correlation analysis revealed a significant positive association between SIL and body weight. Integrative GWAS and functional annotation analysis further identified three pivotal genes, including IL1RAPL1 , PBX1 , and USP35 , which substantially contribute to individual differences in SIL. In addition, a total of sixteen cecal microbial ASVs were significantly associated with SIL, among which the genus Parasutterella showed prominent correlations with intestinal length. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that SIL is a critical determinant of chicken body weight, and highlight the dual regulatory roles of host genetics and gut microbiota in shaping intestinal length phenotypes. In the present study, body weight at 70 days of age and ADG from 1 to 70 days of age were both significantly positively correlated with SIL. This observation indicates that intestinal development and intestinal length are key determinants driving postnatal growth performance in chickens, which is in line with the findings of Kimiaeitalab et al., who reported that broiler chickens with enhanced growth performance exhibit greater intestinal length compared to slow-growing breeds [ 23 ]. As the primary organ responsible for nutrient absorption, the small intestine is typically modeled as a cylinder with absorptive surface area calculated by the formula S = 2πrd [ 10 ]. Accordingly, a longer small intestine provides a larger absorptive surface area, which represents one of the essential prerequisites for the efficient absorption of key nutrients, including carbohydrates, amino acids, and proteins [ 24 – 26 ]. Furthermore, a longer small intestine is associated with prolonged digesta transit time [ 27 ]. Extended digesta transit time can facilitate greater nutrient digestibility and modulate the secretion of satiety and appetite regulation [ 28 , 29 ]. Such regulatory effects may enhance feed intake and energy assimilation [ 30 ] and consequently promote body weight gain. Conversely, a shortened small intestine or one suffering from pathological damage exhibits diminished effective absorptive surface area, resulting in malabsorption syndrome that subsequently compromises growth and development [ 31 ]. The heritability of SIL estimated in this study ( h² = 0.41) falls into the moderate range (0.2 &lt; h² &lt; 0.5), which is highly consistent with previous reports on the heritability of intestinal traits in poultry ( h² = 0.30–0.37) [ 32 , 33 ]. This moderate heritability range has also been validated in other species; for instance, the estimated heritability of SIL in Yorkshire pigs is 0.25 [ 11 ]. Furthermore, different segments of the chicken small intestine (e.g., duodenum: h² = 0.36; jejunum: h² = 0.43; ileum: h² = 0.49)and their relative lengths ( h² = 0.28 ± 0.06) also generally exhibit moderate to high heritability [ 12 ]. Despite certain variations among breeds or species, the heritability of SIL and its respective segments is generally at or above the moderate level [ 34 ]. A moderate heritability indicates that although SIL is influenced by environmental factors [ 35 ], its basic length is still subject to considerable genetic regulation. This provides a robust theoretical basis and feasibility for improving SIL trait through selective breeding [ 36 ]. Key genes governing SIL in chickens, namely IL1RAPL1 , USP35 , and PBX1 , were identified through GWAS analysis. We set P &lt; 1 × 10 − 6 as the suggestive significance threshold instead of the stringent Bonferroni threshold to accommodate our limited sample size and avoid missing moderate-effect loci. Among these genes, IL1RAPL1 is a member of the Toll/IL-1 receptor family and is mainly reported to be involved in synaptogenesis and X-linked intellectual disability [ 37 ]. USP35 belongs to the deubiquitinating enzyme family and participates in multiple cancer-related biological processes [ 38 ]. To date, no studies have reported direct links between these two genes and chicken intestinal length development. Nevertheless, IL1RAPL1 has been associated with high‑body‑weight chicken lines [ 39 ]. This gene displays clear pleiotropic effects [ 40 ] and has been proposed as a candidate selection marker for enhancing feed efficiency [ 41 ]. Given that feed efficiency is tightly linked to appetite regulation mediated by the olfactory system, research in Holstein cattle has shown substantial overlap between feed efficiency‑related genes and olfactory genes [ 42 ]. Moreover, studies in zebrafish have established the critical role of IL1RAPL1 in presynaptic differentiation of olfactory sensory neurons [ 43 ]. Collectively, these observations suggest that IL1RAPL1 ’s role in neural development may provide clues for its potential involvement in appetite regulation, which requires further experimental validation. PBX1 is a nuclear-localized protein and a member of the TALE homeodomain PBC transcription factor family [ 44 ]. Accordingly, PBX1 may serve as a crucial candidate gene underlying variation in chicken SIL. Existing evidence indicates that PBX1 can physically interact with Cdx-2, a core intestinal transcription factor, and markedly augment the transcriptional activation of the proglucagon gene promoter by Cdx-2 [ 45 ]. The downstream peptide product of this gene, glucagon-like peptide-2 (GLP-2), is a potent intestinal trophic hormone [ 46 ] that drives intestinal development by enhancing crypt cell proliferation and suppressing Epithelial apoptosis [ 47 ]. These genes are involved in transcriptional regulation, cell cycle control, and immune regulation, which may indirectly affect intestinal development and growth. Studies have reported a strong significant association between the PBX1 gene and body weight in Rwandan chickens [ 48 ]. As a pivotal transcriptional regulator, PBX1 broadly governs the development of nearly all organs and tissues during embryogenesis [ 49 ]. In addition, the homeodomain of PBX1 is indispensable for the activation of fibroblast growth factor (FGF) signaling [ 50 ], and FGF2 , a key member of the FGF family, exerts critical regulatory effects on chicken growth and development [ 51 ]. Importantly, our exploratory validation experiment using another chicken population also confirmed tight associations between these three candidate genes and chicken SIL. In addition, A total of 16 ASVs associated with SIL were identified in this study, which were mainly affiliated with Bacteroidota, Firmicutes and Proteobacteria. Fifteen of these ASVs had positive Z-scores and exerted promoting effects on the elongation of the small intestine. Among them, ASV13 ( Parasutterella ) and ASV201 ( Clostridia_vadinBB60_group ) showed the most prominent positive regulatory effects. Only ASV20 and ASV172 ( Faecalibacterium ) had negative Z-scores, suggesting their potential inhibitory effects on small intestinal growth. Parasutterella is a core genus of the phylum Proteobacteria. Numerous studies have confirmed that this taxon is closely associated with intestinal development and growth performance in poultry, and its abundance is positively correlated with host growth traits [ 52 – 54 ]. Increased abundance of Parasutterella has been shown to help maintain intestinal mucosal homeostasis [ 55 ]. This genus is widely involved in the metabolism of tyrosine, cholesterol, bile acids and other substances, and exerts important regulatory effects on host metabolic processes [ 56 ]. It can degrade dietary fiber and ferment them to produce short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs). SCFAs provide energy for intestinal epithelial cells, promote the growth of intestinal villi and crypt proliferation, and ultimately improve the nutrient absorption capacity and overall growth performance of chickens [ 54 ]. In addition, Parasutterella can break down high-energy substances and regulate lipid metabolism and fat deposition, thereby facilitating weight gain in poultry [ 57 ]. In contrast, Faecalibacterium is a typical genus belonging to the phylum Firmicutes. Its abundance is strongly linked to intestinal inflammatory status [ 58 ]. Relevant studies on poultry have found that an excessive abundance of Faecalibacterium may inhibit the proliferation of other beneficial bacteria [ 59 ]. To further explore the regulatory effects of host genetic variation on the abundance of SIL-related gut microbiota and metabolites, we performed GWAS using microbial and metabolite abundances as phenotypic traits. Notably, no significant genetic loci were identified. This preliminary observation only hints at a potential relatively independent correlative pattern between host genetics and gut microbiota in regulating SIL within this limited population. It cannot rule out subtle host-microbe interactive effects, as our sample size ( n = 194) lacks sufficient statistical power to detect loci with minor effects on microbial abundance. Although this study offers novel insights into the genetic architecture underlying SIL in chickens, it possesses several limitations. First, the GWAS population comprised only 194 individuals, which limited statistical power for detecting minor-effect QTLs and may increase the risk of false-negative results. Second, we used a suggestive significance threshold ( P &lt; 1 × 10 − 6 ) rather than a strict Bonferroni correction to balance false positives and statistical power in a small population. Third, the biological functions of IL1RAPL1 , USP35 , and PBX1 in regulating intestinal length remain speculative; no in vivo functional validation (e.g., gene editing) was performed to confirm their causal roles. Future studies with larger populations, stricter thresholds, and functional experiments are needed to verify these loci and clarify their molecular mechanisms. Conclusion This study identified a significant positive phenotypic correlation between SIL and body weight in Qiandongnan Xiaoxiang chickens, and the estimated heritability of SIL was 0.41. Eleven genome-wide significant SNPs associated with SIL were captured, from which three core candidate genes ( IL1RAPL1 , PBX1 and USP35 ) were pinpointed. Microbial association analysis further detected 16 intestinal microbial ASVs correlated with SIL, and the genus Parasutterella exhibited prominent associations with intestinal length. The candidate genes and intestinal bacterial biomarkers obtained in this research offer basic theoretical support for the genetic improvement of growth traits in indigenous chickens. Nevertheless, the sample size for the current GWAS was limited. Larger independent populations are needed in subsequent research to validate the robustness of our identified loci and microbial markers. Supplementary Information Supplementary Material 1.
