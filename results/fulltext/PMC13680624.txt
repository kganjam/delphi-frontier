Introduction Salivary gland (SG) hypofunction has a profound impact on the quality of life of affected individuals, leading to difficulties in speaking, swallowing, and eating 1 . The available treatment options for these disorders are limited, providing only temporary relief, and are often accompanied by undesirable side effects 1 , 2 . As a result, there is a pressing need for novel therapeutic approaches to promote salivary gland regeneration. Current therapies include the use of human salivary gland epithelial progenitor (SGEP) cells 3 – 5 from minced glandular tissue or mesenchymal stem cells 6 – 9 as a source of trophic factors to promote glandular regeneration. However, these approaches have limitations including scalability, standardization, reproducibility, and cell/tissue delivery strategies that may result in poor engraftment. A potential alternative cell source for glandular regeneration are salivary gland epithelial progenitor (SGEP) cells derived from human induced pluripotent stem cells (hiPSC). hiPSC possess the remarkable ability to differentiate into all human somatic cell types, including salivary gland cells and can be readily derived from easily accessible adult somatic cells, such as skin or blood cells, making them an attractive cell source for therapeutic applications. Recent studies have shed light on the use of iPSC-derived SGEP for gland regeneration 10 , 11 . However, the current methods used for differentiation rely on forced gene overexpression via viral transduction to induce maturation 10 ; or technically challenging and inefficient techniques, such as microdissection of micrometre-sized epithelial buds from organoids 10 , 11 , hindering the widespread adoption of these protocols. In addition, while differentiation strategies that rely on embryoid bodies are relatively simple and scaffold-free, they often produce heterogeneous and disorganized structures containing multiple cell types, thereby complicating the isolation of specific cell populations or multicellular assemblies 12 . Moreover, the use of external forces, such as centrifugation, to aggregate cells can promote artificial cell–cell interactions that may not accurately reflect physiological conditions 13 . While these studies provided important proof-of-concept that SGEPs can be derived from hiPSCs, the methods are difficult to reproduce, underscoring the need for robust and scalable strategies to generate SGEPs for both basic research and the development of cell-based therapies. In this study, we present a step-by-step strategy that mimics SG development to generate multipotent SGEP cells that can self-assemble into lumenized acinar or ductal organoids, without the need for genetic modification or challenging microsurgical techniques for separating well-differentiated parts of microtissues. We utilize a scaffold in the form of a hydrogel for the growth of 3D organoids from the SGEP cells, directing differentiation toward the salivary gland lineage to obtain a more homogenous population. Furthermore, we demonstrate that hiPSC-SGEP-derived salivary gland organoids are functional in vitro and that they can be successfully engrafted into the submandibular gland of athymic nude mice. Notably, the transplanted organoids exhibited tri-potent differentiation potential, giving rise to acinar, ductal, and myoepithelial structures, providing compelling evidence for the potential of hiPSC-derived SGO as platforms enabling drug discovery and screening efforts or development of cell therapies for salivary gland regeneration. Results Establishment of the surface ectoderm lineage We hypothesize that salivary gland epithelial progenitor (SGEP) cells can be derived from human pluripotent stem cells by precise temporal control of pathway activation or inhibition that mimics embryonic salivary gland development. To address this hypothesis, we used two human pluripotent stem cell (PSC) lines, ATCC-BXS0114, an iPSC line sourced from bone marrow CD34+ cells and WA09 (H9), an ESC line modified with a SOX10 ::GFP BAC reporter 14 for live monitoring of Sox10 expression and a constitutive promoter, EF1α driving expression of the fluorescent protein, mCherry. Principal component analysis of RNA-seq data revealed that the two cell lines clustered closely at every time point, indicating comparable transcriptional dynamics throughout the 46-day differentiation process (Fig. 1a ). First, we treated the hPSC with an optimized differentiation factor cocktail comprising BMP-4 10 , 15 , SB431542 10 , 16 (a potent inhibitor of TGF-β signaling) and DAPT 17 , 18 (an inhibitor of NOTCH signaling) (Fig. S 1a–i ) for three days to guide the differentiation towards a naïve surface ectoderm (SE) fate 10 , 16 . RNA-seq showed downregulation of mRNA levels of key pluripotency factors such as NANOG, MYC and OCT3/4 ( POU5F1 ) (Fig. 1b , Fig. S 2 a, Fig. S 3a ) 19 – 22 along with upregulation of early SE markers 16 , 18 , 23 – 29 (Fig. 1c ). RT-PCR and western blot (WB) verified downregulation of NANOG (Fig. 1d, g ) at the mRNA and proteins levels, indicating departure from pluripotency. The preservation of E-Cadherin ( CDH1 ) (Fig. 1d ) and downregulation of classical epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) markers such as CDH2 and SNAI1 (Fig. 1b ) at this stage indicated maintenance of the epithelial phenotype 16 , 30 , 31 . Furthermore, we noted that the primary neuronal regulators ASCL1 and OTX2 were expressed at low levels, while key neural crest genes, FOXD3 and NGFR were downregulated significantly (Fig. 1b, e ), indicating a strong SE phenotype 32 – 35 . A week after BMP-4 withdrawal (Fig. S 1j–u ), the cells matured into a multipotent SE as seen by transcriptional upregulation of key SE markers like MSX1/2 and TP63 (Fig. 1f, h ) 16 – 18 , 36 . Overall, we observed a transition in gene expression from early-stage naïve (e.g. KRT7/8 , ISL1 &amp; TBX3 ) 16 , 18 , 23 – 29 to late-stage mature (e.g. COL5A1, COL12A1, COL17A1, TFAP2A/B &amp; KRT19 ) SE markers 15 , 29 , 36 – 38 (Fig. 1c , Fig. S 2 b, Fig. S 3b ). Furthermore, genes associated with SE derivatives such oral epithelium (OE) ( SLC4A4 ) 39 , presumptive lens ectoderm and cornea ( MEIS1/2 ) 40 and limbal development ( IGFBP5 ) 41 were expressed or even upregulated (Fig. 1c ), indicating that these cells may have the potential to differentiate to more than one lineages. β3-tubulin ( TUBB3 ), an early ectoderm marker 42 that later recedes to the neuronal ectoderm and its derivatives, was shown to be downregulated by day 10 (Fig. 1b). At the protein level, a small fraction of these nascent cells expressed low levels of β3-tubulin (Fig. 1i &amp; Fig. S 1s ), but high levels of SOX2, E-cadherin and Pan-cytokeratin (Fig. 1j, k &amp; Fig. S 1t, u ), indicating that these cells maintained an epithelial phenotype. Therefore, at this stage we established a robust multipotent SE phenotype with the potential to differentiate into its daughter lineages. Specification to oral epithelium and SG epithelial progenitors The salivary gland originates from the placode of the oral epithelium where Activin/BMP/SMAD activity is suppressed 43 . This prompted us to treat the SE cells with an optimized differentiation factor cocktail (Fig. S 4a–j ) comprising of LDN193189 10 and FGF-2 10 , 44 to inhibit the BMP-SMAD1/5/8 pathway and activate the FGFR pathway, respectively to coax the SE into the oral epithelium (OE). After three days of treatment, we observed upregulation of PITX1/2 proteins (Fig. 2a ) as well the mRNA levels of SOX2 45 , PITX2 45 and other genes associated with proper orofacial development 37 , 39 , 45 – 51 (Fig. 2b, l , Fig. S 3c ). Concomitantly, mRNA and protein levels of NANOG and β3-tubulin were further reduced (Fig. 2c, f, i ), while the mRNA levels of ASCL1 , NGFR and FOXD3 remained low (Fig. 2d ), indicating departure from the neural and neural crest lineages. Furthermore, there was downregulation in mRNA levels of MSX1/2 and TP63 (Fig. 2e ), indicating a departure from surface ectoderm. These newly formed cells continued to maintain an epithelial phenotype, as evidenced by further reduced levels of β3-tubulin and high expression of SOX2, E-cadherin and Pan-cytokeratin (Fig. 2f–k , Fig. S 4k–m ). Genes associated with the dental lamina 39 , 46 , 49 , 52 ( SOX11, FOS, FST ), and the stratum intermedium 39 , 48 ( NOTCH1 ), which are OE derivatives continued to be expressed ( FST ) or upregulated ( SOX11, FOS, NOTCH1 ) on day 13 (Fig. 2l , Fig. S 2 c, Fig. S 3c ). However, only those specifically associated with the salivary gland lineage 39 ( SOX9, FOXC1, BARX2, COL14A1 ) were further upregulated by day 17, indicating salivary gland specification (Fig. 2l , Fig. S 2 d, Fig. S 3d ). Activation of the FGFR2(IIIb) and RARα pathways was shown to increase expression of SOX9 53 , 54 , the primary regulator of salivary gland (SG) progenitors 39 , 54 . In addition, the developing gland exhibited active Wnt signaling solely in the mesenchyme, while inhibition of the Wnt pathway was necessary to generate the epithelial progenitors 55 . These findings prompted us to coax differentiation of OE cells into salivary gland epithelial progenitors (SGEP) with a combination of differentiation factors (Fig. S 5a–j ), including FGF-10 10 , 54 , the retinoic acid receptor alpha agonist Am580 53 , 56 and Wnt inhibitor Wnt-C59 55 . The transition from the OE was characterized by decreased mRNA levels of SOX2 and PITX1/2 (Fig. 3a ), and increased the expression of key salivary gland initiation genes such as SOX9 53 , 54 , 56 , FOXC1 10 , TP63 , and KRT5/14 15 , 18 (Fig. 3b ). Moreover, this SOX9+ population displays two distinct lineage markers, indicative of proximal and distal progenitors, mirroring observations made during salivary gland development (Fig. 3c ) 57 . Specifically, a gene marking the distal tip acinar progenitors, SOX10 58 (Fig. 3d ), as well as genes specifying ductal progenitors, like ASCL3 59 and KRT8 60 (Fig. 3e ) were significantly upregulated, indicating generation of a mixed population of multipotent progenitor cells. Flow cytometry revealed that 98.22% of SGEP were positive for SOX9 (Fig. 3f ) when gated to the isotype control. Immunofluorescence analysis showed nuclear localized SOX9, membrane bound E-cadherin and Pan-cytokeratin (Fig. 3g , Fig. S 5k, l ) in these cells. Some SGEP initiation genes like SOX9, FOXC1, COL14A1 39 were upregulated on day 17 and some continued to increase up to day 25 ( IRF6 61 ) or up to day 32 ( FOXC1) ; while others increased from day 17 to day 25 e.g., ETV4/5 62 , 63 . In contrast, genes related to the dental lineage derivatives 39 (e.g., SOX11, GAD1, LEF1, PRSS23, EDNRB, FOS &amp; FST ) were downregulated after day 13 or day 17, with major downregulation occurring after day 17 (Fig. 3h , Fig. S 6 ). Therefore, through these sequential inductions with differentiating factors over time, we successfully established a robust SOX9 + SGEP phenotype via a transitional OE stage. Furthermore, single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) revealed that the majority of SGEP cells were bipotent progenitors expressing both acinar and ductal genes. Smaller subsets of cells were identified as exclusively acinar or ductal, or exhibited low expression of either lineage (Fig. 3i , j). Overall, SGEP cells showed higher expression of ductal genes and lower expression of acinar genes. Consistent with bulk RNA-seq analysis, genes characteristic of oral ectoderm, surface epithelium, and myoepithelium were not detected at this stage (Fig. 3k , l). Generation of lumenized and SOX10 + organoids To form SG organoids (SGO), day 17 SGEP were embedded in Cultrex (an ECM rich in laminin, collagen IV, and nidogens, basement membrane components that play critical roles in salivary gland development and promote epithelial cell polarization 64 ) in the presence of an optimized combination of differentiation factors (Fig. S 7a–d ), including FGF10 65 , 66 , EGF 67 , 68 and dexamethasone 69 , for 8 days (day 17 to day 25) as these factors are well known to aid in organoid development (Fig. S 7a–d ). This was followed by a week-long treatment (day 25 to day 32) with another optimized mixture of differentiation factors (Fig. S 7e–g ) containing HGF, which is known to induce amylase promoter activity promoting acinar cell fate 69 ; along with EGF 67 and dexamethasone to induce SOX10 expression within the organoids (Fig. 4a ). By day 25, the SGEP embedded in Cultrex formed organoids with well-defined lumens (Supplementary Movie 1 ), a phenotype that was maintained through day 32 (Fig. 4b &amp; Suppl. Movie 2 ). At this stage, the organoids displayed significant upregulation of SOX10 as shown by RT-PCR (Fig. 4c , Fig. S 7 ) and SOX10 promoter ( pSOX10 ) activity as shown by pSOX10 -driven GFP expression (Fig. 4d , Suppl. Movie 3 ). Flow cytometry revealed an increase in the percentage of cells that express the pSOX10 -driven GFP as they differentiated from day 17 to day 25 and 32 (Fig. 4e ). Salivary gland initiation genes that were upregulated on day 17, such as SOX9 , HEY1 70 , LAMA1 71 , and COL14A1 were downregulated by day 25 and 32 (Fig. 4f , Fig. S 2e ). At the same time, two distinct gene sets associated with later stages of glandular development peaked on day 25 (IRF6, ITGA6 71 , LAMA3 71 &amp; MYC 10 ) and day 32 ( ETV4/5, ITGA3 71 , LAMA5 71 , MUC1/4/16 72 &amp; FOXC1, KRT17, KRT19, ST14 ) (Fig. 4f, g , Fig. S 3e, f ). These results demonstrate the formation of lumenized SOX10 + organoids expressing many key SG progenitor markers. Maturation of SGOs into acinar and ductal phenotypes Subsequently, the SOX10 + organoids were further differentiated by treatment with EGF 73 , retinoic acid 66 , hydrocortisone 74 , and triiodothyronine 75 . Interestingly, the p SOX10 -GFP signal continued to increase during this differentiation period (Suppl. Movie 4 ). By day 46, the organoids reached an average diameter of 50-60 μm, with the largest organoids measuring 100–110 μm in diameter (Fig. S 8 ). At this stage, the organoids exhibited positive immunofluorescence staining for mature acinar cell markers such as MIST1 76 (Fig. 5a , i), NKCC1 77 (Fig. 5b, i ), and apically localized AQP5 78 (Fig. 5c ). They also expressed the adherens junction protein, E-cadherin 79 (Fig. 5d, i ) and tight junction protein, ZO1 79 (Fig. 5e, i ). Notably, the organoids expressed AMY1A 80 and exhibited an apical F-actin ring (Fig. 5f &amp; Suppl. Movie 5 ), indicative of acinar cell maturation and the establishment of epithelial polarity. Genes associated with early developmental stages like ETV4/5 , MYC , IRF6 , ITGA3/6 and XBP1 63 , 76 reached maximum expression between days 25 and 32 (Fig. 5j, k , Fig. S 2f ); whereas genes associated with mature stages of gland development like SLC12A2 ( NKCC1 ), CFTR 81 , PIP 63 and ST14 82 reached their highest expression on day 46 (Fig. 5k , Fig. S 2 g, Fig. S 3g ). Mature SGOs develop salivary secretory function Saliva secretion is stimulated by the neurotransmitter acetylcholine, which is secreted by the parasympathetic nervous system and binds to muscarinic receptor 3 (M3R), prompting intracellular calcium release from the endoplasmic reticulum 83 . In turn, this leads to activation of NKCC1, which promotes recruitment of AQP5 to the apical cell membrane, enabling saliva secretion from the acinar cells into the lumen 84 . We examined whether organoids developed functionality at different times of maturation by measuring the calcium flux in D32 and D46 organoids in response to the acetyl choline analog, carbachol using Fluo4-AM. To this end, D32 or D46 organoids were released from Cultrex and placed on the surface of Matrigel coated wells. After settling onto the surface, the organoids were incubated with Fluo4-AM and stimulated with Carbachol to release intracellular calcium, cleaving the AM tag and generating a short fluorescence pulse at 525 nm upon excitation at 488 nm (Supplementary Movie 6 ). Representative fluorescence signatures of D46 (blue line) and D32 (red line) organoids in response carbachol are shown in Fig. 5l . The maximum fluorescence intensity (mFI) was used as a metric to compare D32 and D46 organoids. As shown in Fig. 5m , salivary organoids exhibited significantly higher mFI on D46 as compared to D32 (background level) ( n = 12, p &lt; 0.0001). These results indicate that our salivary organoids develop secretory function between D32 and D46, coinciding with expression of mature acinar cell markers MIST1, NKCC1 and apical AQP5 (Fig. 5a–c ). SGO transplantation into an orthotopic SMG wound model At day 32, SGOs are Sox10⁺, show high cell viability ( &gt; 98%) and express multiple key salivary gland progenitor markers, but are not yet fully mature. We therefore hypothesized that day 32 SGOs retain sufficient plasticity to generate all salivary gland lineages within the native gland microenvironment in vivo. To test this hypothesis, we examined whether the day 32 SGO could successfully engraft and differentiate into acinar and/or ductal tissue upon transplantation into an orthotopic submandibular gland wound model in vivo. The advantage of the wounded model over an irradiated model is the ability to control the size of the wound, thereby enabling direct monitoring of wound regeneration and the fate of the organoids over time. For this purpose, a surgical wound was created in the submandibular glands of athymic nude mice using a 3 mm biopsy punch and SGO were then introduced into the wound space within the Cultrex matrix (one dome per wound) (Fig. 6a ). To track human cell fate in vivo and distinguish them from mouse cells, the H9 hESC cells were transduced with lentivirus encoding the mCherry reporter. The wound site was examined at 6-weeks post -transplantation. Discussion We present a strategy for deriving salivary gland organoids from human PSC. By providing stage-specific cues in a temporal sequence that mimics glandular development via the ectodermal germ layer and progenitor phenotypes, we generated salivary gland organoids exhibiting lumenized architecture and physiologically appropriate localization of proteins associated with distinct glandular compartments. To assess the efficacy of this strategy, we closely followed the expression of notable genes affected at each stage as they are discussion here (Suppl. Table 5 ). First, the SE phenotype was induced via stimulation of the BMP-SMAD 1/5/8 pathway 85 ; while concomitant inhibition of the TGFβ and Notch pathways were employed to suppress mesendoderm differentiation 86 and promote TP63 expression 17 , respectively. SE differentiation was accompanied by loss of embryonic factors like NANOG , MYC and OCT3/4 . The loss in OCT3/4 expression was directly linked to down-regulation of ETS-related transcription factors ETV4 and ETV5 (ETV4/5 ) along with ES1 ( LINC01108 ), a long noncoding RNA that is specifically expressed in undifferentiated embryonic stem cells and regulates pluripotency 62 , 87 . Other genes associated with the maintenance of pluripotency like MT1X 21 , PCSK9 88 , TDGF1 89 , POLR3G 90 , ZIC3 91 and LINC00678 92 were also significantly downregulated. At the same time, genes that specify mature SE, including the Adaptor Protein-2 ( AP-2α/β/γ ) transcription factor family 15 , 37 along with SCUBE3 38 , GABRP 36 , TGM2 93 , SFN 15 and members of the collagen family 36 ( COL17A1 , COL12A1 , COL5A1 ), were significantly upregulated. Genes associated with SE derivative lineages such as COL3A1 94 and IGFBP5 41 (limbal development); IGFBP3 95 and MEIS1/2 40 (presumptive lens and corneal ectoderm); GUCY1A1 96 (olfactory epithelium); MMP2 97 (linked to teeth, lens, hair follicle, otic and mammary gland morphogenesis); SLIT2/3 98 (surface pharyngeal ectoderm); C5orf46 99 (linked to the eccrine sweat and seborrheic glands of the skin); SLC4A4 39 (OE) were significantly upregulated, demonstrating the multipotency of the resulting SE. The OE lineage was derived by inhibiting the SMAD1/5/8 10 while activating the FGFR pathway 10 to suppress the formation of other SE derivatives. This was evident by loss of MSX1/2 , and downregulation of MEIS1 , GUCY1A1 and SLIT3 with a concomitant increase of BNC1 100 (a p63 transcriptional target), SOX11 39 , 49 , FOS 39 , 49 , FST 46 , CCN2 39 , 44 , PROSER2 39 (dental lamina), SKAP2 39 (anterior epithelium), and ubiquitous expression of SOX2 throughout the cell monolayer. Later specification of OE to the SGEP compartment was coaxed by activating the FGFR2(IIIb) 54 and RARα 53 pathways, while inhibiting the Wnt 55 pathway, as seen in the developing gland parenchyma. This led to ubiquitous albeit heterogenous expression of SOX9, which is known to specify distal acinar progenitors while simultaneously promoting ductal branching morphogenesis. Consistent with this role, loss of SOX9 results in arrested salivary gland development 54 , 57 , underscoring its central function in maintaining the bipotent salivary gland progenitor identity of SGEPs. At the same time, there was upregulation of SEMA3A 101 (cell growth and morphogenesis), TIMP3 102 , CELSR 103 (involved in branching morphogenesis), CRABP2 104 (RAR pathway), NID2 105 (basement membrane protein); and marked downregulation of odontogenic (dental) related genes FOS , FST , GAD1, SLC4A4 . Interestingly, when embedded in Cultrex, SGEP cells self-assembled into 3-dimensional luminal organoids containing polarized salivary gland cells. Formation of lumenized organoids was accompanied by a shift in gene expression from SGEP initiation markers on day 17 to transition markers on day 25, including SERPINB2 66 (ductal cell development), CEBPB 63 (pro-acinar cell development), TGM2 106 (early myoepithelial cell marker), PTPRB 107 (inhibition of cell proliferation), SPRY1 108 , DUSP6 109 (branching morphogenesis) and DNER 70 (non-secreted salivary gland protein). A week later (day 32), the day 25 genes were downregulated, while more acinar genes were upregulated including XBP1 76 , 110 (activator of MIST1 promoter), ZNF185 111 (a TP63 target that maintains epithelial integrity by interaction with E-cadherin), NID1 105 (basement membrane protein) and BARX2 112 (involved in proliferation and differentiation of the gland epithelial cells). On day 46, XBP1 and ZNF185 were downregulated while late-stage acinar markers ZBTB20 63 (pro-acinar cell development), SLPI 113 , PIP 66 (salivary proteins), AREG 114 , EREG 115 (plays roles in glandular patterning) and GATA3 116 (maintains luminal epithelial differentiation) were upregulated. At the same time, genes associated with ductal network development and salivary gland maturation such as MUC1 / MUC16 72 , ST14 117 (involved in secretory epithelial cell function) were also upregulated significantly. While other ductal markers like CARHSP1 63 , SERPINB2 , TFCP2L1 118 , KRT8/17/18/19 60 and myoepithelial marker TGM2 were downregulated. Nevertheless, the levels of some of those ductal genes, especially, KRT8/18/19 and myoepithelial gene, TGM2 continued to be high, suggesting development of a salivary gland phenotype with mixed acinar and ductal gene expression. Indeed, immunostaining of day 46 SGOs demonstrated differential expression of mature markers like MIST1, KNCC1 and K7 , among cells comprising each organoid, possibly reflecting the differential response of individual cells to differentiation signals, or the heterogeneity of cell populations within organoids. Nevertheless, these organoids exhibited signs of maturation and function as shown by their response to the acetylcholine analogue, carbachol. Future studies are needed to define conditions that further enhance maturation and understand cell heterogeneity within organoids, which may further improve the secretory function and regenerative capacity of salivary gland organoids. Orthotopic transplantation of SGO into wounded mouse salivary glands demonstrated their multipotency and differentiation potential into mature salivary glands. The advantage of the wound model over the irradiated model is the ability to control the wound size and ensure the precise delivery of the hPSC-derived SGO at the desired location. As a result, it is relatively easy to assess cell viability and differentiation potential of human cells in the native microenvironment of the salivary gland. Notably, human iPSC-derived organoids were able to survive, engraft and differentiate into all salivary gland lineages including acinar (positive for AQP5), ductal (positive for KRT7,17/19), and myoepithelial cells (positive for αSMA) in vivo. This result demonstrates the multipotency of day 32 SGO, in agreement with the transcriptomic data. It would be interesting to examine how the stage of cell/organoid differentiation affects engraftment and differentiation in vivo; the long-term fate of human cells in the mouse glandular environment; and whether they contribute to saliva production. These studies may require longer-term in vivo evaluation, during which potential inflammatory responses and fibrotic encapsulation will also be assessed. Given that tumor-derived matrices, such as Matrigel and Cultrex, may elicit host responses including inflammation, foreign body reactions, and tissue remodeling, the development and evaluation of alternative biomaterials with enhanced biocompatibility and translational potential will be an important focus of future work 119 . Finally, the design and adaptation of biomaterial platforms and bioactive cues to promote the neovascularization and innervation of human organoids in vivo will also be explored, building on approaches that we have previously demonstrated to be effective 120 . In summary, our results demonstrate the successful derivation of SGEP cells and salivary gland organoids from hPSC. Human SGO exhibited discernible lumens and salivary function in vitro and could regenerate acinar, ductal and myoepithelial salivary gland cells in vivo upon transplantation into mouse SMG. Such hPSC-derived SGEP and SGOs may be a valuable model for studying gland development and disease and an effective platform for drug screening and testing to prevent xerostomia, as well as for cell therapy approaches for gland regeneration following injury and loss of function. Media optimization Media optimization for the surface ectoderm (SE) stage was done by qPCR analysis for highest expression levels for SE related genes like MSX1/2 16 and p63 17 , while maintaining low expression of embryonic, neural and neural crest associated genes. Hence following this guideline, we optimized concentrations of the growth factor BMP-4 10 , 15 (3.5–35 ng/ml), a known SE inducer and inhibitors like SB431542 10 , 16 (1–10 µM, TGF-β pathway inhibitor) and DAPT 17 , 18 (1–10 µM, Notch pathway inhibitor) that are known to inhibit the mesendoderm development and upregulate the p63 pathway respectively (Fig. S 1 ). Subsequently, the media optimization for the oral ectoderm (OE) was done similarly with target genes being SOX2 10 , 45 and PITX2 10 , 45 , while downregulating embryonic, neural, neural crest and SE related genes. Hence, similarly we optimized concentrations of FGF-2 10 , 44 (5–50 ng/ml), a known inducer of FGFR signaling and LDN193189 10 (50–450 nM, BMP pathway inhibitor) a known inhibitor of dental precursors (Fig. S 2 ). Concomitantly to see the effect of the FGFR signaling on the OE development we inhibited it using SU5402 121 (5 µM) (Fig. S 2 ). Next, to optimize the differentiation towards salivary gland epithelial progenitors (SGEP), we targeted progenitor specific genes like SOX9, FOXC1, p63, Keratin 5 and 14. Here we optimized FGF-7/10 10 , 54 , 65 , 66 (10–100 ng/ml, FGFR2b agonists) and Am580 53 (25–250 nM, RARα agonist) concentrations as they are known salivary gland morphogens and inducers of SOX9, along with perturbation of the Wnt pathway with CHIR99021 56 (0.2–2 µM, Wnt pathway agonist) or Wnt-C59 55 (0.2–2 µM, Wnt pathway inhibitor), as Wnt activity is restricted to the mesenchyme in the developing gland (Fig. S 3 ). For the next stage, organoid formation from the SGEP in Cultrex was key for the media component optimization, the presence of EGF 66 , 122 being critical for organoid formation (Fig. S 4 ). We further defined the media by using combinations of previously optimized concentrations of FGF-10, Am580 and Wnt-C59 to target an upregulation in SOX10 and FOXC1 while downregulating SOX9 (Fig. S 4 ). Similarly, for the further differentiation of the organoids we targeted the same genes as above with the goal of further upregulating SOX10 and FOXC1 while maintaining low levels of SOX9 (Fig. S 4 ). Here we optimized the concentration of HGF 68 , 122 , 123 (10–100 ng/ml, Met receptor agonist) with or without FGF-10. HGF is a growth factor known to be involved in the regulation XBP1/MIST1 and hence aids the maturation of the salispheres. The resulting up- and down-regulation of several notable genes throughout the protocol are included here in the order in which they appear in the paper’s discussion (Fig. S 5 ). Wounded submandibular gland mouse model The submandibular gland (SMG) wound model was created using a method previously reported 141 . Briefly, 12 female 8-week old NU/J mice were anesthetized with isoflurane (2.5%, 100 mL/min), and a 0.5 cm skin incision was made along the anterior surface of the neck. Mouse SMGs were exposed, and the surgical wounds were created using a 3 mm diameter biopsy punch. Next, a 20 μL dome of Cultrex containing D32 SGO as described above was placed into each wound with fresh Cultrex injected to fill the remaining empty space. An aluminum plate was placed underneath for 15 s to prevent hydrogel leakage. Finally, the skin incision was sutured, and the mice were housed in cages in a room with a controlled environment for a period of 1 month. All animal usage, anesthesia, and treatments were conducted in compliance with ARRIVE guidelines and approved by the University of Missouri-Columbia Animal Care and Use Committee (ACUC). SMG collection The mice were euthanized in a CO 2 chamber at 2- and 4-weeks post-treatment, followed by exsanguination through incision of the abdominal aorta for tissue collection. Then, collected SMGs were visualized for red fluorescence using GelDoc Imaging System to briefly confirm the presence of injected cells. SMGs without cell injection were used to compare and subtract the endogenous fluorescence signal. SMGs were then fixed in 10% formalin at room temperature overnight, dehydrated in a 70% ethanol solution, embedded in paraffin wax, and cut into 5 μm sections. The survival and differentiation status of injected cells were assessed by mCherry fluorescence and visualized using a Stellaris 5 confocal microscope (Leica). Statistics and reproducibility Statistical significance was determined by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test for multiple comparisons. Statistical significance between two groups were calculated using student’s t-test. All experiments were repeated 3 times, yielding similar results. All the data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 8 and Microsoft 365 Excel. All the immunofluorescence and PCR experiments were performed independently at least three times with reproducible results. No statistical method was used to pre-determine sample size. The experiments were not randomized. The investigators were not blinded to allocation during the experiments or outcome assessment.
