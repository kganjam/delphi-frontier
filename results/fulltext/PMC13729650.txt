Introduction The cilium is a microtubule-based organelle that extends from the surface of many eukaryotic cells and is commonly found in almost all vertebrate cells [ 1 – 3 ]. Although cilia were first described over a century ago, their full range of functions remained unclear until the early 2000s, when it was discovered that they play key roles in various signaling processes, particularly in development [ 4 , 5 ]. These specialized structures are classified into primary and motile cilia, each contributing uniquely to cellular function. Primary cilia are antenna-like structures that detect and transmit extracellular signals, playing fundamental roles in embryonic patterning and organogenesis. They act as sensory organelles, mediating key signaling pathways such as Hedgehog (Hh), Wnt, and PDGF, which are critical for proper cellular differentiation and development. Defects in primary cilia function can lead to inherited developmental disorders known as ciliopathies, which arise from mutations in genes encoding ciliary proteins. These ciliopathies include conditions such as polycystic kidney disease, Bardet-Biedl syndrome, and Joubert syndrome, highlighting the essential role of primary cilia in human health [ 6 , 7 ]. Motile cilia are miniature, hair-like organelles whose beating generates a directional fluid flow [ 8 ]. The flow generated by ciliated epithelia is of great interest, as defective ciliary motility results in severe human diseases called motile ciliopathies [ 9 ]. Motile cilia at the left-right organizer (LRO) generate leftward extracellular fluid flow, which is crucial for the asymmetric gene expression that establishes left-right (LR) asymmetry during embryo development [ 10 ]. Breaking of LR symmetry takes place at the LRO, a Kupffer’s vesicle (KV) of zebrafish [ 11 ], a gastrocoel roof plate (GRP) of Xenopus [ 12 ], and a node of mammals [ 13 ]. In the mouse embryo node, motile cilia at the center generate a leftward fluid flow that establishes LR asymmetry. Meanwhile, peripherally located primary cilia detect and transduce this flow-mediated signal to coordinate cellular responses [ 14 , 15 ]. The Hh signaling pathway was discovered as a key pathway in embryonic patterning and development. In mammals, almost all cells possess a single primary cilium that houses the components of the Hedgehog signaling pathway [ 16 ]. Recent studies have revealed that cilia play essential roles in facilitating the proper transduction of Shh signals by serving as a platform for the localization and activation of key pathway components, including Smoothened (Smo), Gli transcription factors [ 17 ] and the intracellular Ca 2+ signaling [ 18 , 19 ]. Ca 2+ signaling is one of the most evolutionarily conserved and ubiquitous signaling mechanisms [ 20 ]. Moreover, Ca 2+ signaling plays a vital role in various developmental processes, such as pattern formation, axis specification, and organogenesis. During pattern formation, calcium waves guide cellular differentiation and tissue morphogenesis by coordinating the spatiotemporal activation of signaling molecules [ 21 ]. In axis specification, localized Ca 2+ signaling helps establish embryonic polarity and symmetry breaking, which is crucial for LR asymmetry and dorsal-ventral axis formation [ 22 , 23 ]. During organogenesis, transient Ca 2+ oscillations regulate the activity of transcription factors and signaling pathways, ensuring the proper development of organs, such as the heart and brain. These processes underscore the importance of calcium signaling as a central regulator of embryonic development [ 24 ]. The Ca 2+ concentrations in primary [ 25 ] and motile cilia [ 26 ] have been higher than in the cytoplasm. The elevated Ca 2+ concentrations in both cilia suggest that these organelles serve as specialized microdomains for calcium signaling, potentially allowing for more precise and localized regulation of signaling pathways compared to the surrounding cytoplasm. Despite this observation, much remains to be uncovered about how cilia regulate Ca 2+ dynamics and how these processes influence cellular signaling and development. Several ciliary Ca 2+ channels have been studied, such as polycystin-1,2 (PC1,2) [ 27 , 28 ] and transient receptor potential channels (TRPC) [ 29 , 30 ]. Nevertheless, a complete understanding of the Ca 2+ influx and signaling pathways through these channels has not yet been achieved. Store-operated Ca 2+ entry (SOCE) is a central Ca 2+ entry mechanism activated by the depletion of the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) Ca 2+ stores [ 31 , 32 ]. STIM1 is a single-transmembrane Ca 2+ sensor located in the ER [ 33 ], whereas Orai1 is a plasma membrane-located Ca 2+ channel that harbors the pore subunit of SOCE [ 34 ]. SOCE modulates the activity of various Ca 2+ -dependent transcriptional pathways, including a nuclear factor of activated T cells (NFAT) [ 35 ]. NFAT plays a regulatory role in several developmental processes, such as forming a complex nervous system, a recombinational immune system, and a vascular system with a complex heart [ 36 ]. However, whether SOCE and NFAT contribute to ciliogenesis or cilia-mediated signaling during embryonic development remains unclear. We hypothesized that Orai1, a key component of SOCE, may regulate ciliary Ca 2+ signaling and thereby influence ciliogenesis and cilia-dependent developmental processes. In this study, we examined the role of Orai1 in regulating ciliary Ca 2+ dynamics and ciliogenesis. We also investigated how Orai1 influences signaling pathways that govern vertebrate embryonic development and the establishment of left-right asymmetry. Results Orai1 localizes at the periciliary membrane Orai1 is the pore-forming subunit of SOC channels and a key regulator of cellular Ca 2+ homeostasis at the plasma membrane. Despite this critical role, studies on Orai1-mediated SOCE in cilia remain underexplored, and whether Orai1 localizes to cilia has not been examined. Several Ca 2+ channels have been identified in the ciliary membrane after ciliogenesis, prompting us to investigate the localization of Orai1 in various ciliated cell lines. Before and after 24 h of serum starvation, hTERT-immortalized Retinal Pigment Epithelial cells (hTERT RPE-1) were immunolabeled with anti-acetylated α-tubulin and anti-Orai1 antibodies. Interestingly, Orai1 was consistently detected as a single cluster prior to serum starvation. Upon serum starvation-induced ciliogenesis, these clusters became markedly intensified and were specifically enriched at the ciliary base (Fig. 1 A). A similar pattern was reproducibly observed in other ciliated cell types, including NIH-3T3 fibroblasts (Fig. 1 B), IMCD3 kidney epithelial cells commonly used for cilia research (Fig. 1 C), and MCF10A breast epithelial cells characterized by abundant primary cilia (Fig. 1 D). In all these ciliated cell types, Orai1 clusters exhibited increased intensity following cilia formation (Fig. 1 E). To clarify whether the observed changes in Orai1 distribution were a result of cilia formation rather than serum starvation, we used the MCF7 cell line, which is known to inherently lack cilia. Surprisingly, Orai1 clusters were not present in MCF7 cells before serum starvation, and, notably, no changes in Orai1 localization were observed even after serum starvation (Fig. 1 F). To precisely determine the localization of the Orai1 cluster near the ciliary base, we employed Structured Illumination Microscopy (SIM) combined with Wheat Germ Agglutinin (WGA)-488 as a plasma membrane marker. Imaris was used for 3D reconstruction to assess the spatial arrangement of Orai1 relative to the cilium (Fig. 1 G). SIM imaging revealed that Orai1 is specifically localized at the periciliary membrane, distinct from other ciliary Ca 2+ channels previously reported [ 37 , 38 ]. This unique positioning is consistent with the directionality of ciliary Ca 2+ influx from base to tip, as described in earlier studies (Fig. 1 H). H Schematic diagram of cilia and Orai1 Ciliary Ca 2+ influx is induced by SOCE activation from the cilia basement to the tip Orai1-mediated SOCE is a well-established regulator of local Ca 2+ concentration. We observed that Orai1 is localized to the periciliary membrane. These findings prompted us to examine SOCE-mediated Ca 2+ influx in ciliated cells. Ciliary Ca 2+ concentration was measured using 5HT 6 -Cherry-GECO1.0, a cilia-specific Ca 2+ indicator [ 39 ]. 5-Hydroxytryptamine receptor isoform 6 (5HT 6 ) is a transmembrane protein that localizes primarily to primary cilia via a ciliary targeting sequence, ensuring that the fused GECO1.0 reporter accurately reports Ca 2+ dynamics within the cilium [ 40 ]. We induced cilia formation through serum starvation after expressing this ciliary Ca 2+ indicator in hTERT RPE-1 cells. Before serum starvation, this indicator was expressed at the plasma membrane. It was detected within the cilia upon serum starvation, and the ciliary Ca 2+ concentrations were confirmed to be higher than those in the cytosol, as visualized through fluorescent imaging (Fig. 2 A). Next, we conducted time-lapse Ca 2+ imaging to determine whether SOCE regulates the rise in ciliary Ca 2+ concentration. Store depletion was induced in 0 mM Ca 2+ Tyrode’s solution with Thapsigargin (TG) treatment, followed by SOCE activation by adding 10 mM Ca 2+ Tyrode’s solution. Using the Ca 2+ indicator, we observed a significant rise in ciliary Ca 2+ levels following SOCE activation. This response was confirmed to be SOCE-mediated, as it was suppressed by applying the SOCE inhibitor 2APB ( n = 12, Fig. 2 B and C). This finding enhances our understanding of calcium signaling within cilia and suggests that SOCE is critical in regulating ciliary function and related biological processes. Furthermore, the observed increase in cytosolic Ca 2+ near cilia further supports a model in which Orai1 at the periciliary membrane acts as a central hub, facilitating Ca 2+ influx into both the cilium and its adjacent cytosolic compartment. We aimed to examine the entry point and dynamics of Ca 2+ influx contributing to the rise in ciliary Ca 2+ concentration induced by SOCE. The identified ciliary Ca 2+ channels are distributed throughout the entire cilia body, which does not explain the previously known Ca 2+ movement from the base to the tip of the cilia. To observe the SOCE-mediated ciliary Ca 2+ flow direction, we recorded high-frequency images at 100 Hz with precise resolution. Store depletion was induced in 0 mM Ca 2+ Tyrode’s solution with TG treatment, and SOCE was then triggered by adding 10 mM Ca 2+ Tyrode’s solution, allowing us to monitor SOCE progression in real-time. The migration of ciliary Ca 2+ began within one second of introducing 10 mM Ca 2+ Tyrode’s solution into SOCE-activated hTERT RPE-1 cells and reached the tip of the cilium after approximately 10 s (Fig. 2 D). These results represent the first measurement of ciliary Ca 2+ influx mediated by SOCE, demonstrating that the influx direction from the base to the tip of the cilium is consistent with previously established findings. Consistent with this, cytosolic Ca 2+ imaging with cyto-RGECO in SMO-GFP-RPE1 cells (Fig. S1A) revealed that SOCE preferentially elevates basal cytosolic Ca 2+ levels near the ciliary base relative to distal cytosolic regions, with higher Ca 2+ concentrations observed at the ciliary base (Fig. S1B and C). These findings suggest a model in which Orai1-mediated local Ca 2+ increases may facilitate Ca 2+ influx into the cilium. To determine whether SOCE contributes to ciliary Ca 2+ influx under physiological conditions, we stimulated 5HT6-Cherry-GECO-expressing hTERT RPE-1 cells with fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Fig. S1D). FBS stimulation induced a substantial increase in ciliary Ca 2+ concentration. Importantly, the FBS-induced increase in ciliary Ca 2+ concentration was significantly attenuated by the SOCE inhibitors 2APB and YM58483, demonstrating that SOCE mediates calcium influx into primary cilia under physiological stimulation (Fig. S1E and F). These results suggest that physiological stimuli, such as serum exposure, can modulate ciliary calcium levels through SOCE, thereby influencing ciliary signaling and downstream calcium-dependent cellular processes. Scale bar, 2 μm Orai1 contributes to the regulation of RFX3 and FOXJ1, transcription factors involved in ciliogenesis Having confirmed that SOCE mediates ciliary Ca 2+ dynamics, we next asked whether it also contributes to the formation of cilia. Various Ca 2+ channels have been reported to influence ciliogenesis, underscoring the importance of Ca 2+ signaling in cilia-related processes. However, despite the established role of SOCE in maintaining intracellular Ca 2+ homeostasis, its contribution to cilia formation remains poorly understood. To investigate the role of Orai1 in ciliogenesis, we generated Orai1 knock-out (KO) cells. Orai1 fl/fl mice (The Jackson Laboratory strains 036232) [ 41 , 42 ] were crossed with UBC-CreERT2 mice (The Jackson Laboratory strains 007001). Mouse embryonic fibroblast (MEF) cells derived from the Orai1 fl/fl ; UBC-CreERT2 mice (Fig. S2A) were treated with 4-hydroxytamoxifen (4-OHT), resulting in the generation of Orai1 KO MEF cells (Fig. S2B and C). Efficient Orai1 deletion was confirmed by immunocytochemistry and immunoblotting (Fig. S2D-F). The successful suppression of Orai1 activity was further verified through Ca 2+ imaging (Fig. S2G). These cells were then used for downstream experiments. Cilia formation was induced by culturing Orai1 KO and CTL MEFs in a serum-free medium for 24 h (Fig. 3 A). Intriguingly, we observed a significant decrease in the proportion of Orai1 KO MEFs with cilia compared to the CTL MEFs. In Orai1 KO MEFs, only 57% of the cells exhibited cilia formation after 24 h of serum starvation, compared to 87% in the CTL MEFs (Fig. 3 B). Cilia length was also significantly reduced in Orai1 KO MEFs compared to CTL MEFs (CTL = 2.01 μm, Orai1 KO = 1.36 μm average cilia length) (Fig. 3 C). Based on these results, we hypothesized that Orai1 is required for ciliogenesis. To assess the importance of Orai1-dependent SOCE in ciliogenesis, rather than its other non-channel function, we treated Orai1 inhibitors during 24 h of serum starvation at hTERT RPE-1 (Fig. 3 D and E). The percentage of ciliated hTERT RPE-1 cells was 58.9% in the control group, whereas cells treated with 2APB, SKF96365 , and YM58483, known Orai1 channel inhibitors, showed significantly lower ciliation rates of 27.4%, 33.9%, and 31.8%, respectively (Fig. 3 F). Furthermore, cells treated with inhibitors exhibited a significantly shorter average cilia length of approximately 1.7 μm, whereas CTL cells had an average length of 1.9 μm (Fig. 3 G). To define the role of SOCE in ciliogenesis while minimizing non-specific effects on other Ca 2+ channels, we conducted experiments using hTERT RPE-1 cells transiently expressing dominant-negative (DN) Orai1 (R91W) [ 43 ] and STIM1 (ΔCAD) [ 44 ] (Fig. 3 H). Because STIM1 activates Orai1 in response to ER Ca 2+ depletion, we hypothesized that STIM1 also contributes to ciliogenesis. To test this, we employed loss-of-function mutants of Orai1 and STIM1, which impair SOCE without affecting their expression or localization. This strategy enabled a direct assessment of how SOCE inhibition influences ciliogenesis. The percentage of ciliated cells decreased from over 60% in the control to less than 30% in those expressing DN Orai1 (R91W), DN STIM1 (ΔCAD), or both, in hTERT RPE-1 cells (Fig. 3 I). Moreover, the average length of cilia was significantly reduced in cells expressing DN SOCE components (CTL GFP = 2.47 μm, DN Orai1 (R91W) = 2.00 μm, DN STIM1 (ΔCAD) = 2.03 μm, and both = 1.98 μm average cilia length) (Fig. 3 J). Co-expression of DN Orai1 and DN STIM1 did not further reduce ciliogenesis efficiency, indicating that Orai1 and STIM1 converge on the shared SOCE-regulated pathway. RFX3 and FOXJ1 are essential transcription factors that regulate ciliogenesis. Loss of their function reduces the transcription of many ciliary genes, resulting in ciliogenesis defects [ 45 , 46 ]. Given the essential roles of RFX3 and FOXJ1 in ciliogenesis, we tested whether their expression is regulated by SOCE. At hTERT RPE-1 cells, both RFX3 and FOXJ1 increased following TG treatment during serum starvation (Fig. 3 K and L). To confirm that this effect is mediated specifically by SOCE activation, NIH-3T3 cells were incubated in either standard or Ca 2+ -free medium, thereby blocking SOCE-mediated Ca 2+ influx. As expected, the mRNA levels of RFX3 and FOXJ1 upregulated by SOCE activation did not increase in the absence of Ca 2+ in the culture medium (Fig. 3 M and N). Consistently, pharmacological inhibition of SOCE during serum starvation in NIH-3T3 cells resulted in a slight reduction in RFX3 mRNA levels but a significant decrease in FOXJ1 expression (Fig. S3A and B). Similarly, Orai1 KO MEF cells exhibited no significant change in RFX3 expression, whereas FOXJ1 levels were significantly reduced following serum starvation compared to control cells (Fig. S3C and D). These results suggest a role for SOCE in regulating ciliogenesis at the transcriptional level. Based on our findings, the presence and activity of Orai1, particularly its role in SOCE-mediated Ca 2+ uptake, appear to contribute to ciliogenesis. Summarizing so far, Orai1 at the periciliary membrane acts as a central hub, facilitating Ca 2+ influx into the cilium and adjacent cytosol. SOCE is also required for the mRNA expression of key cilia regulators, linking calcium signaling to gene regulation during ciliogenesis. Together, these findings demonstrate that Orai1-mediated SOCE plays a critical role in both calcium dynamics and the transcriptional control underlying cilia formation. Dysfunctional SOCE inhibits ciliogenesis in the Xenopus embryo and induces the destruction of LR asymmetry To extend our findings to a physiological context, we aimed to validate the role of SOCE in ciliogenesis using an in vivo model. This approach allows examination of calcium signaling in developing tissues, providing insight into its broader impact on ciliogenesis and tissue development. Primary cilia are key sensors for various signaling pathways, while motile cilia are vital for driving fluid and cell movement. Both types of cilia rely on precise calcium signaling for their functions. For instance, in primary cilia, calcium is essential for mediating mechanosensing and cell polarity during embryonic development, which affects signal transmission [ 47 ]. Meanwhile, motile cilia utilize calcium signaling to regulate their beating pattern, which is crucial for fluid transport in tissues such as the respiratory tract and the reproductive system [ 8 ]. Therefore, investigating the impact of SOCE on ciliary function is crucial for understanding how calcium signaling supports the distinct and complementary roles of both primary and motile cilia. In Xenopus , one of the well-studied types of cilia is the motile cilia found on the surface of the early embryo’s multiciliated cells (MCCs) [ 48 ]. To assess the necessity of SOCE in motile cilia formation and its role, we first observed the changes in Xenopus MCCs using the SOCE inhibitor, 2APB. Xenopus embryos were exposed to 2APB (5, 25 µM) from stages 20 to 28, during which multiciliated cells are formed. Although 2APB can activate SOCE at lower concentrations and inhibit it at higher concentrations, 5 µM was insufficient to inhibit SOCE in Xenopus , as no significant inhibition was observed at this concentration. In contrast, embryos cultured with the higher concentration of 2APB (25 µM), known for its SOCE inhibitory effect, exhibited developmental malformation phenotypes. These included defective head and tail formation, as well as a markedly smaller size compared to the CTL embryos (Fig. 4 A). We fixed embryos at stage 26 ~ 28 and stained them with an acetylated α-tubulin antibody to visualize multicilia on MCCs (Fig. 4 B). Notably, the MCCs of Xenopus epithelial cells significantly decreased at high concentrations of 2APB (Fig. 4 C). To address the issue of the non-specific inhibition of channels other than Orai1 by 2APB, we decided to pursue an alternative approach. This decision was also motivated by the potential cytotoxicity and pleiotropic effects commonly associated with chemical inhibitors, particularly at high 2APB concentrations. To confirm Orai1-specific effects independent of potential 2APB off-target activity, we examined the multicilia on MCCs in embryos injected with Xenopus DN Orai1 (R65W), which was designed based on its alignment with human Orai1 (R91W). Injections were targeted to the ventral-animal site of the blastomeres at the 2- or 4-cell stages to target MCC tissues and further investigate the functional role of Orai1 in regulating cilia formation. The MCCs of Xenopus epithelial cells exhibited a dose-dependent decrease upon DN Orai1 injection (Fig. 4 D and E). To further validate these results, we performed additional experiments using Orai1 morpholino (MO) and WT Orai1 mRNA injection. The efficiency of Orai1 knockdown by MO was confirmed (Fig. S4A). While WT Orai1 injection did not significantly affect MCC formation, Orai1 MO injection resulted in a marked reduction of MCCs, comparable to the phenotype observed with DN Orai1 (Fig. S4B and C). This study reveals that Orai1 is important not only for primary cilia formation but also for motile cilia. Importantly, our findings reveal that Orai1 is crucial for embryonic development in vertebrates, including epithelial cilia formation. It emphasizes that SOCE, mediated by Orai1, is essential for regulating both primary and motile cilia, offering new insights into its broader impact on ciliary function and developmental processes. Next, we examined the role of Orai1 in nodal cilia on the left-right organizer (LRO). The LRO, a mono-ciliated epithelium, generates asymmetric fluid flow essential for inducing left-right (LR) body axis asymmetry [ 10 ]. In Xenopus , the GRP functions as the LRO, where nodal cilia generate leftward fluid flow and establish proper body patterning and organ development along with the left-right axis. Thus, disruption of ciliary function in the GRP can lead to abnormal LR asymmetry, resulting in defects in organ positioning and morphology, underscoring the importance of cilia in early developmental processes. To target the GRP of Xenopus laevis embryos, we injected DN Orai1 into each dorsal-vegetal site of the blastomeres at 2- or 4-cell stages. Embryos were fixed at stage 17 and stained with an acetylated α-tubulin antibody to label nodal cilia in GRP tissue. Notably, the GRP cilia in embryos injected with DN Orai1 were significantly shorter, averaging approximately 5.43 μm, compared to 7 μm in the CTL embryos (Fig. 4 F and G). We also observed the expression of an early marker of global LR patterning, the transcription factor PITX2. PITX2 is induced by nodal signaling on the left lateral-plate mesoderm (LPM) of tadpoles, while its expression is inhibited on the right side [ 49 ]. PITX2 expression in stage 28 tadpoles (before heart-tube looping) was assayed by whole-mount in situ hybridization (WMISH) [ 50 ]. DN Orai1 was injected at doses that minimally disrupted anterior-posterior and dorsal-ventral development at this stage. In all 71 control-injected embryos, PITX2 displayed left LPM-specific expression. Surprisingly, among tadpoles injected with DN Orai1, 22 out of 68 exhibited abnormal PITX2 transcripts (Fig. 4 H and I). Heart looping is one of the most noticeable morphogenetic events that is influenced by the proper LR asymmetries. During early development, the initially straight embryonic heart tube transforms into a helically wound structure, forming a D-loop, which represents the normal rightward looping. DN Orai1 was injected at the 2-cell stage, and embryos were raised to stage 45, where heart-looping was assessed. Approximately 30–40% of DN Orai1-injected embryos displayed abnormal heart looping, presenting L-loops and indicating disrupted LR patterning. These defects in heart looping increased in a dose-dependent manner following DN Orai1 injection (Fig. 4 J and K). In contrast, WT Orai1 injection did not affect heart looping, indicating no significant impact on LR patterning (Fig. S5D). Overall, these results highlight the significance of Orai1-mediated SOCE in regulating nodal cilia formation, which is crucial for establishing proper LR asymmetry during embryonic development. Orai1 increased Gli1 and PTCH1, Sonic Hedgehog signaling molecules We previously confirmed that SOCE activation increases the expression of the ciliogenesis regulator genes RFX3 and FOXJ1. While this highlights its role in ciliogenesis, it remained unclear whether SOCE also affects ciliary signaling in functionally competent cilia. To specifically assess effects on ciliary signaling, chemical treatments were applied after ciliogenesis had been completed. Accordingly, we induced ciliogenesis in NIH-3T3 cells by serum starvation for 21 h and subsequently activated SOCE through a 3 h TG treatment on the preformed cilia. We then assessed the expression levels of cilia-related genes using a SYBR green-based quantitative real-time RT2 PCR array. Our analysis revealed that 25 genes were upregulated by more than 2-fold, while 3 genes were downregulated by more than 2-fold following TG-induced SOCE activation (Fig. 5 A and B). The list of differentially expressed genes identified is shown in Table 1 (Supplementary data). To further evaluate the functional relevance of these transcriptional changes, we performed pathway enrichment analysis using ShinyGO. This analysis revealed significant enrichment of Hedgehog signaling pathways (Fig. 5 C). These findings suggest that SOCE influences cilia signaling as well as cilia formation. Many upregulated cilia-related genes in this PCR array were associated with cancer pathways. However, our focus was on developmental processes in this study. Previous studies have revealed that the primary cilium functions as a signaling compartment for calcium in the Shh pathway, a critical aspect of ciliary signaling essential for embryonic development in vertebrates [ 51 ]. Therefore, we specifically targeted genes related to Shh signaling. Among these, we concentrated on Gli1 (GLI Family Zinc Finger 1, 3.74-fold) and PTCH1 (Patched 1, 2.81-fold) [ 52 ], key components of the Shh pathway, which are also known to be direct targets for Shh signaling. Based on these results, we sought to determine how SOCE influences Shh signaling under conditions where the pathway is activated by the smoothened agonist (SAG). To this end, Gli1 and PTCH1 mRNA levels were quantified in NIH-3T3 cells by real-time PCR. Shh activation was induced with 0.1 µM SAG for 24 h of serum starvation. To test whether SOCE-regulated Shh signaling involves this pathway, we treated cells with calcineurin inhibitor, Cyclosporin A (CsA), after ciliogenesis and examined the expression of Shh target genes induced by SAG (Fig. 6 A). Because NFAT functions as a transcription factor, we asked whether its inhibition would alter the transcription of these genes. Indeed, mRNA levels of Gli1 (~ 70%) and PTCH1 (~ 75%) were significantly reduced after 6 h of CsA treatment following 24 h of serum starvation with SAG (Fig. 6 B and C). CsA treatment after ciliogenesis did not affect cilia incidence or length (Fig. S5G-I), indicating that the transcriptional changes are not due to alterations in these parameters. These results suggest that NFAT acts as a transcriptional regulator in Shh signaling and may contribute to developmental regulation. We performed dual luciferase reporter assays to investigate whether NFAT1 can regulate Gli1 gene expression. Using the prediction program ALGGEN PROMO, which references the TRANSFAC database, we aimed to identify NFAT1 binding sites within the − 559 to + 6 bp region of the Gli1 promoter. The mouse Gli1 promoter sequence was obtained from the National Center for Biotechnology Information (GenBank). Following the analysis of 565 bp DNA sequences (from − 559 to + 6 bp) of the Gli1 promoter region, three putative NFAT1 binding sequences were identified (Fig. 6 D). We cloned the sequence from − 559 to + 6 bp relative to the transcription-starting site of the Gli1 gene using genomic DNA extracted from MEFs. These sequences were cloned into the Firefly luciferase (pGL4.10) reporter construct to test their effect on luciferase expression and determine the region responsible for basal promoter activity. Firefly luciferase activity of the reporter plasmid was normalized to Renilla luciferase (pRL-TK) activity, and an empty pGL4.10-Basic vector was used as a negative control. These constructs are transiently expressed in HEK 293T cells. The − 559 bp construct showed a 2-fold increase at the SOCE-activated condition (0.1 µM TG) and a 2-fold decrease at the NFAT pathway-inhibited condition (0.1 µM CsA) in promoter activity compared with the CTL condition. In contrast, no significant differences were detected at the pGL4.10-Basic transiently expressed cells (Fig. 6 E). Consistent results were also obtained in NIH-3T3 cells, a cilia-relevant model, where co-expression with NFAT1 further increased luciferase activity (Fig. S6A). To identify the functional NFAT1 binding site, we generated mutant promoter-luciferase constructs. Point mutations were introduced at Mut − 359 bp (GGAAA → GGAcc), Mut − 184 bp (TTTCC → ccTCC), and Mut − 34 bp (TTTCC → ggTCC). In the Mut − 184 bp and Mut − 34 bp constructs, the 3-fold increase in luciferase activity observed in the wild-type promoter under TG stimulation was significantly reduced (Fig. 6 F). Throughout these experiments, Renilla expression levels remained consistent, confirming the reliability of our data. Our data show that NFAT1 regulates Gli1 transcription through direct promoter binding, enhancing its expression. To validate NFAT1 function in Gli1 expression in vivo, we performed the luciferase assay in Xenopus embryos. In Xenopus , the NFAT1-dependent activation observed in the wild-type promoter was abolished in the Mut − 184 bp and Mut − 34 bp constructs, consistent with the results in mammalian cells (Fig. 6 G). To further confirm the direct binding of NFAT1 to the Shh target gene, a CUT&amp;RUN-based PCR assay was performed to determine whether NFAT1 is enriched at the Gli1 promoter. The CUT&amp;RUN technique enables the detection of protein enrichment at accessible chromatin regions. According to the manufacturer’s protocol (EpiCypher) and previously reported methods [ 54 ], experiments were performed using NFAT1-GFP transfected MEF cells or wild-type controls. An anti-GFP antibody was used as the primary antibody to bind with NFAT1, followed by pA/G-MNase-mediated cleavage of genomic DNA at sites bound by the antibody. The amplified DNA fragments were subjected to PCR using primer pairs targeting each NFAT1-binding region within the Gli1 promoter (Fig. 6 H). The CUT&amp;RUN-PCR assay demonstrated enrichment of NFAT1 at the Gli1 promoter specifically in NFAT1-GFP transfected MEFs when using an anti-GFP antibody, compared with pA/G-MNase-only controls (Fig. 6 I). A similar enrichment pattern was observed by real-time PCR using the same samples and primer sets. These results indicate that NFAT1 directly associates with these regions in the genomic context (Fig. 6 J). Consistently, comparable enrichment patterns were also obtained using an anti-NFAT1 antibody (Fig. S6B and C), providing additional evidence for the specificity of NFAT1 binding to the Gli1 promoter. Discussion Store-operated Ca 2+ entry (SOCE) has traditionally been studied in the context of immune signaling and general Ca 2+ homeostasis; however, our research provides several critical insights into the role of SOCE in ciliogenesis and embryonic development through ciliary signaling. Our findings reconcile divergent perspectives on the physiological function of Orai1-mediated SOCE by showing that Orai1 localizes to the periciliary membrane, thereby providing a structural and functional basis for Ca 2+ entry into the ciliated cells. This localization implicates regulating ciliary Ca 2+ dynamics and raises the possibility that SOCE contributes to ciliogenesis through this mechanism. In addition, the periciliary membrane, also referred to as the ciliary diffusion barrier, is surrounded by the pericentriolar material (PCM). This region is enriched with proteins involved in Ca 2+ regulation. PCM includes calmodulin, a multifunctional Ca 2+ -binding protein, centrins, calmodulin-like proteins associated with centrosomes, and members of the CEP family, which localize to the basal body and play critical roles in ciliogenesis, ciliary function, and Ca 2+ regulation [ 55 ]. Within this context, the presence of Orai1 at the periciliary membrane highlights its potential role in shaping Ca 2+ dynamics essential for proper ciliary function. We observed that SOCE contributes to Ca 2+ influx into ciliated cells. While previous studies have associated ciliary Ca 2+ channels to flow-mediated signaling [ 37 ], recent evidence suggests that Ca 2+ may rapidly move from the cytosol into the cilium with directional flow, instead of depending only on channels within the cilia body [ 56 ]. The Orai1-mediated ciliary Ca 2+ elevation we observed may account for this phenomenon and influence signaling pathways involved in developmental processes. However, the precise mechanisms and downstream consequences of Orai1-mediated ciliary Ca 2+ entry remain unclear, highlighting the need for further investigation. We also demonstrated that SOCE contributes to ciliogenesis by regulating the expression of the ciliary master regulators RFX3 and FOXJ1. Notably, SOCE activation increased the expression of both factors, whereas SOCE inhibition resulted in a significant reduction in FOXJ1 expression, with only modest changes observed in RFX3 levels. Given that RFX3 is a key regulator of primary cilia formation, its relatively stable expression may indicate a requirement for maintaining the basal transcriptional programs. Accordingly, such findings underscore that defects in Ca 2+ signaling are not merely supportive abnormalities but can directly influence the transcriptional architecture of ciliogenesis, providing a potential explanation for the profound ciliary and developmental phenotypes associated with SOCE dysfunction. Nevertheless, the precise mechanisms by which SOCE regulates RFX3 and FOXJ1 expression remain to be elucidated and require additional studies. Furthermore, SOCE-mediated ciliogenesis was closely linked to developmental outcomes in Xenopus embryos. Suppression of Orai1 or STIM1 produced pronounced defects in LR asymmetry, manifested as abnormal right-sided PITX2 expression and aberrant heart looping. These phenotypes directly reflect impaired ciliary function, suggesting that SOCE-dependent calcium signaling is crucial for the structural and functional integrity of cilia during early development. By influencing calcium dynamics, SOCE appears to coordinate key developmental processes, ensuring proper LR patterning and organogenesis. Collectively, these findings highlight the indispensable role of functional SOCE in linking ciliary signaling to embryonic morphogenesis and in safeguarding against developmental abnormalities. Given the importance of Ca 2+ as a secondary messenger in multiple signaling pathways, and based on our results, we aimed to explore whether SOCE-mediated Ca 2+ influx is involved in ciliogenesis and ciliary signaling within functional cilia. Indeed, SOCE appears to function as a positive intracellular regulator of Shh signaling, a principal pathway in ciliary signaling. Our findings demonstrate that SOCE-activated NFAT1 functions as a transcription factor for Gli1, the principal transcriptional mediator of Shh signaling. These outcomes underscore the essential role of SOCE in modulating this pathway. Although the − 359 bp site of the Gli1 promoter was initially considered a candidate NFAT1 binding motif, mutations at this region (GGAAA → GGAcc or aaAAA) unexpectedly abolished luciferase activity. This result may be influenced by secondary structural alterations in the promoter sequence that could affect reporter expression. Notably, however, this site showed clear enrichment in the CUT&amp;RUN assay, supporting its potential involvement in NFAT1 binding. Therefore, while its functional contribution could not be conclusively determined by the luciferase assay alone, these findings suggest that this site may still play a role in Gli1 promoter regulation. The in vivo consequences of disrupted SOCE function were evident as Shh signaling was compromised. This perturbation could lead to significant defects in tissue patterning and organ development, highlighting the essential role of SOCE in regulating Shh-dependent developmental processes. Collectively, our observations provide new insights into the role of SOCE in ciliogenesis and establish the first evidence of a functional connection between Shh signaling and SOCE. Our study has limitations. In this study, we employed an Orai1 floxed mouse line (JAX:036232), which has been previously reported and characterized [ 41 , 42 ], to achieve widespread tamoxifen-inducible deletion of Orai1. Based on reports that constitutive whole-body Orai1 knock-out mice can survive for a limited period after birth (approximately 0.5–1.5 days), we initially aimed to induce Orai1 deletion during critical windows of Shh signaling. We then sought to examine the consequences of SOCE dysfunction throughout embryogenesis until birth, including left-right asymmetry and other Shh-dependent patterning processes. However, using the Orai1 fl/fl ; UBC-CreERT2 model, we found that ubiquitous deletion of Orai1 resulted in severe developmental failure, with embryos rarely maintained beyond mid-gestation and failing to develop normally past approximately E12.5. Because Orai1 deletion was induced in both embryonic and maternal tissues, pregnancy could not be sustained, thereby precluding analysis of both cilia-dependent patterning events, such as LR asymmetry, and later developmental processes such as limb bud maturation, late organogenesis, and perinatal development. Although it was reported that approximately 30% of constitutive whole-body Orai1 knock-out mice (JAX:023352) survived up to 90 days, this survival was observed only after extensive backcrossing to the ICR strain. In contrast, whole-body Orai1 knock-out mice on a C57BL/6 background were embryonically lethal or survived for only one day after birth [ 57 ]. These findings indicate that sustained viability in the absence of Orai1 is restricted to permissive strain backgrounds and underscore a critical requirement for Orai1 during development. Future research should focus on the molecular mechanisms by which SOCE regulates cilia formation and Shh signaling, and the potential for therapeutic interventions targeting SOCE. Such investigations could lead to new strategies for addressing developmental disorders, particularly dysregulation of the Shh pathway and ciliopathies. Our findings emphasize the pivotal role of SOCE in both cilia formation and Shh signaling, underscoring its significance in understanding and treating developmental disorders linked to ciliary dysfunction and the associated complications of ciliopathies. Cilia-induced cells were fixed in 4 % paraformaldehyde for 10 min at room temperature, washed three times with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), permeabilized in 0.2 % Triton X-100 for 10 min at room temperature, washed with PBS three times, blocked in 3 % BSA at room temperature for 2 h, and incubated with primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight. The cells were washed three times with PBS, stained with secondary antibodies at room temperature for 1 h and mounted with Aqua polyMount solution (Polysciences) at room temperature. Cloning information and transcription The full-length sequences of the Xenopus laevis Orai1 gene were obtained from the Xenbase database. Total Xenopus laevis RNA was purified from stage 25 and 35 embryos, and it was reported that Orai1 was highly expressed. Next, we reverse-transcribed cDNA using GoScript™ Reverse Transcriptase (Promega, WI, USA) with a random hexa-primer mix (NEB, MA, USA). We designed the primers to include a restriction enzyme site for cloning, as shown in Table 2 (Supplementary data). Amplified Xenopus laevis Orai1 PCR products were cloned into the CS108 vector and were fused to flag-tag sequences at the C-terminus of the CDS. The dominant-negative mutants of Xenopus laevis Orai1, well-known and well-conserved sequences with human Orai1 mutants, were generated from previously cloned Orai1 plasmids. We designed primers for point or deletion mutants, as listed in Table 2 (Supplementary data). The dominant-negative form of Orai1 was amplified with point-mutated primers. After PCR amplification using the primers, the DpnI enzyme was treated and transformed into DH5α E. coli. Wild-type or dominant negative mutants of Orai1 mRNAs were transcribed by using mMESSAGE mMACHINE™ SP6 Transcription Kit (Invitrogen) from the cloned plasmids. Cloning of the mouse Gli1 and PTCH1 gene promoter region A 565 bp DNA fragment, corresponding to the region −559/+6 bp relative to the transcription-starting site of the Gli1 gene, was amplified using genomic DNA extracted from MEFs by PCR using the primers sense −559 and antisense +6. An 872 bp DNA fragment, corresponding to the region −866/+6 bp relative to the transcription-starting site of the PTCH1 gene, was amplified using genomic DNA extracted from MEFs by PCR using the primers sense −866 and antisense +6. This PCR product was gel-purified, digested with KpnI and XhoI (New England Biolabs), and subcloned into the pGL4.10-Basic firefly luciferase vector (Promega). Site-directed mutagenesis was used to inactivate the three predicted NFAT1 binding sites in the Gli1 promoter, performed by a Quick-change PCR approach. All these primers used are listed in Table 2 (Supplementary data). All plasmid constructs were confirmed by DNA sequencing. Luciferase assay HEK 293T cells were transfected with the indicated luciferase constructs. Cotransfection with the Renilla luciferase gene (pRL-TK) was used as an internal control for cell number, transfection efficiency, and NFAT, as a transcriptional factor. After 18-24 h, assays were performed using the Dual Luciferase® Reporter Assay System (Promega). For each condition, luciferase activity was measured with samples from triplicate wells with a 96-well automated luminometer (Bio-Rad). Results are calculated using the ratio of Firefly to Renilla luciferase activity. RNA isolation and quantitative real-time PCR Total RNA was isolated with RiboEx (GeneAll) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. 1 μg of RNA was used per reverse transcribed using oligo (dT) primers and First Strand cDNA synthesis Kit (ToYoBo). The reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) amplification was done using a C1000 Touch Thermal Cycler (Bio-Rad). Amplification started with initial denaturation at 95 ℃ for 3 min. Then 25-33 cycles of denaturation at 95 ℃ for 30 sec, annealing at 55 ℃ for 30 sec, and extension at 72 ℃ for 1 min. Gel electrophoresis was used to identify the PCR products in a 1.5 % agarose gel using ethidium bromide staining. Real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) analysis was performed using the SYBR green method. Synthesized cDNA and SYBR green Master Mix (ToYoBo) were run on a LightCycler480 II (Roche). Relative expression levels of mRNA were calculated using the 2^-ΔΔCt method. PCR array PCR array results were obtained using a Mouse Primary Cilia RT2 Profiler PCR array (Qiagen). Against 84 key cilia genes was used according to the manufacturer's instructions. The qPCR reactions were performed in a Roche LightCycler480 II. The PCR array reproducibility was checked by analyzing Ct values for positive PCR control (PPC) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Quantitative RT-PCR was performed with gene expression (relative) calculated, with each replicate cycle threshold (Ct) normalized to the average Ct of the software automatically selecting the listed optimal set of housekeeping/reference genes with the most stable expression across the samples based on the results from the entire PCR array (Nek8, Hsp90ab1, Wnt9b, Ift80, Akt1). Fold change values are then presented as average fold change &gt; 2 (average ∆Ct). Data from multiple independent arrays were analyzed using Qiagen RT2 Profiler PCR array data analysis. Pathway enrichment analysis was performed using ShinyGO (version 0.85) with the KEGG pathway database. Enriched pathways were ranked by fold enrichment, and false discovery rate (FDR) was used for multiple testing correction. CUT&amp;RUN-PCR assay CUT&amp;RUN (Cleavage Under Targets &amp; Release Using Nuclease) assay was performed except making library, followed with manufacturer’s method. CUT&amp;RUN assay was progressed under NFAT1-GFP transfected MEF cells. And anti-GFP or anti-NFAT1 antibodies were used as the primary antibody. pA/G-MNase (EpiCypher) cut chromatin DNA around GFP or NFAT1 antibody binding site and release fragmented DNA was purified with SPRIselect magnetic bead (Beckman). PCR was performed with each primer pair of the NFAT1 binding site at the Gli1 promoter. Real-time PCR was used to quantify the released DNA levels at each site in MEF cells transfected with NFAT1-GFP, with or without antibody, and normalized to an internal control region.
