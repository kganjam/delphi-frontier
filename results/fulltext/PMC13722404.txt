Introduction Anthropogenic CO 2 has reshaped surface-ocean carbonate chemistry at planetary scale, and meta-analyses have long established that it depresses calcification, development and recruitment across a wide range of marine taxa [ 1 – 4 ]. For marine animals with complex life cycles, however, the key uncertainty is not simply whether ocean acidification (OA) harms larvae, juveniles, or adults, but whether tolerance measured at one developmental stage predicts adaptive capacity across the organism’s life cycle. This distinction matters because roughly 80% of marine invertebrates carry a bipartite or multi-stage life history [ 5 – 8 ], yet the dominant experimental paradigm in ocean acidification biology measures tolerance at one developmental stage—most often the larval phase—and extrapolates from it [ 9 – 15 ]. This workflow is understandable: larval assays are tractable, scalable, and often capture the stage at which mortality is most severe. But larvae and post-metamorphic animals face different physiological problems. Larvae must build a first shell and complete development within a narrow temporal window, whereas juveniles and adults must sustain shell maintenance, acid–base regulation, growth and reproduction over much longer time scales. A family that performs well at one stage therefore need not perform well at another. The biological question is whether single-stage tolerance is an adequate proxy for life-cycle tolerance, or whether metamorphosis changes the axis on which acidification tolerance is expressed. In evolutionary terms, this is a problem of covariance. The direction in which selection can move a complex-life-history population is set not only by the strength of selection within each stage, but also by the cross-stage covariance of trait-mediated fitness—the off-diagonal structure of the multivariate G-matrix [ 16 – 20 ]. Standing genetic variation can be abundant at each stage yet still fail to generate favorable life-cycle adaptation if the selection vectors acting before and after metamorphosis are not aligned in the same genetic coordinates [ 19 , 20 ]. Because larval and post-metamorphic stages differ in size, exposure, physiology and fitness bookkeeping, the same allele, genotype, or family can occupy different positions on different adaptive surfaces across ontogeny [ 5 – 10 , 12 , 13 , 21 , 22 ]. In a Fisher geometric framework [ 23 – 25 ], movement toward the optimum on one axis can move performance away from the optimum on another; the same logic applies across life stages whenever stage-specific selection vectors are noncollinear [ 26 – 29 ]. These geometric considerations are not abstract. They also explain why, despite two decades of OA experiments on bivalves, sea urchins and other calcifiers, the cross-stage structure of tolerance has remained poorly characterized [ 9 – 15 ]. Larval and adult assays typically operate on different biological material: larval tolerance is screened at the vial or well-plate level on mass-spawned cohorts, whereas adult performance is usually measured on independently sourced juveniles or adults, and the two datasets are rarely connected by a shared genetic scaffold [ 11 – 13 ]. This matters because a cross-stage trade-off can only be seen if the same biological units—the same families, genotypes, or lineages—are measured before and after metamorphosis. If larval and adult performance are scored on different animals, even a strong trade-off leaves no trace in the data [ 20 , 29 , 30 ]. A systematic map of the marine complex-life-history literature confirms how rarely this condition is met: of 3,315 experiments, 67% measured a single-stage and only 1.7% tracked the same individuals across stages [ 31 ]. The result is an experimental literature that can document severe impacts at each stage while remaining silent on whether the same biological lineages—families, genotypes, alleles—that thrive as larvae will also thrive as adults. If the cross-stage covariance is close to zero, this silence is harmless. If it is negative, the silence is consequential: the field can then accumulate confident forecasts pointing in the wrong direction. Three possible scenarios emerge ( Fig 1 ). A whole-organism integration model predicts positive cross-stage covariance because a largely shared tolerance architecture benefits both stages ( Fig 1a ). An ontogenetic decoupling model predicts near-zero covariance because metamorphosis genetically buffers stages from one another [ 7 , 8 ] ( Fig 1b ). A third possibility—less frequently tested but more consequential—is stage-partitioned conflict, in which tolerance gained at one stage is gained at the expense of tolerance at another ( Fig 1c ). Under this third model, OA does not simply depress performance at each stage in parallel: it reshapes the fitness landscape so that the same larval-acting selection produces categorically different adaptive trajectories depending on the sign of the cross-stage covariance [ 19 , 20 , 26 – 29 ] ( Fig 1d – 1f ). Stage-specific selection on the most vulnerable phase can then become a genetic bottleneck, concentrating the breeding pool around families that traverse one filter while eroding variance along another life-stage axis [ 19 , 20 , 30 ]. Which of these three scenarios describes OA tolerance is therefore a first-order question for climate-adaptation forecasting, not a technical footnote. Population-genetic theory for complex life histories makes the third of them the expected outcome rather than an exotic one: extensions of Fisher’s geometric model show that pleiotropic trade-offs between life-cycle stages arise readily from both divergent selection and mutation, and that conflict between stages escalates as adaptation proceeds [ 32 , 33 ]. 10.1371/journal.pbio.3004079.g001 Fig 1 Three competing models show that climate tolerance can integrate, decouple or reverse across metamorphosis. The prediction is deliberately strong: all three hypotheses are mathematically compatible with OA-depressed performance at both stages, and distinguishing among them requires matched-stage phenotypes on the same genetic units, not separate single-stage datasets compared post hoc. Only within a shared pedigree can cross-stage covariance be identified independently of differential survival, differential sampling across stages, or differential environmental exposure. This identifiability requirement has been noted in the quantitative-genetic literature on multivariate selection [ 20 , 29 , 30 ] but rarely implemented in OA biology, where hatchery-level logistics tend to force stage-separate experiments. Here we provide such a test. We bred 51 families of the hard clam M. mercenaria in a partial half-sib pedigree (12 sires × 6 dams) and measured the same families both before and after metamorphosis, under present-day and end-century acidification. This lets us ask three questions that single-stage designs cannot: do the families that tolerate acidification best as larvae also tolerate it best as juveniles and adults; is any cross-stage relationship a property of the multivariate genetic architecture rather than of one summary score; and, if the relationship is negative, what does that imply for populations evolving under sustained acidification? We answer the first with matched stage-specific tolerance scores, the second with the among-family covariance matrix G and its principal axes, and the third with a transparent 20-generation model in which families are re-weighted each generation according to their relative fitness. Nor is this merely a concern for bivalves. Whenever a complex-life-history species has been treated as a single adaptive unit for the purposes of climate-tolerance forecasting—corals, sea urchins, crustaceans, many polychaetes and most commercially exploited mollusks [ 8 , 13 , 21 , 34 , 35 ]—the same identifiability problem applies. Our question is therefore not whether OA harms one stage more than another, but whether the fitness axis itself can bend between stages, and what happens to adaptation when it does. Results To move from the three conceptual models to an empirical test, we first anchored every stage-specific measurement to the same genetic units. Each operational family was propagated through fully replicated ambient (pH T 8.05 ± 0.02, Ω arag 2.18) and acidified (pH T 7.69 ± 0.02, Ω arag 0.98) seawater systems with stable carbonate chemistry ( S1 and S2 Figs ; S1 Table ). Larval traits (survival, shell area, thickness, hardness and days to metamorphosis) were measured at vial level within a block → tank → vial hierarchy. Post-metamorphic traits (shell growth, dissolution and MO 2 , the mass-specific rate of oxygen uptake) were measured on individual juveniles after a 90-day OA nursery, and gonadal index was measured on the same animals after two further years in a common ambient garden. The two exposures were independent—no animal was acidified at both stages—so the larval and post-metamorphic measurements are connected only by shared family membership ( Materials and methods , §4). Gonadal index is therefore a delayed consequence of the animal’s own earlier post-metamorphic exposure, not of anything that happened to it as a larva. All analyses retained the nested random-effect structure of the design, and the partial half-sib pedigree provided the numerator-relationship scaffold for the bivariate pedigree animal model ( S2 Table ). We therefore begin with the question that decides between the three models—whether family rankings are preserved or inverted across metamorphosis—and then turn to the stage-specific filters, and to the multivariate genetic architecture, that explain the inversion. Metamorphosis reverses family rankings for climate tolerance We first tested the strongest prediction of the conflict model: the families that look climate-resilient before metamorphosis should not be the same families that look resilient afterward. For each family, we calculated stage-specific composite tolerance as weighted geometric means of OA/ambient trait-retention ratios, so that tolerance reflects stage-wide performance rather than a single endpoint ( Materials and methods , Eqs. 2 and 3 ; S3 Table ). The cross-stage answer was strongly negative: larval and post-metamorphic tolerance were inversely associated across families (Pearson r = −0.530, 95% CI [−0.691, −0.332], P = 6.2 × 10 −5 ; Spearman ρ = −0.548, P = 3.1 × 10 −5 ; Fig 2a ). The reversal was visible at the level that matters for selection and screening. Of the 13 families in the top larval quartile, 10 fell below the adult median; of the 13 families in the top adult quartile, 11 fell below the larval median; no family occupied the top quartile at both stages ( Fig 2b , 2c ). The reversal is robust to pedigree structure and tolerance definitions We next asked whether the inversion was a structural property of the pedigree rather than a consequence of one scoring rule or a few influential families. A 5,000-shuffle permutation null placed the observed cross-stage correlation in the extreme negative tail ( P &lt; 1/5,000; Fig 2d ; S4 Table ). Removing sire and dam structure left a negative residual association ( r = −0.486, P = 3.0 × 10 −4 ; Fig 2e ), and leave-one-sire/dam analyses never changed the sign ( S3d Fig ). The result also survived five alternative tolerance definitions, including equal-weight geometric means, arithmetic means of ratio terms, and adult composites omitting gonadal index or MO 2 ( S3 Fig ; S5 Table ). Quantitative-genetic summaries pointed in the same direction: collapsing to the 12 shared sires gave r = −0.66 (95% CI [−0.90, −0.14], P = 0.019), dam-level summaries remained negative, and a bivariate pedigree animal model supported a negative cross-stage genetic correlation ( S4 Fig ; S6 Table ). The sign is therefore not a family-outlier effect, not a composite-weight artifact and not created by ignoring the partial pedigree. Ocean acidification imposes different filters before and after metamorphosis Having established the inversion, we then asked how acidification imposes within-stage filters that could generate it. In larvae, OA reduced vial-level survival from 0.573 ± 0.004 s.e. to 0.432 ± 0.007 s.e. ( β = −0.587 ± 0.028 s.e.; Wald χ 2 = 439.56, P &lt; 0.001; S7 Table ; Fig 3a , 3b ). Shell area, shell thickness and shell hardness declined, whereas days to metamorphosis increased by 1.89 d (all P &lt; 0.001; S8 – S11 Tables ; Fig 3c – 3f ). Importantly, acidification did not erase selectable family variance: family-level intraclass correlations (ICCs) were 0.187 for survival, 0.337 for shell area, 0.314 for thickness, 0.289 for hardness and 0.258 for metamorphosis ( Fig 3g , 3h ). Using the Lande–Arnold/Arnold–Wade selection-analysis framework [ 17 , 36 ], we then found that OA steepened directional selection toward larger first-shell area and faster metamorphosis ( Fig 4a , 4b ): the standardized selection differential for shell area rose from +0.17 under ambient conditions to +0.28 under OA, and that for days to metamorphosis from −0.10 to −0.21 (treatment × trait interaction P &lt; 10 −5 for both; S12 Table ), narrowing larval fitness around a high-calcification, rapid-development combination ( Fig 4c , 4d ), with the differentials summarized in Fig 4e . Acidification reorganizes the multivariate architecture into a cross-stage conflict Composite scores show that family rankings invert, but not whether the covariance structure itself is responsible, so we estimated the among-family covariance matrix G for all nine traits at once, separately within each environment ( Materials and methods , §7.4; S17 and S18 Tables ). Two quantities summarize such a matrix. Its leading eigenvector, g max , is the single weighted combination of the nine traits along which families differ most; the effective number of dimensions, n D , is the sum of the eigenvalues divided by the largest, and is 1 when all among-family variance lies on g max alone and 9 when it is spread evenly across the traits. Under acidification, the architecture is close to one-dimensional: g max accounts for 57% of the among-family variance (95% CI 49%–66%; n D = 1.77) and exceeds its design-based null ( P = 5 × 10 −4 ; Fig 2f ). Nineteen of the 20 cross-stage correlations are negative, with a mean of −0.375 (95% CI −0.50 to −0.20; P = 5 × 10 −4 ; Fig 2g ). Under ambient conditions, the same 51 families show no such structure: the mean cross-stage correlation is −0.001 ( P = 0.49; Fig 2h ), g max accounts for only 37% against a null expectation of 35%, and variance is spread over more dimensions ( n D = 2.70). Under acidification g max loads positively on all five larval traits and negatively on all four post-metamorphic traits, so the dominant axis of family variation is the cross-stage conflict itself, whereas the ambient g max loads on the post-metamorphic traits without a consistent sign (−0.22 for metabolic efficiency, +0.29 for gonadal index; Fig 2i ), so it describes no cross-stage conflict. The contrast is well supported ( Δ r = −0.374, 95% CI −0.56 to −0.16), and the two matrices differ in orientation as well as dimensionality ( g max 42.5° apart; Krzanowski’s common-subspace statistic 2.28 of a possible 4; mean random-skewers vector correlation 0.66). Acidification does not simply depress performance at both stages—it converts an architecture in which the two stages vary independently into one in which they oppose each other. The consequence for selection follows directly. Under acidification, the predicted multivariate response to selection on larval fitness, R = G β larval , is positive across the larval traits but negative across the post-metamorphic traits (summed post-metamorphic response −1.91, 95% CI −2.57 to −1.20; S6a Fig ; S19 Table ), and it points 100° away from the direction that post-metamorphic selection favors—more than a right angle, so the correlated response actively opposes adult-stage selection rather than merely failing to help it. The reciprocal holds (111°), whereas under ambient conditions the same calculation yields no cross-stage cost at all (+0.09; 88°). The conflict axis is not an artifact of nonadditive or maternal effects: among-sire covariances, which isolate the additive component, give a g max 16.6° from the family-level one ( S6b Fig ), explaining a similar share of variance (58%) and carrying the same opposing loadings (mean cross-stage r = −0.46). An unweighted principal-component decomposition of the nine tolerance ratios recovers the same axis as the a priori weighted composites ( r = +0.95 and −0.70; S6c Fig ), confirming that the weighting in Eqs. 2 , 3 is not the source of the result. Single-stage selection enriches hidden liabilities across the life cycle Finally, we asked whether the observed sign of cross-stage covariance is consequential for adaptation, not merely descriptive of present-day family variance. We projected family frequencies for 20 generations using a relative-fitness family-frequency model with Wright–Fisher mean-fitness normalization ( Materials and methods , Eqs. 5 – 8 ; S20 Table ). Because these projections address evolutionary rather than purely plastic responses in a changing ocean [ 37 , 38 ], the model is not an abundance forecast; it isolates how the sign of cross-stage covariance changes evolutionary direction under the same family pool and the same forcing schedule. Under positive and near-zero scenario surfaces, larval-dominant, balanced, and adult-dominant selection all moved trajectories in broadly adaptive directions, differing mainly in which stage improved faster ( Fig 6a , 6b , 6d , 6e , 6g , and 6h ). Under the empirical conflict surface, however, stage weighting changed direction: larval-dominant selection improved larval tolerance while eroding adult tolerance, adult-dominant selection produced the inverse, and balanced weighting was the only regime that kept both axes net-positive while retaining the most effective family diversity ( Fig 6c , 6f , 6i ). At generation 20, paired family-cluster bootstraps showed that balanced weighting exceeded larval-dominant selection with probability 0.81 and adult-dominant selection with probability 0.87 under the empirical conflict surface ( Fig 6j – 6l ; Eq. 9 ). These are posterior-style probabilities from paired resamples, not significance tests: 0.81 means that in 81% of bootstrap resamples of the family pool balanced weighting retained more diversity than larval-dominant weighting, which is suggestive rather than decisive, whereas the corresponding probabilities under the positive and near-zero surfaces are 0.33 and 0.47—indistinguishable from a coin toss. The informative comparison is therefore between surfaces, not the absolute value of any one probability. In absolute terms, every regime loses most of the initial diversity by generation 20, because the model deliberately contains only selection: it has no mutation, no recombination among families and no immigration, and families are treated as indivisible units. It is a comparator of evolutionary direction under a common forcing schedule, not a forecast of standing variation, and the differences between regimes should be read as relative rather than absolute—balanced weighting retained ~80% more effective family diversity than larval-dominant weighting and about 3.7-fold more than adult-dominant weighting. Because recombination and mutation would replenish variation in all three regimes, incorporating them would be expected to raise every trajectory without reversing their order, but this remains to be tested. Continuous stage-weight scans placed the diversity maximum near balanced selection ( w L ≈ 0.45; Materials and methods , §8.4), and balanced weighting retained the most diversity in all 36 combinations of forcing intensity, ramp and horizon ( S7 and S8 Figs ; S21 and S22 Tables ). These projections turn the empirical ranking reversal into an operational warning: single-stage selection does not merely omit information from the other stage; when metamorphosis reverses family rankings, it actively enriches hidden liabilities elsewhere in the life cycle. Discussion OA tolerance in M. mercenaria is partitioned across life stages rather than integrated across the organism. Family-level larval and adult composites covary negatively; the sign survives every alternative specification, and rank reversals are pervasive. Because the sign of cross-stage covariance dictates the direction of projected evolution, single-stage weighting yields gains in one stage at the cost of the other, and only balanced weighting keeps both trajectories net-positive while preserving family diversity. The multivariate analysis shows that this is a property of the architecture rather than of a scoring rule: under acidification, a single axis carries most of the among-family variance and sets larval against post-metamorphic performance, whereas under ambient conditions that axis does not exist. The covariance geometry is the constraint, not statistical decoration. This sharpens evolutionary-rescue theory [ 39 – 42 ]. Evolutionary-rescue models calibrate rescue against the distance between mean performance and a moving optimum; in a complex life cycle, the fitness axis itself bends between stages. A population rescued on the larval axis can simultaneously lose ground on the adult axis under identical selection—a developmentally structured evolutionary trap that single-stage assays cannot resolve. The most sensitive stage is therefore not the best window onto adaptive capacity: its survivors often carry the largest hidden cost downstream, and single-stage rescue metrics can converge on adaptive futures that are demographically unviable. Two nonexclusive mechanisms fit the data. First, an energetic trade-off [ 10 , 13 , 21 , 22 ]: families that rapidly clear the larval carbonate bottleneck may pay through elevated maintenance metabolism, reduced dissolution resistance and diminished reproductive allocation—a thermodynamic, not genetic, conflict. Second, antagonistic pleiotropy or tight cross-stage linkage [ 26 , 28 , 29 ]: alleles that favor pH homeostasis, first-shell mineralization and developmental tempo at the larval stage may oppose shell maintenance, chronic acid–base regulation and gonadal investment thereafter. Metamorphosis does not erase cross-stage genetic covariance in marine invertebrates: in the ascidian Ciona intestinalis , Aguirre and colleagues [ 29 ] likewise recovered a single supported eigenvector of the viability G matrix that spanned the metamorphic boundary and set larval viability against viability on either side of it. Our result extends that finding in two ways—the conflict axis here concerns tolerance of an environmental stressor rather than viability under benign conditions, and it is induced by that stressor, being absent from the same families under ambient conditions. A third possibility we cannot exclude is that the ambient architecture is not truly modular but merely has too little among-family variance in the relevant direction to detect. Our design does not localize the underlying loci, but the sign is biologically expected because larval and adult tolerance recruit partly different physiological axes [ 9 , 10 , 12 , 13 , 21 , 34 ]. The single-stage paradigm has persisted because its blind spot is invisible by construction: when stages are measured on different pedigrees, the cross-stage covariance sign cannot be recovered. The field has thereby converged on a workflow that systematically under-observes a first-order adaptive constraint, and confidence calibration worsens as within-stage sample size grows. The remedy is matched-stage experiments within a shared pedigree [ 29 , 30 , 43 ], replicated across source populations and forcing intensities, with the covariance sign reported as a primary outcome rather than an afterthought. The phenomenon is unlikely to be confined to bivalves. Many benthic marine invertebrate species—including most commercially and ecologically important calcifiers—share the relevant architecture: a small, carbonate-limited pelagic larva with a narrow developmental window, followed by a benthic adult with different energetic demands and delayed reproductive bookkeeping [ 5 , 7 , 8 , 44 ]. Corals, echinoderms and crustaceans show stage-dependent sensitivities consistent with cross-stage conflict but have rarely been tested for covariance sign within a shared pedigree [ 9 , 12 , 34 , 35 , 45 ]. Our result therefore makes a testable prediction: matched-stage assays in these taxa should frequently recover nonpositive, and in some cases negative, cross-stage covariance, and single-stage forecasts should be biased toward whichever stage was screened. The sharper and more easily falsified half of the prediction concerns induction rather than sign: because the conflict axis reported here is absent from the same families under ambient conditions, matched-stage assays run in a benign and a stressful environment should find among-family variance dispersed across several axes in the benign environment and concentrated onto a single cross-stage axis under stress. That comparison is within reach of any system in which the same families can be phenotyped on both sides of a life-history transition, and the design-based null used here ( Materials and methods , §7.4) provides a ready test of it. The same logic should extend to warming and hypoxia [ 20 – 22 , 45 ]. Currently, most OA-adaptation programmes emphasize early larval traits because they scale cheaply and dominate early mortality [ 9 – 15 ]. Uncorrected, such screening will enrich broodstock that traverse the larval filter while exporting costs to adult maintenance and reproduction; the same logic distorts restoration triage of wild cohorts. Selection indices that integrate stage-specific performance with cross-stage covariance—explicit balanced weighting of the kind implemented here—provide a transparent corrective at low operational cost [ 20 , 30 ]. Because the relevant covariance appears only under acidification, such indices have to be estimated under the stressor rather than inferred from performance in benign conditions. The cost of inaction is a generation of selection pressure applied to the wrong axis. The forecasting consequences are larger. When single-stage collinearity fails, the screened stage does not merely under-sample adaptive capacity; it reports adaptive capacity oriented in the wrong direction. Resilience in complex-life-history taxa is not the variance expressed in the most vulnerable stage, nor the magnitude of the strongest immediate response: it is the subset of variance that remains favorable after the life cycle re-weights performance across stages [ 46 , 47 ]. The critique extends beyond OA to warming, hypoxia, and multi-stressor regimes wherever metamorphosis reorganizes exposure and selection. The result sits in a clear conceptual lineage: OA reshapes within-stage fitness landscapes [ 15 ], and evolution of one climate-tolerance trait can carry correlated physiological and life-history costs [ 48 ]; here, the geometry operates across entire life stages, and under OA it is antagonistic. Several limitations define the next steps. We examined one source population at one OA intensity; sign and magnitude may vary with genetic background and forcing gradient [ 37 , 38 , 49 ]. We also did not test parental or transgenerational acclimation. All comparisons should therefore be interpreted as within-generation family-level tolerance and covariance under a common experimental background, rather than as evidence for or against inherited plasticity across generations. The projections treat family composites as indivisible units of selection and do not model recombination, mutation, migration, or density dependence [ 40 , 42 ]; they therefore describe the direction in which selection pushes a fixed family pool, not the amount of genetic variation a real population would retain, and the absolute endpoint diversities they report should not be read as predictions. Adding these processes is a tractable extension. G itself was estimated at the among-family level on a partial pedigree, so it contains nonadditive and maternal as well as additive variance; the close agreement between the family- and sire-level conflict axes indicates that the additive component carries the same sign and orientation, but resolving their relative magnitudes will require a larger and denser breeding design. Studies setting out to emulate this design should increase sire replication in particular. Twelve sires proved sufficient to confirm the sign and orientation of the conflict axis at the additive level, but constraining the additive genetic variance itself needs a larger paternal array, and that is where additional effort would repay first. The pedigree animal model strengthens inference on covariance sign but does not resolve the full additive-genetic architecture [ 30 , 43 , 50 ]; localizing the loci, and distinguishing antagonistic pleiotropy from linkage, will require denser pedigrees and scaled genotyping. These caveats locate the next experiments rather than weaken the central message. Resilience is not a trait of a single-stage; it is a distributed property of the developmental sequence, and environmental change can reorganize that distribution in ways that invert winners and losers. What must be measured, and preserved in downstream forecasts, is the covariance structure of performance across the life cycle that selection actually sees. Materials and methods The experimental design, measurement timing, seawater chemistry, and pedigree scaffold are summarized in S1 and S2 Figs and S1 – S2 Tables . 1. Study organism and broodstock conditioning Adult Mercenaria mercenaria (shell length 62–81 mm; n = 130) were collected from a single intertidal–subtidal flat in Long Island Sound, Connecticut, USA, in order to minimize confounding by population structure and to retain a common evolutionary background across the pedigree. Animals were transported to the hatchery and acclimated for 21 d in flow-through, UV-treated seawater (20 ± 0.5 °C; 30 ± 0.5 psu; pH T = 8.05 ± 0.02), and fed ad libitum with Isochrysis galbana and Chaetoceros calcitrans at a 1:1 ratio (~5 × 10 4 cells mL −1 ). Shell length, total wet mass, and sex were recorded before crossing. Spawning was induced by thermal stimulation and was carried out within a restricted temporal window to limit uncontrolled spawning-order effects. Broodstock were strip-spawned only when necessary to complete missing crosses. Embryos from each cross were rinsed through 40-µm mesh, checked for fertilization success after first cleavage, and retained only when cleavage exceeded 80%. This procedure generated the operational family units that were carried consistently through larval assays, juvenile assays, source-data worksheets and pedigree-aware models. 2. Quantitative-genetic family design The pedigree was intentionally partial rather than fully factorial [ 30 ]. Each of 12 sires was crossed to a subset of the 6 dams, yielding 51 operational families ( S1 Fig ). This design sacrifices some balance relative to a complete diallel but preserves the paternal half-sib structure needed for quantitative-genetic inference while remaining practical for a nonmodel bivalve spawning programme. Operational families were mapped to an explicit sire–dam table and then carried through every analysis in one of three ways: as family identifiers, as nested pedigree terms within mixed models, and as the numerator-relationship structure used for the supplementary animal model [ 30 , 50 ]. Families sharing a sire are not independent evolutionary units, so retaining the pedigree in all downstream models prevents spurious certainty in family rankings and provides a conservative framework for evaluating cross-stage covariance. Because the pedigree is partial and the phenotypic inputs used for the bivariate animal model are family-mean composite scores rather than repeated individual records, the quantitative-genetic analysis is interpreted as a formal support for covariance sign and mode rather than as a definitive estimator of all narrow-sense parameters. This boundary is made explicit in the main manuscript. 3. Seawater chemistry manipulation and characterization Ambient and acidified systems were maintained independently and continuously monitored following established ocean acidification best-practice workflows [ 51 ]. Ambient seawater targeted present-day local conditions, whereas the acidified treatment targeted end-century carbonate chemistry under a high-emissions trajectory (pH T ≈ 7.70, Ω arag ≈ 1.0). Independent treatment loops were used to avoid pseudo-replication at the chemistry level and to preserve treatment-specific carbonate-system variance for downstream mixed-model inference. pH was logged continuously with NBS-calibrated electrodes and independently checked by benchtop spectrophotometric measurements. Total alkalinity was measured regularly by open-cell titration, and carbonate-system variables were recalculated from pH and total alkalinity with CO2SYS [ 52 ], following best-practice carbonate chemistry reporting [ 53 ]. Temperature and salinity were recorded daily. Treatment separation and its stability over the exposure are shown in S2 Fig , and the full carbonate-system record is summarized in S1 Table . Treatment systems were serviced separately to avoid gas-exchange cross-talk, and water-exchange rates were adjusted to keep dissolved oxygen, temperature, and salinity tightly overlapping between treatments. 4. Larval experiment: Hierarchical design and phenotyping The larval assay was designed to separate treatment effects from temporal and vessel effects. Two temporal blocks were staggered by 5 d. Each family was reared in its own independently plumbed 20-L polycarbonate tank within each treatment × block combination (51 families × 2 treatments × 2 blocks = 204 tanks), and each tank contained three 250-mL larval vials. Each vial received exactly N 0 = 180 larvae from a single family, counted individually under stereomicroscopy, giving 51 × 2 × 2 × 3 = 612 vials in total ( S1 Data ). Larval and post-metamorphic exposures were entirely independent of one another: no animal experienced acidification at both stages. Larvae used in the larval assay were terminated at that assay’s endpoint, and the juveniles entering the post-metamorphic experiment were drawn from separate ambient-reared cohorts of the same 51 families and then randomly assigned to the ambient or acidified nursery. Larval and post-metamorphic tolerance are therefore linked only through shared family (and hence pedigree) membership, which is precisely the level at which selection operates and at which cross-stage covariance is identifiable; no result in this paper depends on within-individual carry-over. Larvae were reared under constant gentle aeration and fed every 48 h with I. galbana plus C. calcitrans with a 1:1 mixture at 10 4 cells mL −1 . Vials were terminated when at least 50% of survivors reached the pediveliger stage, or at day 21 if that threshold had not yet been reached. All surviving larvae were counted, and dead individuals were identified by collapsed or darkened tissue. From each vial, up to 20 survivors were sampled for shell area (calibrated image analysis), shell thickness (cross-sectional SEM), nanoindentation hardness (Hysitron TI950; Berkovich probe; 500 µN peak load; 5-s hold) and days to metamorphosis. Image analysis, indentation, and SEM scoring were performed blind to family and treatment identity. 5. Adult experiment: Phenotyping and delayed reproductive signature The adult phase was designed to capture both acute post-metamorphic maintenance costs and a delayed reproductive signature of earlier OA exposure. Therefore, our designation of “adult” stage includes reproductively active adults as well as post-metamorphic juveniles. Five juveniles per family were assigned to each treatment ( n = 510 total individuals) and held individually in partitioned chambers nested within flow-through treatment tanks during a 90-d OA nursery phase. Shell growth rate was calculated from repeated caliper measurements of maximum shell length over the nursery interval and is reported as mm per 30-d period. Shell dissolution and shell growth are not alternatives: in bivalves the periostracum-covered growing margin continues to accrete new carbonate while the older, abraded umbonal and lateral surfaces—where the periostracum is thin or worn—are exposed to undersaturated water and corrode. Net linear growth and localized surface dissolution therefore proceed simultaneously on the same shell, and both were scored. Dissolution was quantified on the external shell surface as a 0–4 ordinal scale (0 = intact periostracum and no visible etching; 1 = localized loss of surface luster; 2 = visible etching or chalky patches confined to the umbo; 3 = etching extending over the lateral surfaces with exposed prismatic layer; and 4 = extensive pitting or perforation of the outer layer) by two trained scorers working independently and blind to family and treatment identity (Cohen’s κ = 0.83); disagreements were resolved by consensus. Oxygen consumption (MO 2 )—the mass-specific rate of oxygen uptake, used here as an index of routine maintenance metabolism—was measured by sealed-chamber respirometry using a YSI ProODO optical O 2 logger during 3-h incubations at days 30, 60, and 90; rates were standardized to dry mass. Nearly all juveniles survived the 90-day nursery (survival rate ≈ 1), so post-metamorphic fitness variation among families is expressed through growth, shell maintenance, metabolic cost and subsequent reproductive output rather than through differential mortality. After the OA nursery phase, all juveniles were transferred back to ambient pond culture. Gonadal index was then quantified after two years of common grow-out as (gonad wet mass/total soft-tissue wet mass) × 100%, so reproductive status is interpreted as a delayed signature of earlier OA exposure rather than a trait measured concurrently with the 90-d maintenance phenotypes. This timing distinction is preserved throughout the analysis and the discussion. 6. Statistical analyses For each trait, we first screened biologically justified random-effect structures and then estimated treatment effects using the most conservative supported structure. Candidate random terms followed the mating design and the experiment hierarchy: sire, dam, operational family, and block for larval traits; sire, dam, and operational family for adult traits. The objective was not to maximize model complexity but to preserve pedigree and block structure whenever it remained estimable and informative. All analyses were implemented in Python with pandas, numpy, scipy, statsmodels, openpyxl and matplotlib, following mixed-model logic commonly used in lme4 and glmmTMB workflows [ 54 , 55 ]. 6.1 Larval survival (pedigree-aware mixed model). Larval survival was analyzed as a pedigree-aware mixed model on the empirical-logit transform [ 56 ] of vial-level survival proportions: l o g i t * ( p i ) = l o g ( p i + 1 2 n i 1 − p i + 1 2 n i ) , where p i is the survival proportion and n i the initial vial stocking. The response was modeled as a function of treatment while retaining the supported pedigree and block structure: l o g i t * ( p i ) = μ + β · T i + u b l o c k ( i ) + u s i r e ( i ) + u d a m ( s i r e , i ) + u f a m i l y ( i ) + ε i , with T i a treatment indicator (0 = ambient, 1 = acidified), β the fixed effect of acidification, each u . a Gaussian random effect and ε i a Gaussian residual. This transformation stabilizes variance, avoids boundary infinities and yields effect sizes that remain interpretable on the logit scale. 6.2 Continuous larval and adult traits (linear mixed models). Continuous larval and adult traits were analyzed with pedigree-aware linear mixed models [ 54 ]. We did not force a single identical random structure across traits, because doing so would privilege formal symmetry over biological fidelity and numerical stability. Instead, each trait retained the richest nonsingular structure supported by the observed data distribution, as recorded for that trait in the statistical supplement. 6.3 Intraclass correlation. Robustness of the cross-stage signal was evaluated in four ways: Pearson and Spearman correlation on the observed family composites; pedigree-conditioned residual correlation after removing sire and dam structure by OLS; alternative composite definitions and trait subsets; and threshold sweeps that asked whether the sign changed when only the top-ranked fraction of families was emphasized. These sensitivity analyses are archived in S4 – S5 Tables and S3 Fig . Composite-score associations were accompanied by family-cluster bootstrap confidence intervals [ 57 ]. 7.2 Bivariate pedigree animal model. As a formal quantitative-genetic sensitivity analysis, we fitted a bivariate pedigree animal model to larval and adult composite tolerance. Standardized family composite scores were stacked into a two-trait response vector, fixed effects were limited to an intercept for each trait, and additive covariance was induced through the numerator-relationship matrix A implied by the partial pedigree, following standard animal model logic [ 43 , 50 ]: y = X β + Z a + e , a ~ N ( 0 , A ⊗ G ) , e ~ N ( 0 , I ⊗ R ) . (4) Here y is the stacked two-trait response vector, X and Z are the fixed- and random-effect design matrices, β the vector of fixed effects, a the additive-genetic effect, e the residual term, G the 2 × 2 additive-genetic variance–covariance matrix, R the 2 × 2 residual variance–covariance matrix, and the additive cross-stage genetic correlation is r G = G L A G L L G A A . The model was fitted by restricted maximum likelihood (REML; [ 58 ]), and the profile-supported region of r G was characterized by the likelihood-ratio threshold 2Δ REML = 3.84 ( S6 Table ; S4 Fig ). Because the fitted phenotypes are family means on a partial pedigree, the model is interpreted as quantitative-genetic support for the direction of cross-stage covariance rather than as a complete resolution of the pleiotropic architecture. 7.3 Sire- and dam-level quantitative-genetic summaries. To complement the animal model, family composite scores were collapsed separately to shared sires and shared dams. For each sire or dam, larval and adult composite tolerance were averaged across associated operational families and the cross-stage association was quantified with Pearson correlation and Fisher-z confidence limits ( S6 Table ; S4 Fig ). The sire-level summary is especially informative because sires define the shared paternal half-sib structure retained in the pedigree [ 30 ]. 7.4 Multivariate among-family covariance matrix (G). The bivariate animal model of Eq. 4 answers the primary, directional question most powerfully, but a full nine-trait additive G is not identifiable on this pedigree (45 covariance parameters from 51 family means). That limit applies to the additive matrix, not to the among-family matrix, which the balanced within-stage replication does support. We therefore also estimated the among-family covariance matrix G for nine traits—five larval (survival on the empirical-logit scale, shell area, thickness, hardness and developmental rate, the reciprocal of days to metamorphosis) and four post-metamorphic (shell growth, dissolution resistance, metabolic efficiency, and gonadal index)—separately within each environment, with every trait oriented so that larger values are better and standardized to unit variance. Because the design is balanced, G follows in closed form from a nested analysis of covariance: the within-stage blocks are ( MS among − MS within ) k , and the cross-stage block is the covariance of the larval and post-metamorphic family means, which is unbiased because the two stages were measured on different animals. MS denotes a mean square—the among-family and within-family mean cross-product matrices—and k is the number of replicates per family per treatment: six vials for the larval traits and five juveniles for the post-metamorphic ones. Matrices were bent to the nearest positive-definite matrix before eigen-decomposition. Uncertainty came from family-cluster bootstrapping ( B = 3 , 000 ). The bootstrap resamples the 51 families with replacement, each draw taking a family’s whole set of vials and juveniles together so that the resampling unit is the unit of inference; G is re-estimated from scratch within every draw and every derived statistic recomputed from it, and the intervals reported are the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the resulting distributions. Support was assessed against a design-based null—2,000 simulations with the cross-stage covariance set to zero and every other feature of the design preserved—which propagates the sampling error of the estimator itself [ 59 ]; effective dimensionality is reported as n D , the sum of the eigenvalues divided by the largest [ 60 ], so that n D = 1 would mean all among-family variance lies on a single axis. Each eigenvector of G is a weighted combination of the nine traits and is read as a pattern rather than as a set of separate effects: traits loading with the same sign vary together among families, traits loading with opposite signs vary in opposition, and the size of a loading is that trait’s contribution to the axis, while the associated eigenvalue is the amount of among-family variance lying along it. It is the sign structure of g max , not its magnitude, that makes the leadin g axis a cross-stage conflict rather than a general axis of vigour. The two environments were compared in three ways. The angle between their leading eigenvectors measures how far the dominant axis of family variation has rotated. Krzanowski’s common-subspace statistic [ 61 ] asks how much of the space spanned by the four leading eigenvectors of one matrix is also spanned by the four leading eigenvectors of the other, on a scale running from 0 for two entirely distinct subspaces to 4 for two identical ones. Random skewers draws 5,000 random unit selection vectors, projects each through both matrices and records the correlation between the two predicted responses, so that a mean well below 1 means the same selection pressure would be redirected differently by the two matrices. To express the consequence for selection, we estimated stage-specific gradients under acidification— β larval from vial-level relative survival and β adult from family-level relative gonadal index, the appropriate fitness proxy at a stage where survival was near-unity—and evaluated the predicted response R = G β . We report its direction rather than an absolute rate, because family means carry nonadditive and maternal as well as additive variance; the decomposition was therefore repeated on among-sire covariances. Because a change in G can arise either from a change in the correlations among traits or from a change in how much among-family variance there is to distribute, both are reported separately. G is deposited in its correlation form in S17 Table , the among-family variance of each trait in each environment in S23 Table , and the eigen-analysis is repeated on the correlation scale so that the two can be told apart. Retaining the family-level is deliberate: nonadditive variance contributes materially to adaptive potential under environmental change in marine invertebrates [ 62 ], and the family is the unit that hatchery and restoration programmes actually select. 8.
