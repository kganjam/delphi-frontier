Background Secondary infections are a major contributing factor to the high incidence and treatment difficulty of fish diseases in aquaculture. Unlike infections caused by a single pathogen, many fish diseases result from complex interactions involving multiple pathogens, which significantly complicates disease prevention and control strategies in farmed fish populations [ 1 ]. Recent studies have revealed that several representative fish diseases are, in fact, multifactorial in nature. For instance, gill disease in Salmo salar has been identified as a condition caused by a combination of pathogens, including ectoparasites and bacteria, and is frequently associated with co-infection by the recently discovered salmon gill poxvirus (SGPV) [ 2 ]. Infection with Argulus coregoni significantly increases the susceptibility of Oncorhynchus mykiss to Flavobacterium columnare , leading to markedly higher cumulative mortality [ 3 ]. Although secondary infections involving parasites, viruses, and bacteria are commonly observed in aquaculture settings, research on their mechanisms and dynamics in fish remains limited, and the understanding of their pathogenesis is still incomplete [ 4 ]. Larimichthys crocea is a commercially important marine aquaculture species in southeastern China. It is highly valued for its rich nutritional content and desirable taste. Currently, it ranks as the second most productive marine aquaculture species in China, with an annual output of 280,997 tons [ 5 ]. However, the rapid expansion of farming scale and the widespread adoption of high-density rearing practices have led to deteriorating water quality and increased disease outbreaks. These issues have become major obstacles to the sustainable development of the L. crocea industry [ 6 ]. Among the common diseases, marine white spot disease caused by Cryptocaryon irritans is one of the most widespread parasitic infections. It affects a wide range of marine fish and is often accompanied by secondary infections with opportunistic pathogens, such as Vibrio parahaemolyticus . These co-infections significantly increase fish mortality and result in substantial economic losses to the aquaculture sector [ 7 ]. At present, the control of C. irritans -induced secondary infections in L. crocea relies mainly on salinity regulation, antibiotics, and chemical disinfectants [ 8 – 10 ]. However, these approaches often show limited effectiveness under high-density farming conditions in large water bodies. Moreover, the overuse of antibiotics contributes to the emergence of multidrug-resistant bacteria and antibiotic-resistant infections [ 11 ], posing serious environmental risks and public health concerns [ 12 ]. Therefore, there is an urgent need to develop safe, effective, and environmentally friendly alternatives to antibiotics for the prevention and control of secondary infections caused by C. irritans . Such alternatives are essential for improving fish health and promoting the sustainable development of L. crocea aquaculture. Antimicrobial peptides (AMPs) are considered promising alternatives to traditional antibiotics due to their broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity [ 13 ]. In our previous work, we developed a novel recombinant fusion AMP, Scy-hepc, by combining two natural peptides, Scygonadin and PC-hepc. This fusion peptide exhibited strong inhibitory effects against a variety of aquatic pathogens [ 14 ]. When used as a feed additive at 10 mg/kg in L. crocea , Scy-hepc significantly promoted growth. It activated key signaling pathways, including the GH-Jak2-STAT5-IGF1 axis, PI3K-Akt, and Erk/MAPK pathways [ 15 ]. It also improved the microbial composition of the foregut and midgut, enhancing the host’s metabolic potential [ 16 ]. In another study involving Epinephelus akaara, Scy-hepc was effectively absorbed in the foregut and midgut. It modulated the expression of genes related to bacterial flagellar assembly, promoting the colonization of beneficial bacteria. This contributed to enhanced disease resistance in the host [ 17 ]. Although these studies demonstrate the immunostimulatory and growth-promoting effects of Scy-hepc, its role in protecting fish against co-infections remains unverified. The gut microbiota plays a critical role in host immunity and metabolism. In addition to aiding nutrient digestion, gut microbes help strengthen the intestinal barrier, regulate mucosal immune responses, and competitively inhibit pathogen colonization [ 18 , 19 ]. As such, modulating the gut microbiome using AMPs has emerged as a promising strategy to improve disease resistance in aquaculture species [ 20 , 21 ]. Our previous findings suggest that Scy-hepc can enhance disease resistance in E. akaara, by regulating gut microbial communities. Building on this, we aimed to determine whether Scy-hepc could confer similar protective effects in L. crocea under conditions of secondary infection. This investigation holds potential value for both theoretical research and practical applications in aquaculture. Secondary infections are common in aquaculture and are often difficult to prevent and control due to their complex etiology. These infections have become a major factor affecting farming efficiency. Moreover, establishing stable and reproducible models of secondary infection under laboratory conditions remains challenging. This limitation hinders the in-depth investigation of pathogenic mechanisms and the development of effective prevention strategies [ 1 ]. This study was initially designed to evaluate the beneficial effects of low-dose dietary Scy-hepc on the health status of L. crocea . However, during the experimental period, which coincided with the peak disease outbreak season at the farm (September to October, characterized by high water temperatures), both the experimental fish and other farmed fish experienced widespread disease. We isolated and identified large numbers of C.irritans and V. parahaemolyticus from the diseased fish. These findings indicated that the fish were undergoing a typical secondary infection involving both parasitic and bacterial pathogens. All other experimental conditions were kept consistent. As a result, this naturally occurring outbreak provided an opportunity to assess whether dietary Scy-hepc could enhance disease resistance in L. crocea under real-world infection pressure. The experiment offered new insights into the practical application of AMPs for disease control in aquaculture. Building on our previous work on gut microbiota and AMP-based dietary interventions in marine fish [ 16 ], we further examined the effects of different feed additives on survival rate and immune gene expression in L. crocea . Our findings not only deepen the understanding of how AMPs function under actual farming conditions but also provide a new theoretical basis for the prevention of secondary infections in aquaculture species. Methods Experimental design for Scy-hepc feeding trial The AMP Scy-hepc used in this study was produced based on our previous work using a Pichia pastoris expression system [ 15 ]. The target protein was secreted into the culture supernatant using a yeast secretion expression system. After centrifugation to remove yeast cells, Scy-hepc was harvested and purified from the supernatant. Under optimized expression conditions, Scy-hepc constituted the majority of the total soluble protein in the supernatant. The vector lacking the Scy-hepc gene was constructed and expressed using the same method, and the resultant product was used as a negative control. The feeding trial was conducted following the procedures described in our previous studies on L. crocea and E. akaara [ 17 ]. Five treatment groups were established: Scy-hepc group, vector group, florfenicol group, Bacillus subtilis group, and control group. All feed additives were standardized to an effective concentration of 10 mg/kg in the diet. The treatment details were as follows: Scy-hepc group: purified Scy-hepc was mixed with the commercial basal diet at a final concentration of 10 mg/kg. Vector group: the yeast-expressed empty vector product was added to the basal diet at the same concentration. Florfenicol group: florfenicol (active ingredient 30%) was purchased from Hebei Jiupeng Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd. and incorporated into the commercial basal diet at 33 mg/kg to yield an effective concentration of 10 mg/kg of active compound. B. subtilis group: B. subtilis (viable count 10 12 CFU/g) was purchased from Henan Anmuyu Biotechnology Co., Ltd. and added at 10 mg/kg, resulting in a final viable count of approximately 10 7 CFU/g in the commercial basal diet. Control group: fed with the basal diet without any additives. The basal diet was a commercial feed formulated specifically for L. crocea , manufactured by Fuzhou Haima Feed Co., Ltd. As all experimental groups were fed the same commercial diet, the diets were considered isonitrogenous and isolipidic. And the proximate composition of the diet was as follows: crude protein 44%, crude lipid 3%, lysine 1.9%, crude fiber 3%, crude ash 16%, total phosphorus 2%, and moisture 10%. Experimental L. crocea were obtained from Fufa Aquaculture Co., Ltd. (Ningde, Fujian, China) with an average body weight of 33.9 ± 7.8 g and an average length of 14.9 ± 1.45 cm. Prior to the experiment, fish were acclimated for one week in 1200 L fully-aerated circular seawater tanks. Each treatment group included 120 fish, evenly distributed into three replicate tanks (40 fish per tank). Throughout the experiment, fish were fed and the water was exchanged twice daily. Water quality parameters were maintained within optimal ranges: temperature at 24–27 °C, salinity at 21–23‰, dissolved oxygen at 5.12–5.86 mg/L, and pH between 7.74 and 7.91. Notably, the present study constitutes an observational evaluation under natural infection conditions, rather than a strictly controlled pathogen challenge experiment. This study was originally designed to evaluate the health benefits of low-dose dietary supplementation with Scy-hepc in L. crocea . During the experimental period, which coincided with the peak disease outbreak season at the aquaculture farm (September–October) under relatively high water temperatures, widespread disease occurred among both the experimental fish and other cultured stocks. As all other experimental conditions remained unchanged, this unexpected event provided an opportunity to assess the effects of Scy-hepc supplementation on the health status of L. crocea under natural conditions of complex pathogen infection. Observation of clinical signs, microscopic examination, and bacterial isolation and identification The L. crocea exhibited distinct clinical symptoms following disease onset. These included the initial appearance of numerous white spots on the skin and fins, persistent abnormal behaviors such as rubbing against tank walls, and excessive mucus secretion on the body surface. Subsequently, inflammatory manifestations, such as redness, hemorrhage, and ulceration, were observed on the head, fins, and particularly around the mouth. Affected fish showed rapid progression to mortality. To identify the causative pathogens, on-site samples were collected from moribund fish ( n = 5) and fish exhibiting mild clinical symptoms ( n = 5). Skin mucus and fin tissues were collected and subjected to wet mount examination. Briefly, mucus, epidermis, and fin tissue samples were aseptically scraped using sterile coverslips, placed onto glass slides, and covered with a drop of sterile saline. After adding a coverslip, samples were examined under a microscope (Olympus BX40, Olympus, Japan) to observe parasite morphology and perform preliminary identification [ 22 ]. To further confirm the presence of C. irritans , skin tissue samples from diseased fish were collected ( n = 6). Total genomic DNA was extracted and subjected to specific PCR amplification using C. irritans -targeted primers for molecular identification [ 23 ]. To investigate potential bacterial infections, gut, ulcerative skin lesions from moribund fish ( n = 5) and skin tissues from fish with mild clinical symptoms ( n = 5) were sampled for bacterial isolation. Samples were streaked onto brain heart infusion agar (BHIA; BD Difco, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA) plates and incubated at 27 °C. Single colonies were then transferred into liquid BHI broth and cultured at 27 °C for 24 h, with three consecutive passages performed to obtain pure bacterial isolates. Genomic DNA from purified bacterial isolates was extracted, and the 16S rRNA gene was amplified and sequenced for taxonomic identification, following previously described protocols [ 24 ]. Sequences were compared against the NCBI BLAST database ( http://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/BLAST ) for identification, and evolutionary trees were constructed using the Neighbor-Joining method with a bootstrap of 1000 replicates in MEGA (v11.0.13). All primers used in the study are listed in Supplementary Table S1. All the fish were anesthetized for 2 min using 0.1 g/L MS-222 (tricaine methanesulfonate; Sigma-Aldrich, USA), following protocols described in our previous study [ 17 ]. Under sterile conditions, the gut, ulcerative skin lesions and skin tissues were carefully dissected. Then samples were immediately snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and subsequently stored at − 80 °C. Sample collection and DNA/RNA extraction On day 1 and day 35 of the feeding experiment, three fish were randomly sampled from each tank within every treatment group, totaling nine fish per group. All sampled fish appeared clinically healthy with no visible signs of organ pathology. Fish were anesthetized for 2 min using 0.1 g/L MS-222 (tricaine methanesulfonate; Sigma-Aldrich, USA). Under sterile conditions, the guts were carefully dissected and evenly divided into foregut, midgut, and hindgut sections. The contents of each gut segment were gently squeezed out and collected into sterile cryotubes to ensure complete evacuation, following protocols described in our previous study [ 17 ], Samples were immediately snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and subsequently stored at − 80 °C for downstream gut microbiota analysis. Additionally, liver and spleen tissues were collected from fish in each group on day 35 to assess the expression of immune-related genes. For each tissue type, three biological replicates were prepared, each consisting of pooled tissues from three fish. All tissue sampling and processing were performed by a trained technician following standardized procedures to ensure experimental consistency and data comparability.Body weight (BW), body length (BL), and survival rates were recorded and evaluated for each treatment group on day 35. Microbial genomic DNA was extracted using the QIAamp DNA Stool Mini Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany), following the manufacturer’s instructions and protocols described in our previous study [ 16 ], which ensured the extracted DNA met the quality requirements for subsequent 16S rRNA sequencing. And total RNA from liver and spleen samples was extracted using the TRIzol Reagent (Invitrogen, USA), according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The purity and concentration of the extracted RNA were assessed using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (NanoDrop Technologies, USA). RNA integrity was further verified by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis. After quality assessment, the extracted total RNA was used for quantitative real-time PCR to evaluate the expression levels of immune-related genes in different groups. Quantitative real-time PCR analysis Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) was performed using the CFX Opus 384 Real-Time PCR System (Bio-Rad, Singapore) to assess the expression levels of immune-related genes in the liver and spleen of L. crocea . Target genes included pro- and anti-inflammatory cytokines ( IL-1β , TNF-α , TGFβ , and IL-10 ), AMPs ( Hepcidin and NK-lysin ) and antioxidant-related genes ( Mn-SOD , CAT , GPx-1a , and GR ). The housekeeping genes β-actin and ubiquitin were used as internal controls for normalization. cDNA synthesis and qPCR reactions were carried out using the HiScript III RT SuperMix for qPCR (+ gDNA wiper) kit (Vazyme, China), following the manufacturer's protocol. Gene expression levels were calculated using the 2 −ΔΔCt method. All experiments were performed with at least two independent biological replicates. The primer sequences used are listed in Supplementary Table S1. 16S rRNA gene sequencing To analyze changes in the intestinal microbial composition, samples from experimental fish collected on day 1 and day 35 were subjected to 16S rRNA gene sequencing. The V4 hypervariable region of the bacterial 16S rRNA gene was targeted using specific primers for PCR amplification, followed by construction of the amplicon library. Paired-end sequencing of the constructed libraries was performed using the Illumina MiSeq 2000 platform. Sequencing services were provided by Guangzhou Genonova Biotechnology Co., Ltd. The primers, PCR conditions, library preparation protocols, and reagents used were consistent with those described in our previous study [ 16 ], ensuring continuity and comparability of data. Sequence data processing Raw sequencing data quality control and analysis were performed using the Quantitative Insights Into Microbial Ecology (QIIME, v1.8.0) pipeline [ 25 ]. First, high-quality paired-end reads were merged and barcode information was integrated using FLASH software. Subsequently, sequences were clustered into Operational Taxonomic Units (OTUs) at a 97% similarity threshold using USEARCH (v9.0). Representative OTU sequences were then subjected to taxonomic annotation via the Greengenes database (v13.8) and classified using the RDP Classifier (v2.2) with a confidence threshold of 0.5 [ 26 ]. To mitigate biases arising from uneven sequencing depth among samples, all datasets were rarefied to 6,000 sequences per sample. The resulting OTU abundance profiles were obtained across six taxonomic levels for downstream community structure analyses. Comparison of gut communities and bioinformatics analysis Based on quality-controlled OTU data, alpha diversity indices, including the Shannon index, were calculated using the QIIME software package [ 25 ], with the significant differences between two groups were determined using a two-tailed Student’s t-test. For beta diversity analysis, Bray–Curtis distances were employed to assess differences in community structure. Principal Coordinates Analysis (PCoA) was performed using QIIME and the vegan package in R to visualize variations in microbial composition among samples. The different groups were statistically compared through the similarity analysis (ANOSIM). The overlap of the microbial communities was determined by the R values from the ANOSIM, according to the method from Buttigieg and Ramette [ 27 ], with R &gt; 0.75 indicating well separated, 0.50 &lt; R ≤ 0.75 indicating separated but overlapped, 0.25 &lt; R ≤ 0.50 indicating separated but strongly overlapped, and 0.25 = R indicating barely separated. LEfSe-derived LDA scores were used to generate a cladogram (circular hierarchical tree) illustrating significantly enriched or depleted bacterial taxa in the gut microbiota [ 28 ]. Taxonomic levels from phylum to genus are shown from the inner to outer rings. Colors denote different groups, and labeled taxa represent biomarkers contributing to group differences (LDA &gt; 2.0). To explore the interactions within the gut microbiota of Larimichthys crocea under secondary infection conditions, a microbial co-occurrence network was constructed. Pairwise correlations between OTUs were calculated using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient, and associations with r &gt; 0.6 and P &lt; 0.01 were considered significant co-occurrences [ 29 ]. In the network, nodes represent distinct bacterial taxa (OTUs), and edges indicate significant positive or negative correlations between nodes. Network analysis was conducted using the igraph, vegan, and Hmisc packages in R, and network visualization was performed with Gephi software [ 30 ]. Additionally, taxonomic trees illustrating community differences among treatment groups were generated using the metacoder R package [ 31 ]. To investigate correlations between gut microbiota composition and host immune status, Mantel tests were conducted using the ggcor package in R, evaluating associations between microbial community structure and immune gene expression levels [ 28 ]. Unless otherwise specified, all statistical analyses and visualizations were performed in the R environment (version 4.5.1). Statistical analysis Statistical analyses were conducted with SPSS (Version 26.0) and GraphPad Prism (Version 9.0.0). Data comparisons between groups were made using one-way ANOVA followed by Dunnett’s multiple comparisons test or Student’s t -test. A p -value of less than 0.05 was deemed to indicate statistical significance. Results Feeding with AMP Scy-hepc is associated with reduced host mortality under natural secondary infection by C. irritans During the AMP feeding trial, mass mortality was observed in all feed additive groups starting from day 5. Affected fish exhibited typical clinical symptoms, including numerous white spots on the skin and fins, persistent abnormal behaviors such as rubbing against tank walls, excessive mucus secretion, and pronounced inflammatory responses such as swelling, hemorrhage, and ulceration, particularly around the head, fins, and oral regions, followed by rapid death (Fig. S1a). Based on concurrent disease outbreaks in the aquaculture facility and long-term farming experience, the cause was preliminarily identified as a secondary infection triggered by C. irritans . Microscopic examination revealed a large number of characteristic protozoan parasites attached to the skin surface of both moribund fish and fish exhibiting mild clinical symptoms. PCR amplification using species-specific primers confirmed the infection as C. irritans (Fig. S1b, c). In addition, large numbers of V. parahaemolyticus were isolated from skin ulcer lesions of moribund fish, whereas no V. parahaemolyticus was isolated from skin tissues of fish exhibiting mild clinical symptoms. These findings further support the diagnosis of a secondary infection following parasitic invasion (Fig. S1b, d). Given that all experimental groups were maintained under identical environmental and management conditions, and similar outbreaks occurred in other rearing tanks within the facility. These characteristic features and findings confirmed that the event was a natural outbreak of V. parahaemolyticus secondary infection induced by C. irritans in a marine aquaculture farm. This naturally occurring secondary infection provided a potential opportunity to evaluate the effects of different dietary additives on disease resistance in L. crocea under practical farming conditions. Therefore, we systematically assessed the effects of dietary supplementation with AMPs, antibiotics, probiotics, empty vectors, and basal diets on host resistance to secondary infection (Fig. 1 a). The results showed that dietary supplementation with the AMP Scy-hepc may significantly improved survival in L. crocea under secondary infection. By day 35, survival rates ranked as follows: Scy-hepc (41.32%) &gt; Vector (31.4%) &gt; Control (29.75%) &gt; B. subtilis (26.44%) &gt; Florfenicol (19.83%) (Fig. 1 b). Although there were no statistically significant differences in individual body weight or body length among groups, the total weight of fish in the Scy-hepc group was higher than that of the other treatments (Fig. 1 c and Fig. S2). This phenomenon is likely primarily attributable to the relatively higher survival of L. crocea fed by Scy-hepc under secondary infection, rather than to enhanced growth performance of individual fish. Notably, the Scy-hepc group showed a clear anti-inflammatory and immune-enhancing pattern, characterized by significantly reduced expression of the pro-inflammatory genes IL-1β and TNF-α , together with increased expression of the anti-inflammatory genes IL-10 and TGF-β , as well as the AMP genes Hepcidin and NK-lysin in liver and spleen tissues. In contrast, the other treatment groups showed more variable responses, generally involving partial downregulation of pro-inflammatory genes and partial increases in AMP-related genes (Fig. 2 a). In addition, we assessed the expression of antioxidant-related genes in the liver and spleen. In the liver, the Scy-hepc group showed a significant upregulation of CAT compared to the Control group, while no significant changes were observed in the other groups. In the spleen, Mn-SOD expression was significantly elevated in the Scy-hepc group. The Vector, Florfenicol, and B. subtilis groups also exhibited significantly increased Mn-SOD expression but showed a significant decrease in CAT expression (Fig. 2 b). Based on our previous findings that long-term feeding with Scy-hepc primarily altered the microbial composition of the foregut and midgut, thereby promoting growth [ 16 ], we systematically assessed its effects on the microbiota of different gut parts under the secondary infection. After 35 days of feeding, substantial shifts in microbial composition were observed across all gut parts (foregut, midgut, hindgut, and gut content) compared with Day 1 (Fig. 3 ), indicating that infection markedly disturbed the gut microbial community. Among the treatments, the Scy-hepc group showed the most distinct microbial profile, characterized by a notable enrichment of Tenericutes , particularly in the foregut midgut and hindgut. In contrast, the Vector and Control groups were mainly associated with increased Spirochaetes in the foregut and midgut. While the Florfenicol and B. subtilis groups showed less consistent phylum-level shifts, with a notable enrichment of Other in the foregut midgut and hindgut. Across treatments, a general decline in Proteobacteria and increase in Cyanobacteria within gut content were observed, suggesting a broad microbiota disturbance under infection pressure (Table S3). Notably, the enrichment of Tenericutes in the Scy-hepc group may be associated with enhanced host disease resistance. In addition, the increased proportion of Other observed in several groups suggests that gut microbial homeostasis was disrupted under secondary infection (Fig. S3). Among all groups at Day 35, the Florfenicol group had the highest α diversity, while the B. subtilis group had the lowest (Fig. S4). Upset plot analysis revealed that the Florfenicol group had the highest number of unique OTUs, whereas the Scy-hepc group had the fewest (Fig. S5). Principal coordinates analysis (PCoA) was used to assess differences in microbial community structure across treatment groups. The results showed that the Scy-hepc group differed significantly from all other groups, with the greatest separation observed when compared with the Control and Florfenicol groups (R &gt; 0.247, P &lt; 0.005), whereas relatively smaller separation was observed when compared with the vector and B. subtilis groups (R &lt; 0.1612, P &lt; 0.05) (Fig. 4 c). Microbial resistance index (Rs) analysis revealed that under secondary infection, feeding with Scy-hepc significantly reduced microbial resistance. No significant differences were observed in the other groups (Fig. 4 d). These networks were used to compare microbial community structures and interaction patterns among different feed additive treatments (Fig. 6 a). The results showed that under secondary infection, the microbial co-occurrence networks varied significantly across treatment groups. Specifically, the Scy-hepc group exhibited a more complex network structure than the Vector, Control, and B. subtilis groups. Its network topology was denser, indicating more extensive and tighter microbial associations. When compared to the Florfenicol group, the Scy-hepc group showed a similar level of network complexity. Both groups had higher OTU richness and greater node connectivity. In all five groups, the number of positive correlations between microbial taxa was much higher than negative ones. More than 61.3% of the edges in each network represented positive correlations (Table S4), suggesting that cooperative interactions dominate within the microbial communities. Compared to the Vector, B. subtilis and Control groups, the Scy-hepc group showed stronger phylogenetic relationships among bacterial taxa. There were tighter interactions among key microbial species, including those from the phylum Proteobacteria . These strong inter-species associations may contribute to the maintenance of host health. A blue edge indicates a positive interaction between two individual nodes, while a red edge indicates a negative interaction. b the mantel test between the microbial communities and characterizing host immunity factors Host–microbiota interactions reveal the potential anti-Infection mechanism of Scy-hepc Previous studies have shown that interactions between the gut microbiota and the host immune system play a key role in determining treatment outcomes [ 19 ]. Therefore, we further investigated whether immune-related parameters associated with host status were correlated with microbial community composition, aiming to elucidate the interplay between the host immune system and gut microbiota. Mantel test results showed that in all five feeding groups, most host immune parameters were significantly correlated with microbial communities. However, the patterns varied across groups. In the Scy-hepc group, microbial composition was significantly associated with survival rate, body weight, and the expression of NK-lysin , Hepcidin , GPx-1a , and CAT (Fig. 6 b). In the Vector group, microbial communities were significantly correlated with hepcidin and IL1β . In the Control group, they were correlated with TNFα and Mn-SOD . In the Florfenicol group, significant correlations were observed with survival rate, body weight, CAT , and TGFβ . However, no significant correlations among the immune-related or health-related parameters were detected in the B. subtilis group. These findings suggest that Scy-hepc may help protect against secondary infections by modulating gut microbiota and enhancing host immune responses. In addition, functional prediction analysis revealed that the Scy-hepc group showed increased abundance of microbial functions related to metabolism and host-beneficial pathways compared to other feeding groups (Fig. 7 ). These functions included nucleotide transport and metabolism, signal transduction mechanisms, coenzyme transport and metabolism, inorganic ion transport and metabolism, carbohydrate transport and metabolism, replication, recombination and repair, translation, ribosomal structure and biogenesis, and cell wall/membrane/envelope biogenesis. Fig. 7 Differences in the abundance of KEGG functions at level among five feed additives feeding under the secondary infection Discussion Secondary infections, involving diverse pathogens and complex mechanisms, have become one of the most challenging diseases to control in modern aquaculture. Under real farming conditions, fish diseases are often caused by co-infections rather than a single pathogen. Each pathogen contributes differently to disease progression [ 32 ]. Among these, parasite-induced secondary bacterial infections represent one of the most common co-infection patterns [ 33 ]. These infections not only exacerbate clinical symptoms and increase mortality but also reduce the effectiveness of standard treatments. As a result, they pose a serious obstacle to sustainable aquaculture and lead to significant economic losses [ 34 ]. Although increasing attention has been paid to disease control in L. crocea , most studies focus on single-pathogen infections, such as C. irritans . In contrast, secondary infections involving multiple pathogens in L. crocea remain underexplored [ 35 ]. At the same time, antibiotic overuse continues to drive the emergence of resistance. There is an urgent need for safe, eco-friendly, and effective alternatives to antibiotics. In this study, we confirmed that all experimental tanks experienced V. parahaemolyticus secondary infection induced by C. irritans . This was validated by microscopic examination, bacterial isolation, and PCR-based identification. The infection patterns matched concurrent outbreaks in the farm. We therefore took advantage of a naturally occurring secondary infection induced by C. irritans to evaluate the effects of different feed additives on disease resistance, immune responses, and gut microbial homeostasis in L. crocea . Due to the challenges of the complexity of pathogens and reproducibility under practical aquaculture conditions, few studies have evaluated the efficacy and potential mechanisms of feed additives during naturally occurring infections. However, the materials and data obtained from this outbreak still provide new insights and offer practical value for developing effective disease management strategies in aquaculture. Compared with previous studies based on laboratory infection models or single-pathogen exposure, the secondary infection in this study presents higher biological complexity and better real-world relevance. In our previous study, Scy-hepc feeding improved survival in E. akaara challenged with Edwardsiella tarda under a single-pathogen model [ 17 ]. Here, we show that Scy-hepc may significantly improves survival in L. crocea facing secondary infection of V. parahaemolyticus triggered by C. irritans . These results suggest that Scy-hepc may have the potential to enhance host immune responses under conditions of complex pathogen exposure. In contrast, survival rates in the Florfenicol and B. subtilis groups were relatively low, possibly due to suboptimal dosing that did not meet recommended levels. In terms of growth performance, unlike our previous findings in L. crocea and grouper, Scy-hepc did not significantly enhance the growth performance of individual fish under secondary infection [ 16 , 17 ]. However, the total weight in the Scy-hepc group increased. We speculate that under secondary infection pressure, fish may allocate more energy to immune defense rather than growth, thereby diminishing individual growth advantages [ 36 ]. Consequently, the increase in total weight is more likely attributable to the relatively higher survival observed in the Scy-hepc group under secondary infection, rather than to enhanced growth of individual fish. Immune gene expression analysis further confirmed the immunomodulatory role of Scy-hepc. In the liver and spleen, Scy-hepc feeding significantly downregulated pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-1β and TNF-α , while upregulating anti-inflammatory genes TGF-β and IL-10 , as well as AMP genes Hepcidin and NK-lysin . It is well established that bacterial infections in fish trigger innate immune activation through pro-inflammatory responses. This leads to immune cell infiltration and cytokine release, often disrupting immune homeostasis [ 37 – 39 ]. TNF-α and IL-1β are key signaling molecules in inflammation. They also play essential roles in pathogen recognition and immune cell activation [ 40 , 41 ]. For example, Pseudomonas plecoglossicida infection in L. crocea induces high expression of IL-1β in the liver and gut [ 39 ]. In our study, both IL-1β and TNF-α were elevated in the liver and spleen under persistent infection, indicating strong immune activation. Feeding Scy-hepc significantly suppressed their expression, helping to alleviate the inflammatory burden. IL-1β can stimulate immune cells to release additional pro-inflammatory cytokines via a positive feedback loop [ 42 ]. TNF-α promotes the expression of E-selectin and various chemokines, enhancing the recruitment and activation of phagocytes [ 43 ]. In addition to cytokines, AMPs such as Hepcidin and NK-lysin are critical components of the host defense system in fish [ 44 ]. Scy-hepc feeding induced the transcription of both genes, further strengthening the antimicrobial barrier. Moreover, bacterial infections often cause oxidative stress through excessive production of reactive oxygen species (ROS), resulting in tissue damage and even apoptosis [ 45 ]. Scy-hepc feeding upregulated the transcription of CAT in the liver and Mn-SOD in the spleen, suggesting improved antioxidant defense. In comparison, Florfenicol, B. subtilis , and Vector groups showed weaker regulation of cytokine, antimicrobial, and antioxidant genes than Scy-hepc. In summary, Scy-hepc significantly improves survival of L. crocea under secondary infection induced by C. irritans . It reduces inflammatory responses and oxidative stress, highlighting its potential as an antibiotic alternative in aquaculture. Recent studies have shown that individual hosts are often co-infected with multiple pathogens, which interact to shape immune responses, clinical outcomes, survival, and disease control [ 46 ]. This phenomenon was clearly observed in our study. Previous research indicated that C. irritans infection alone did not cause significant gut dysbiosis in Epinephelus coioides . In those cases, Firmicutes remained relatively abundant while Proteobacteria levels were low [ 47 ]. In contrast, in our secondary infection caused by C. irritans , both Firmicutes and Proteobacteria were markedly reduced across all feeding groups of L. crocea , accompanied by clear signs of gut microbial dysbiosis. Accumulating evidence suggests that gut microbiota disruption is a key driver of disease in fish, regardless of whether the infection is caused by a single pathogen or multiple co-infecting agents [ 48 , 49 ]. Notably, we found a high number of unclassified OTUs assigned to the category Other across all feeding groups. This is uncommon in previous fish disease models, such as C. irritans -infected E. coioides or P. plecoglossicida -infected L. crocea , where gut microbial structures were more clearly resolved [ 39 , 47 ]. Correlation analysis showed that Other taxa in the Florfenicol and B. subtilis groups were negatively correlated with most immune and health-related indicators. Notably, a significant negative correlation with survival rate was observed (Fig. S6). We speculate that the abundance of Other OTUs reflects the complex pathological processes of natural secondary infection. Under disease conditions, unclassified or low-abundance OTUs are often associated with an increase in opportunistic microorganisms or environmentally derived bacteria. These taxa may include unknown or low-abundance opportunistic pathogens and may participate in complex polymicrobial infection processes [ 50 ]. Disruption of the immune barrier may allow opportunistic microbes from the environment and feed to invade the gut, leading to gut homeostasis imbalance, the core microbial clusters are less abundant and the numbers of OTUs increased. Additionally, our use of 16S rRNA amplicon sequencing may limit the resolution of low-abundance taxa. Future studies incorporating metagenomic sequencing could provide deeper insights into microbial community composition and function. Analysis of different feed additives and associated survival outcomes further revealed that increased abundance of Tenericutes and Spirochaetes in the anterior and midgut may correlate with host disease resistance. Spirochaetes are generally considered potential pathogens or dysbiosis-related bacteria, especially when abnormally enriched [ 51 , 52 ]. Their elevated presence in all feeding groups indicates widespread microbial imbalance across gut segments. Conversely, certain members of Tenericutes , such as Mycoplasma , have been suggested to possess probiotic functions, potentially contributing to immune regulation and nutrient absorption in fish [ 53 ]. Our previous work showed that Scy-hepc improves microbial composition in the foregut and midgut of L. crocea [ 16 ]. In this study, even under conditions of gut dysbiosis due to secondary infection, Scy-hepc still enriched potentially beneficial bacteria such as Tenericutes in the foregut and midgut, supporting enhanced host resistance. By day 35, Scy-hepc-feeded fish also showed increased abundance of other beneficial taxa, including Bacillaceae and Bacteroidales . In contrast, Florfenicol and B. subtilis groups were enriched with potential pathogens such as Clostridiales , Fusobacteriaceae , Spirochaetes , and Vibrionaceae [ 54 – 56 ]. These findings were further supported by Upset analysis. The Florfenicol and B. subtilis groups exhibited the highest number of unique OTUs, while the Scy-hepc group had the fewest. Previous studies on Salmo trutta infected with Tetracapsuloides bryosalmonae have shown that heavier secondary infections result in more OTU clustering and greater loss of microbial homeostasis [ 49 ]. Together, these data suggest that Scy-hepc may help maintain gut microbial stability by limiting abnormal OTU expansion and preserving the structure of core microbial communities. Previous studies have shown that the AMP Scy-hepc can enhance the stability of the gut microbiota and improve its metabolic potential in fish [ 16 ]. In this study, we further applied molecular ecological co-occurrence network analysis to compare the microbial community structure, assembly patterns, and interspecies interactions under different dietary treatments in L. crocea [ 57 ]. The results showed that the Scy-hepc group exhibited a more complex microbial network compared to the Vector and Control groups. This suggests enhanced microbial stability and stronger resistance to external disturbances. Such a highly connected network may contribute to improved host resilience against secondary infections, providing a synergistic defense effect [ 58 ]. Interestingly, the Florfenicol group showed a similar level of network complexity to the Scy-hepc group. However, the underlying mechanisms may be quite different. Scy-hepc likely promotes the growth of beneficial microbes, fostering a stable and cooperative microbiota. In contrast, Florfenicol may disrupt the original microbial community and allow opportunistic bacteria to invade, resulting in a superficially complex network. Mantel test analysis further revealed significant correlations between gut microbial profiles and the expression of immune-related genes Hepcidin and NK-lysin in the Scy-hepc group. This suggests that Scy-hepc may enhance host immunity by stabilizing the microbiota and activating AMP genes closely linked to innate immune defense. These findings support the hypothesis that functional feed additives can promote disease resistance by modulating host immunity and microbiota composition [ 21 , 59 , 60 ]. Moreover, PICRUSt-based KEGG pathway prediction indicated that Scy-hepc significantly improved microbial metabolic functions in the intestine of L. crocea . This is consistent with previous findings under healthy conditions and demonstrates that Scy-hepc can enhance microbial metabolic potential regardless of the host’s physiological state. Such functional improvement may not only facilitate better nutrient utilization and growth performance but also strengthen disease resistance by supporting energy metabolism and maintaining cellular homeostasis [ 61 ]. Notably, although Florfenicol and B. subtilis were included as control treatments in this study, their performance under secondary infection did not meet expectations. For the Florfenicol group, the dosage used in the present study was substantially lower than the recommended level commonly applied in aquaculture practice (2–3 g/kg of commercial feed), which may have limited its effectiveness in mitigating secondary infection. In comparison, the B. subtilis group also showed relatively poor performance. This may be partly attributed to the dosage used in this experiment, which was markedly lower than the recommended application level of the commercial product (3 g/kg of commercial feed). In addition, the effects of probiotics are highly strain-specific, and their potential benefits generally depend on appropriate dosage, host physiological status, and environmental conditions [ 62 – 64 ]. Under infection or immune stress, probiotics may fail to establish stable colonization in the gut due to insufficient dosage, ecological niche competition, or environmental stress, thereby limiting their ability to suppress pathogens or improve host immune indicators [ 62 , 65 ]. When pathogen loads are high or the gut microbiota is severely disrupted, probiotics may further lose their ecological advantage, potentially exacerbating microbial imbalance and disease progression [ 66 ]. These mechanisms may partly explain why the B. subtilis group in this study did not exhibit improvements in survival or gut microbial structure under secondary infection. In this study, secondary infection was mainly associated with V. parahaemolyticus . Therefore, we further quantified the bacterial load of V. parahaemolyticus and evaluated its variation together with the dynamics of Vibrio -related taxa in the gut microbiota (Fig. S7). The results showed that the bacterial load of V. parahaemolyticus in the gut was significantly lower in the Scy-hepc group than in the vector, control, and B. subtilis groups. The control group showed the highest bacterial load, reaching 4.5 × 10 4 copies/ng. In contrast, the lowest bacterial load was observed in the Florfenicol group. In the liver and spleen, the bacterial load of V. parahaemolyticus was generally low and remained below 3 × 10 2 copies/ng. In the liver, the pattern was similar to that observed in the gut. In the spleen, however, the Florfenicol group showed a relatively higher bacterial load (Fig. S7a). We further analyzed the relative abundance of Vibrio in the gut microbiota. The results also showed that the relative abundance of Vibrio in the Scy-hepc group was lower than that in the control, vector, and B. subtilis groups (Fig. S7b). These results suggest that dietary Scy-hepc may inhibit the proliferation of Vibrio -related taxa. This effect may partially modulate the gut microbial community. It may also be consistent with the higher survival rate observed in this group. However, in the Florfenicol group, the relative abundance of Vibrio and the bacterial load of V. parahaemolyticus in the gut was the lowest, while the survival rate was also the lowest. This pattern differed from the other treatments. One possible explanation is the strong inhibitory effect of florfenicol on Vibrio -related taxa. In addition, antibiotic intervention may disrupt the overall structure of the gut microbiota [ 67 ]. This disturbance may reduce microbial community stability. Parasitic infections can significantly damage the skin and mucosal surfaces of fish, creating entry points for bacterial adhesion and colonization [ 68 ]. As parasite load increases, bacterial burden within the host also rises, potentially leading to severe secondary infections and high mortality. Therefore, parasite-induced secondary bacterial infections represent a complex and difficult-to-manage disease in aquaculture. This underscores the urgent need for greener and more sustainable intervention strategies. In this study, we evaluated the in vivo disease resistance effect of the AMP Scy-hepc during a naturally occurring outbreak of C. irritans -induced V. parahaemolyticus secondary infection under real farming conditions. Unlike conventional laboratory-based artificial infection models, our natural infection event reflects real-world disease scenarios relevant to the industry. However, this study still has several limitations. First, our determination of whether this natural outbreak represented a secondary infection or a co-infection relied primarily on clinical symptoms and pathogen detection from skin samples collected at different disease stages. Large numbers of C. irritans were detected on the body surface of both moribund fish and those with mild clinical symptoms, whereas abundant V. parahaemolyticus was isolated only from ulcerative skin lesions of moribund individuals. These findings indicate a possible sequential relationship between the pathogens. Nevertheless, the absence of systematic and highly precise diagnostic approaches makes it difficult to clearly define the order of infection or to distinguish the individual pathogenic contributions of each organism. Second, because natural infections occur spontaneously and cannot be controlled, it is difficult to reproduce all of the data. Moreover, the experiment was conducted under real aquaculture conditions. The complexity of the farming environment may lead to discrepancies when compared with secondary infection models developed later under controlled laboratory conditions. Despite these limitations, the materials and data collected remain informative. They offer a representation of gut microbiota changes in L. crocea during a complex multi-pathogen outbreak in the field. They also provide a valuable basis for evaluating the efficacy and potential mechanisms of antimicrobial peptides used as feed additives under real-world infection scenarios. Thus, future studies should focus on establishing controlled co-infection models with C. irritans and V. parahaemolyticus under laboratory conditions to better elucidate the mechanisms by which Scy-hepc confers disease resistance in the context of secondary infections. And future studies should also verify the pathogenic characteristics and infection sequence of the isolated pathogens. Moreover, future work will incorporate metagenomics, transcriptomics, and integrated multi-omics approaches to dissect how Scy-hepc modulates gut microbial composition, functional pathways, and host–microbiota interactions. These efforts will help provide both theoretical and technical support for the precise application of antimicrobial peptides in aquaculture. Conclusions In summary, this study systematically evaluated the disease resistance effect of the AMP Scy-hepc in L. crocea under a naturally occurring secondary infection caused by C. irritans in a real farming environment. Although the gut microbiota of the fish exhibited clear dysbiosis under secondary infection, daily feeding with Scy-hepc improved disease resistance. This improvement was associated with an increase in potential probiotics, a reduction in opportunistic pathogens, enhanced metabolic functions, modulation of inflammation-related gene expression, and upregulation of AMP genes. This study not only provided an ideal opportunity to assess the in-field efficacy of AMPs but also offered the data support for their practical application in aquaculture production.
