Significance statement Biological and synthetic systems that sense and react to their noisy environments can operate like ratchets, selectively “opting out” of interactions to pursue a goal. When these optional interactions happen between agents in a large collective, group behavior emerges from many local measurements and decisions. Here, we construct a model hard sphere fluid and demonstrate how local information processing drives nonequilibrium collective states, including an informationally driven version of flocking, whose onset can be viewed as a measurement-induced phase transition. We find that such “informational” activity generically increases as noise grows, even for nonthermal noise sources. Our work advances understanding of decision-based collective behavior in situations where groups of information processing agents contend with intense noise, ranging from thermal fluctuations at nanoscopic scales to granular vibrations and turbulent flows. Introduction A macromolecular machine within a cell and a boat on troubled waters are both subject to relentless random forces driving chaotic dynamics: thermal fluctuations in the cell, waves and wind on the sea. At first sight, these strong fluctuations seem to be a challenge opposing the molecular machine’s or boat’s goals. However, both sailors and biological components can exploit noise for their own purposes by sensing their environment, taking actions informed by local measurements, and breaking time-reversal symmetry. This process turns them into “information engines” ( 1–6 ). Information engines operate not by applying forces but rather by restricting the volume of phase space available to the system through measurements. This excluded volume effect leads to entropic forces whose strength is proportional to temperature in a thermal system ( 7–13 ), like the pressure in an ideal gas ( 9 ), elasticity in rubber ( 14 ), or the phoretic forces underlying self-propelled colloids ( 15–18 ). Crucially, information engines can actively change the excluded volume in phase space depending on their state, setting up a feedback mechanism that drives the system out of equilibrium by rectifying noise and chaotic dynamics into a desired motion or behavior through local measurements and information processing ( 5 , 19 ). This mechanism is believed to play a key role in biological and behavioral processes ( 7 , 8 , 20–35 ). Considerable research has focused on single-body information engines, in which a lone agent (colloquially known as a Maxwell demon) exploits information as a resource to perform work toward a goal. However, theoretical descriptions of many-body information engines are comparatively rare ( 36–38 ). Many natural systems are composed of large numbers of cooperating agents that individually collect, process, and act on information (eg cells in a tissue, ant colonies, bird flocks, and human communities). Furthermore, over the last two decades, per-particle external feedback control has been demonstrated in synthetic active matter systems, such as programmable phoretic colloids ( 39–50 ). As colloidal scale objects are engineered to be more complex ( 51 ) and robotic platforms shrunk toward the colloidal regime ( 52 , 53 ), a new category of small-but-capable machines are emerging ( 54–56 ). Yet, little is known about collective phenomena that arise in such many-body systems as a result of their ability to perform local measurements and control actions. We henceforth refer to these many-body systems as informational active matter. In this class of systems, measurements and control actions can counteract the scrambling of information by the underlying chaotic dynamics (eg microscopic collisions). As a consequence, this information, typically encoded in low-entropy ordered states, can survive in a nonequilibrium steady state. To distill the nonequilibrium collective effects that arise purely from informational activity, we introduce a minimal extension of what is perhaps the simplest interacting many-body system, the hard disk gas. This extension, amenable to theoretical treatment, explicitly accounts for the feedback control processes whereby each agent (ie Maxwell demon) makes control decisions informed by the outcome of measurements. Such a gas of Maxwell demons can reach through consensus (but without exerting any work) a nematic flocking-like state that we analyze, from kinetic to hydrodynamic theory, by coarse-graining microscopic decision-making policies informed by measurements, rather than predetermined microscopic forces. We show that the steady-state magnitude of the nematic-flocking order parameter is bounded by the information measured, which suggests viewing the onset of informational flocking as a phase transition induced by measurements ( 57–61 ). This classical mechanism is reminiscent of quantum measurement-induced phase transitions (MIPT), where quantum measurements counteract the scrambling of information originating from the underlying unitary dynamics ( 62–64 ). The demon gas model Consider a gas of hard disks at finite temperature, undergoing random collisions with each other. The velocities of individual particles follow the Maxwell–Boltzmann (MB) distribution. Imagine now that these disks could “opt out” of collisions with other disks: this would allow them to select whatever velocity is desired by simply waiting until they obtain it randomly by collision, then subsequently avoiding scattering interactions. Such a system would act as a distributed version of the demon imagined by Maxwell, that opens and closes a trapdoor to create a temperature difference between two sides of a vessel filled with gas ( 3 , 27 , 65–68 ). Here, we implement such a demon gas by endowing every disk in the gas with the ability to change its size, and therefore its scattering cross-section, depending on its environment. As shown in Fig. 1 a, every period t m , every particle i measures its current state (position x ( i ) and velocity v ( i ) ) as well as its environment E ( i ) (ie some set of information about the current state of nearby particles). Based on these data, it changes its size according to a choice function D ( x ( i ) , v ( i ) , E ( i ) ) giving the new diameter of the particle. In order to bring the informational aspects to the fore, we focus on the less explored limit where the functions D do not result in particles exerting work on each other. In most experimental contexts, the informational (ie entropic) effects highlighted here will generally coexist with active forces of enthalpic origin on which active matter theories typically focus ( 40–42 , 44 , 48 , 50 , 51 ). In the limit of vanishing work, size changes are confined to the times between collisions, ie when such a change would not cause overlaps (Fig. 1 b), requiring that E ( i ) provides proximity information about neighbors. As a consequence, the quantities conserved in a standard hard disk gas (mass, linear momentum, and total kinetic energy) are also conserved in the demon gas (see SI Sections S1 and S2 ). Nonetheless, coupling particle size and velocity drives the system out of equilibrium, as shown in Fig. 1 c. Crucially, collisions are no longer “undone” by reversing particle velocities if sizes also change, and hence detailed balance is broken (a hallmark of active systems but here achieved with energy and momentum conservation intact). As we now show, specifying the choice function D allows us to control the behavior of the fluid. Informational flocking We start by implementing an informational version of flocking. Flocking is typically modeled as arising from interaction torques that bring the orientations of self-propelled particles into alignment. No such forces are present in our model. Instead, flocking is produced by control actions whereby the particles simply align their velocities with their neighbors by selectively reducing their size when they are aligned and increasing their size when they are not aligned. These control actions are local , in the sense that the particles (agents) need not know their global positions. Over many collision events, this selective biasing of scattering cross-section results in aligned groups. As the total linear momentum is conserved, it is not possible to change the average velocity in the system (see below for a variant where this constraint is lifted), so we have a nematic version of flocking in which particles tend to align their velocities v ( i ) modulo reflection v ( i ) → − v ( i ) . This is represented in Fig. 1 d–f: the current particle (centered) computes the average direction of neighboring particles and compares it to its own. Depending on whether the double-sided arrows are aligned or not (white and shaded regions in Fig. 1 e, f), the particle adopts (between collisions) a small diameter D S or a large diameter D L . Each particle takes a diameter D ( θ ) = D S + Δ D H ( cos 2 θ − 1 / 2 ) , where H is the Heaviside step function, Δ D = D L − D S ≥ 0 is the diameter difference, and θ is the angle between the particle’s direction of motion and the local average direction of motion in a region of volume Ω around the current particle (Fig. 1 e, f). Formally, the average direction of motion is determined as the major axis of the local pressure tensor P i j = − ρ ⟨ p i p j ⟩ Ω , in which p ≡ v − u Ω , u Ω ≡ ⟨ v ⟩ Ω , and ρ is the gas density. Indeed, the traceless part Q i j = P i j − ( P k k / d ) δ i j of the (symmetric) pressure tensor can be interpreted as a nematic order parameter and decomposed as Q i j = 2 Q [ n i ( ψ ) n j ( ψ ) − δ i j / d ] where d is the space dimension, n ^ ( ψ ) is a unit vector making an angle ψ with a fixed direction (eg the x axis), and Q 2 ≡ ‖ Q ‖ 2 ≡ Q i j Q j i / 2 ≥ 0 . Note that here, the symmetric traceless tensor Q describes the motion of the particles, in contrast with nematic liquid crystals where the nematic tensor describes their orientation. Figure 1 g, h shows the result of molecular dynamics (MD) simulations of this model with periodic boundary conditions (see Materials and methods and SI Section S1 for details). We observe that the gas acquires a finite nematic order parameter Q starting from a disordered initial configuration (the equilibrium state of the passive gas) as the rotation symmetry of the system is spontaneously broken, see Fig. 1 g for a plot of the local orientation ψ = arctan ( Q 12 , Q 11 ) / 2 (modulo π ) and Fig. 1 h for the time evolution of the histogram of ψ . Nematic defects in the orientation field eventually relax by annihilation (Fig. 1 g), leaving the system with a single dominant (but thermally fluctuating) orientation. Note that these simulations are too small to prove the emergence of true global orientational order ( 69 , 70 ) (see SI Section S3 ), and we leave the development of a fluctuation theory and accompanying scaling simulations to future work. This alignment is accompanied by a stable anisotropy in the system-wide pressure tensor. Fixed diameter disks, in contrast, can only exhibit an isotropic pressure tensor. This ability can be harnessed to perform work. For instance, the gas can asymmetrically inflate a symmetric balloon, as shown in Fig. 1 i. The resulting ellipsoidal shape of the balloon arises from the interplay between its surface tension (that favors a symmetric shape) and the nonequilibrium anisotropic pressure tensor of the gas (that favors an asymmetric shape). Such a mechanism could for instance be exploited to actuate a synthetic membrane from the inside. Thermodynamics of information-driven fluids In order to understand how the model in Fig. 1 harnesses information to maintain a nonequilibrium steady state and to perform work on a load, we turn to the framework of information thermodynamics ( 5 , 71–73 ). In a nutshell, this framework provides a formulation of the second law of thermodynamics that integrates the fact that the entropy of a system can be reduced by acquiring information about it, and subsequently driven out of equilibrium by using control actions conditioned on the measurement results. To do so, one first introduces the nonequilibrium free energy F [ ρ ] = ⟨ U ⟩ ρ − T S [ ρ ] associated to a (possibly nonequilibrium) statistical state represented by a probability distribution ρ , where ⟨ U ⟩ ρ is the mean internal energy in this state and S [ ρ ] the entropy of the distribution. In general, the joint distribution of all measured variables must be considered to compute entropy. Here, we restrict ourselves to the single-particle velocity distribution function (a mean field approximation assuming that the alignment of a local neighborhood changes much slower than the individual velocities within it). In terms of these quantities, our assumption that the particles are not permitted to exert work on each other means that the mean internal energy ⟨ U ⟩ ρ is constant. The second law of thermodynamics for feedback processes then states that the change Δ F between two states satisfies ( 5 , 72 ) (1) Δ F ≤ W + k B T I , where W is the work done on the system during the process, and I the mutual information between the microstates of the system and the results of the measurements used for the feedback control. The quantity k B I corresponds to the entropy reduction due to the control actions. The mutual information I can easily be computed in simple situations, but in general its evaluation is a challenging task, in particular because of correlations between successive measurements ( 5 , 71 , 72 , 74 , 75 ). In our case, all measurements are synchronized (Fig. 2 a), but they take time to influence observable gas properties, because their consequences are only felt during collisions. Hence, their effect is smeared out over the average time between collisions τ col . Once a particle has collided, its previous measurement becomes outdated because it may have changed direction, so the next collision will tend to equilibrate the gas. After a few τ col , we expect the system to behave like an equilibrium hard disk gas until the next measurement. Figure 2 b shows the reduction in entropy relative to the MB distribution S − S MB over time for t m ≫ τ col , showing successive dips in the entropy followed by a relaxation to equilibrium. Note that energy conservation implies that F − F MB = − T ( S − S MB ) . In Fig. 2 c, t m is reduced, and the peaks corresponding to entropy dips are less and less pronounced until a stationary balance between entropy reduction and relaxation toward thermodynamic equilibrium is observed (blue curves). When t m ≲ τ col , the system does not fully relax to the equilibrium MB state, inducing correlations between subsequent measurements. In order to assess the effect of measurements, we therefore take a phenomenological approach that captures the unidirectional coupling between the information-carrying degree of freedom (the particle diameters) and the velocities of the particles. We model the effect of each measurement as an instantaneous change of the free energy F ic of the information carriers by a value F m , which takes place every t m . The free energy stored in the diameters is purely informational (entropic). It relaxes to its equilibrium (MB) value F ic MB through collisions over a relaxation timescale τ , each time it is used to make a decision, and this leads to an equal increase in the free energy F associated with the velocities of the particles. Finally, F also relaxes to its equilibrium value F MB with the same collisional time scale τ , in the same way as in a normal hard disk gas. This translates into the equations of motion (2a ) F ˙ ic = − 1 τ [ F ic − F ic MB ] + F m Ш t m ( t ) and (2b ) F ˙ = − F ic ˙ − 1 τ [ F − F MB ] in which Ш t m represents a series of unit impulse trains spaced by t m . In the steady state, the departure Δ F * of F from the MB value just before a measurement does not change from a period to the next and one can show that Δ F * ∝ F m . a Fitting to numerical free energy data shown in Fig. 2 c provides estimates of the parameters F m and τ (see Fig. S1 in SI Section S1 ). To estimate the amount of information collected by the disks, we describe the binary choice by a two-state Markov process, where the two states correspond to the two possible sizes a particle can take (see SI Section S1 for detailed methods). This neglects correlations between particles but captures correlations between successive measurements. The transition probabilities T i j and steady-state distribution μ i of the Markov chain are estimated from the numerical simulations, yielding the mean information per step as ( 54 , 71 , 76 ), (3) I ≳ I MC ≡ − ∑ i j μ i T i j ln T i j . Strictly speaking, this is only a lower bound on the total information per step I . The results are shown in Fig. 2 d, where we observe that the free energy impulses F m scale with and remain bounded by the Markov estimate of information gained per measurement. Due to correlations between measurements the amount of information gained per measurement decreases when t m &lt; τ col . However, the average nonequilibrium free energy ⟨ F − F MB ⟩ increases with the rate of information acquisition (ie for rapid correlated measurements). For values of t m studied here, increasing the rate of measurement increased d I / d t (ie correlations do not entirely negate the advantage of more rapid acquisition). Figure 2 e shows that average nonequilibrium free energy scales with the rate of information gain. Overall, a larger diameter contrast Δ D leads to a larger effect (Fig. 2 b, d, and e), while measurement time determines the mean and excursion of F − F MB . Similar arguments can be used to obtain estimates of free energy expenditure in the case of a single demon particle immersed in a passive thermalized gas, see SI Section S4 . By analogy with Eq. 1 , we expect Δ F * ∝ F m ≤ k B T I . This bound on the deviation from equilibrium shows to what extent the measurement and feedback control process drives informational active matter out of equilibrium. In order to assess what nonequilibrium states are reached and how they evolve, we focus on the steady-state limit ( t m / τ col ≪ 1 ) and turn to a kinetic theory that includes the effects of active phase space compression. Kinetic theory with compressible phase space Within the framework of kinetic theory ( 77–79 ), we start with the Boltzmann equation, (4) ∂ t f + v ⋅ ∇ x f + F ⋅ ∇ v f = C [ f ] where f ( t , x , v ) is a single particle velocity distribution function, F represents external forces (that we neglect here), and C is the collision operator, which describes the manner in which particle collisions redistribute probability density in phase space. To account for the diameter feedback control process, we consider a modified version of the usual Boltzmann quadratic collision operator (5) C [ f ] = ∫ ( g + − g − ) d σ d v * in which g + = α ( v , v ′ , v * , v * ′ ) B ( σ , Δ v ) f ( v ′ ) f ( v * ′ ) / 2 and g − = B ( σ , Δ v ) f ( v ) f ( v * ) / 2 , where v and v * are the velocities of particles undergoing collision, v ′ and v * ′ are their postcollision velocities, and Δ v = v − v * . The collision kernel B = B ( σ , Δ v ) describes the scattering of particles for a given impact parameter σ . Crucially, the feedback-controlled bias of transitions between velocity states introduces distortions of phase space represented by an α ( v , v ′ , v * , v * ′ ) ≠ 1 term. When detailed balance holds, α = 1 and the steady-state solution of Eq. 4 is the MB distribution. This is not the case with the demon gas, where α is generically a function of measured microscopic properties of the system, eg velocities. Dissipation in granular media ( 80 ) or mechanical self-propulsion in flocking media ( 81 ) can also introduce such compressible phase space flows. In particular, the alignment rules in flocking can be interpreted as the result of information processing and decision making at the level of the agents ( 8 , 82–86 ). In usual models of flocking like the Vicsek model, however, it is difficult to keep track of information exchanges and to disentangle the fully informational (entropic) part from the part requiring mechanical work. For the demon gas, the collision operator should describe the evolution of a variable-diameter hard disk gas. Therefore, the collision kernel B takes on the form for hard disk collisions (see Materials and methods and SI Section S5 ), while α is a ratio of collision cross sections before and after the collision. For homogeneous demon gases, with diameter functions of velocity only, α can be shown to have the form (see Materials and methods) (6) α = D ( v ′ ) + D ( v * ′ ) D ( v ) + D ( v * ) . Only treating diameter as a function of single-particle velocities greatly simplifies the problem but requires other inputs to the diameter function (such as the average local velocity considered in the flocking example above) to be treated as constants. This amounts to requiring a separation of timescales, with single-particle velocities assumed to evolve much faster than the average of their local neighborhood. In the SI ( Sections S5 and S6 ), we show that the steady-state distribution function can be written as f d = ( 1 + D ) f MB as a function of the MB distribution f MB and a function D ( v ) that preserves the conservation of particle number, linear momentum, and kinetic energy. The nonequilibrium distribution f d allows entropy to be directly calculated in the small-density limit, and this quantity bounds how much work the excited demon gas state could perform on an external system as it relaxes to an equilibrium state ( 72 ). Comparison between kinetic theory and simulation demonstrates strong agreement in the limit of small diameter changes and densities. Hydrodynamic theory of informational flocking We now derive a hydrodynamic description of the flocking demon gas (with diameter function described in Fig. 1 d–f) using a single relaxation time approximation ( 89 ) adapted to the demon gas. In addition to the standard Navier–Stokes equations manifesting the conservation of mass, linear momentum, and energy (which are left unaffected), we find that the traceless part Q i j = P i j − ( P k k / 2 ) δ i j of the pressure tensor P i j evolves as (8) D Q i j D t = ∂ k R i j k + 1 τ [ Q 0 Q − 1 ] Q i j in which τ is a relaxation time, Q 2 ≡ ‖ Q ‖ 2 ≡ Q μ ν Q ν μ / 2 ≥ 0 , D / D t is the material derivative, and (9) Q 0 = 4 P 0 π Δ D Δ D + 2 D S , where P 0 = ρ k B T and R i j k is a higher-order current (see SI Section S7 for details). In the homogeneous case, Eq. 8 implies that the order parameter Q quantifying the nematic flocking evolves as (10) ∂ t Q = 1 τ [ Q 0 − Q ] while the direction of the principal axes of the symmetric tensor Q (corresponding to the nematic flocking direction) stays fixed. b In the language of dynamical system theory, this describes a nonanalytic version ( 90 ) of a circle-pitchfork bifurcation ( 91 ) occurring at Q 0 = 0 . When Q 0 &gt; 0 , there is a circle of stable solutions Q with Q = Q 0 and an arbitrary orientation ψ . c The steady-state solution of Eq. 10 is shown in Fig. 2 l and compared with numerical data. Simple arguments based on coupling between nearby regions in the spatially extended gas, corresponding to adding diffusive terms in Eq. 8 by linear response (eg R μ ν ρ ∝ ∂ ρ Q μ ν ) , predict alignment of ψ in the absence of fluctuations. Measurement-induced flocking transition Going back to information thermodynamics, the steady-state magnitude of the order parameter Q (ie Q 0 ) is bounded by the quantity of information measured. Indeed, the work W ( Q 0 ) that the gas can exert on another system during relaxation to the MB distribution is bounded by Δ F * ( 72 ), which in turn is bounded by k B T I . For the case of the flocking diameter function and to lowest order in activity ( Δ D ), we find that Δ F * = 1 2 k B T ( π Q 0 4 P 0 ) 2 . Hence, we get (11) ( Q 0 P 0 ) 2 ≤ 32 π 2 I . In other words, the magnitude Q 0 of the informational flocking order parameter is bounded by the information I accumulated through continuous measurements. This suggests viewing the transition to informational flocking as a classical measurement-induced phase transition ( 57–61 ). In quantum MIPTs ( 62–64 ), local projective measurements may counteract the scrambling of information originating from the underlying unitary dynamics. Here, the combination of local measurements and control actions may counteract the scrambling of information originating from the underlying chaotic dynamics (eg collisions in the gas). Classically, the act of measurement itself does not affect the system d so, as in Refs. ( 59–61 ), control actions are performed that modify the state of the system according to the outcome of the measurements. In contrast with these works, here microscopic control actions are not necessarily designed to mimic quantum measurements, but include bioinspired behavioral decisions. Exploiting nonthermal noise At scales beyond several μ m, thermal noise will be too weak to agitate information-processing agents, while nonthermal noise sources (turbulence, vibration) are common. Here, we use a pattern formation task to demonstrate that nonthermal noise can be used to produce low-entropy collective macrostates even in complex fluctuating environments. We consider two populations of particles (blue and red) that tend to move in opposite directions ± ξ ( x ) set by a fixed field ξ ( x ) . The linear momentum conservation of our model does not preclude the formation of density patterns in single-species gases with diameter functions of position; Fig. 4 demonstrates the formation of a density gradient for a demon gas with a choice function which depends on particle location and velocity. However, momentum exchange between two species greatly enhances informational-self propulsion. In Fig. 6 b, c, we compare patterns formed by continuously agitated variable-diameter demon particles and fixed-diameter particles with the ability to self-propel. In both cases, particles are aware of ξ , however in the case of propelled particles it is used to set the propulsion direction, equivalent to an externally applied potential ∇ U ( x ) = − f w ξ . The magnitude of their propulsive force, f w , controls how far this system departs from equilibrium, whereas Δ D plays an analogous role for demon particles. Self-propelled particles demix (Fig. 6 b) into the desired pattern. However as nonthermal agitation is increased, the patterns become less and less visible because of the noise (expressible as an effective temperature T eff ). In contrast, the resolution of patterns formed by demon particles experiencing the same agitation (and following ξ ) appears to improve with additional noise (Fig. 6 c). In fact, the infinite-time resolution of demon particles is entirely independent of the level of noise, while the time required to obtain a given resolution decreases with effective temperature, ∝ 1 / T eff . The finite-time resolution is set by propulsion speed. Returning to a thermal system in which a demon gas is immersed in a fixed-diameter isothermal gas, kinetic theory (Fig. 6 d-e) accurately predicts that the mean flow of the informational gas takes the form (13) u i = − m 2 k b T ∫ v i | v | 2 f d d v = a ( ρ , Δ D ) 2 k b T / m . The demon gas effectively converts a temperature-independent fraction ( a ( ρ , Δ D ) ) of the mean thermal kinetic energy into directed motion. The noise-independence of patterns at long times arises due to the balancing of two effects which scale identically with noise. Returning again to a thermal system of demon gas particles, but now considering a diameter function of both velocity and location (Fig. 6 f), we observe density gradients resulting from particle measurements. An empirical effective potential, the so-called potential of mean force, U pmf ( x ) = − k b T ln ρ ( x ) / ρ 0 , can be computed to compare the system to one bound by an energetic potential ( 93 ). In Fig. 6 g, the effective potential depth ( Δ U pmf ) is found to scale linearly with temperature, entirely compensating the effect of noise. Similar effects occur in the nonthermal environment of Fig. 6 c, and consequently increasing k b T eff improves the short-time resolution of pattern-forming demon gas particles, while having no effect on their eventual steady state. Meanwhile, pattern formation under a temperature-independent driving force (eg self-propulsion as in Fig. 6 b) only degrades with additional noise. Conclusion Our results shed light on collective processes which exploit measurement and noise to produce nonequilibrium steady states in many-ratchet systems. Such processes are an important component of realistic systems where information processing and mechanical activity (eg self-propulsion) coexist. Both types of activity are engines (ie intermediaries between a nonequilibrated reservoir and a sink that generates work), however informational engines use their reservoir (ie memory, prepared by dissipative erasure) to selectively couple to the momentarily “hot” fluctuating environmental degrees of freedom. The conversion of noise into motive power requires control actions informed by measurements. In practice, these feedback mechanisms will always convert energy stored in some fuel, thus blurring the theoretical distinction between informational and mechanical activity. However at a fundamental level, measurements and the control actions they inform can be performed with arbitrarily small energy expenditure (zero, in the frictionless limit) as in the celebrated Feynman–Smoluchowski single-ratchet example ( 94 ). The only energy cost that is strictly required is the k B T log 2 per bit associated with erasing the memory registry, as stipulated by Landauer’s erasure principle ( 66 ). Saturating Landauer’s bound may be difficult in practice, however a key aspect of informational driving is that work output scales with the magnitude of environmental fluctuations, which we show can be exploited even for nonthermal reservoirs in which fluctuations can be very large. Hence, the practical difference between these two flavors of nonequilibrium driving resembles the distinction between electrical rectifiers and batteries. Informational driving, like a rectifier, produces bias from a fluctuating bias-free input, while a battery (like conventional self-propulsion) directly converts a chemical fuel into a bias. The core concepts of informational activity investigated here by kinetic theory in classical many-ratchet thermal systems and extended to nonthermal systems with reinforcement learning, may also apply to animal, bacterial, and micro-robotic swarms that collectively seek to harness environmental noise to power goal-directed behaviors, and in which measurement-induced phase transitions similar to the measurement-induced flocking described in this work may also occur. This perspective may also be fruitful in the context of recently proposed active quantum systems ( 95–98 ). Kinetic theory of variable diameter hard disks Conservation of momentum and energy determine the velocities of a pair of hard disks after collision. Assuming uniform particle masses, (18) v + v * = v ′ + v * ′ (19) | v | 2 + | v * | 2 = | v ′ | 2 + | v * ′ | 2 . If n ^ is a unit vector that points between disk centers at the point of contact (note that n ^ therefore depends upon diameter), then the postcollision velocities can be written, (20) v ′ = v − ( ( v − v * ) ⋅ n ^ ) n ^ (21) v * ′ = v * + ( ( v − v * ) ⋅ n ^ ) n ^ . For disks of fixed diameter, this scattering process is microreversible; ( v , v * ) maps to ( v ′ , v * ′ ) and ( − v ′ , − v * ′ ) maps to ( − v , − v * ) , resulting in a Jacobian for the change of variables equal to − 1 ( 77 ). This one-to-one mapping allows all four velocity terms to be collected under a common unit disk integration in the typical quadratic Boltzmann collision operator (see SI Section S5 for additional details). When particle diameters are instead functions of velocity, the microreversibility of the collision process is broken but can be restored with a shift of relative positions. In a coordinate frame centered on the point of contact for the forward collision, a shift in position of α = ( D ( v ′ ) + D ( v * ′ ) ) / ( D ( v ) + D ( v * ) ) along the reflection plane of the forward collision, applied to one particle during the reverse process restores the symmetry of velocity mapping (see SI Fig. S5 ). Note that when demon gas particles are immersed in a fixed-diameter gas, one set of diameters (starred or unstarred) is constant.
