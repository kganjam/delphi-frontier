Introduction Denticle-like structures were present on the skin of the extinct, jawless vertebrate genus Astraspis as early as the mid Ordovician, 450 million years ago 1 . Subsequent studies have shown that teeth and dermal denticles are homologous organs, both having odontode-isolated structures consisting of a dentine cone covered by highly mineralized enameloid with a bony base as an attachment 2 , 3 . Because dermal denticles are considered to be the ancestral organs of teeth, their morphology has attracted considerable research attention from multiple disciplines 4 . However, among extant elasmobranchs, only sharks and some batoids are entirely covered by dermal denticles. Sharks are one of the oldest groups of living vertebrates and many are apex predators in the marine realm, today. They can be found in the deep sea (down to 4000 m), open ocean, and the continental shelves and slopes, thus the impact of the dermal denticles on how sharks adapt to various environments has been of great interest to biologists. Today, the functions of some dermal denticle types are well known, such as how ridged denticles of some fast swimming sharks interact with the turbulent boundary layer to reduce the surface drag 5 – 7 . Similarly, this ridged design is successful for antifouling properties 8 . Moreover, recent research is now focusing on how denticles of deep-water sharks are functionally involved in bioluminescence 9 . However, a more general understanding of the functions and properties of the various shark dermal denticles is still uncertain. We also lack comprehensive studies to understand how different denticle morphologies have influenced the adaptation of sharks to various environments throughout their evolutionary history. More importantly, no study has thoroughly investigated the tempo and mode of shark dermal denticle evolution from a macroevolutionary perspective. For instance, how ecological opportunities, such as those created by plate tectonics, i.e., the opening of novel oceans, have influenced the evolution of shark denticle diversity. These important yet understudied questions are likely due to the difficulty in quantifying and classifying the vast morphological disparity of dermal denticles. This study introduces a methodological approach to examine and quantify the morphology-function relationship of dermal denticles. We establish quantifiable parameters describing each denticle morphogroup, and subject these to analyses on a macroevolutionary scale. We use a deep learning approach of artificial intelligence to group, quantify and classify the images of shark dermal denticles. This study defines ten dermal denticle morphogroups across 132 extant shark species. The principle is similar to how humans can learn new things with a small sample size by observing, comparing, and summarizing, with the support of prior knowledge, and without prior annotation of the data. The process of subjectively defining important features is known as feature engineering in the field of artificial intelligence, and an important goal of representation learning is to reduce the need for feature engineering relying on human labours and decisions 10 . Specifically, representation learning is a machine learning approach that aims to automatically discover the most effective way to represent data prior to recognizing differences between pre-assigned data categories. We apply a deep learning approach to quantify denticle morphology, and combined with a recently published phylogenetic tree we can study the evolutionary tempo and mode of these different denticle morphogroups 11 . In addition, we designed a range of mechanical, elemental, and fluid dynamic experiments and simulations that provide insights into the mechanisms behind abrasion strength, defence, and drag reduction functions. Here, we find that the internal properties add to the overall functional capacity of dermal denticles. Among the carpet sharks, which are primarily demersal and reef-associated sharks, we find dermal denticles with low damage resistance—a result challenging previous hypotheses suggesting that reef-associated and benthic sharks carry denticles with high damage resistance to prevent abrasions. We perform a functional trait analysis to provide an integrated understanding of the possible functional trade-offs in different types of denticles. For instance, denticles with low hardness had a higher mineral protection, in terms of a higher fluorine content, to potentially compensate for damage repair when broken. These results show the complexity of the functional properties of dermal denticles. Finally, we conduct a correlation analysis of trait and habitat evolution to understand whether a relationship exists between ecological niche and denticle morphogroup. Here we can conclude a correlation between denticle type, ridged vs. non-ridged, and the ecological niches of the sharks. Results Deep learning constructs the morpho-space of dermal denticles for delineating morphogroups We wanted to categorize dermal denticles based on key morphological features, and for this we used the Variational Sparse Coding model (VSC), a branch of deep learning autoencoders to quantify the visual features of sharks’ dermal denticles 12 , 13 . Prior to morphogroup analysis, we carried out broad taxonomic sampling in order to represent the most morphological variation of dermal denticles in the dataset. We used 2193 SEM images of skin to train the model, representing 204 individual sharks, sampled on 53 different body locations across 132 extant shark species, 2 ray species, and 24 fossil species. The fossil samples are not among the final groupings, as they clustered together as their own group. Excluding them did not affect the current result (Supplementary Fig. 1 ). Each SEM image represents a single body location from a single species and should be regarded as a unique entity, which means that skin samples across the body of the same species may fall into several morphogroups. We only included sexually mature specimens, partly because denticle morphology varies with ontogeny, but also to attempt a common ground for sampling 14 (see ‘Sample collection’ in the Methods section for more detail, Supplementary Fig. 2 ). This VSC model extracted sparse and essential visual features of dermal denticles from the standardized SEM images without human labelling, capable of accounting for denticle density, i.e., the spacing between denticles, overlap, size, and external morphology, and represented the input images with feature vectors in a 512-dimensional morpho-space. The embeddings of all the input images were plotted in 2-D coordinates reduced from the 512-D morpho-space with the UMAP algorithm ( https://umap-learn.readthedocs.io/en/latest/ ) for visualization only (Fig. 1a ). The UMAP in Fig. 1a thus shows a fragment of dimensions when illustrating the placement of morphogroups in the morpho-space. To control the variations of visual features of each group, we used the K-means clustering algorithm to divide the data points embedded in the 512-D morpho-space into K = 20 morphogroups. Further, we excluded any morphogroups represented by fewer than three F1-body location skin samples (the flank region above the pectoral fins), omitting eight morphogroups, for the purpose of simplifying the representations and for using this particular location to compare denticle morphology of different species in the ancestral state reconstruction (Figs. 2 and 3 ). We refer to the ‘Methods’ section for an in-depth account of the grouping of dermal denticles, and for more information on the F1-body location we refer to the next paragraph. Additionally, we merged four morphogroups into two due to their close proximity in the morpho-space minimizing the number of morphogroups to 10 without affecting the group formations (Fig. 1a, b , Supplementary Figs. 3 and 4 ). We compared the grouping patterns of different K with the taxonomically defined clades of a phylogenetic tree of sharks 11 , and found that these groupings were similar. The features of each morphogroup were expressed as an average morphology, which we derived from the mean feature values at the centroid of each morphogroup in the 512-dimension morpho-space (see detailed examples in Supplementary Fig. 3 ). We show these as 3D reconstructed morphogroup models (Fig. 1b , view rotating 3D models on Figshare: doi.org/10.6084/m9.figshare.21229121). The resulting morphogroups show that seven of the 10 groups include denticles with a variety of ridges on the crown (grps 1–6, 8), each identified with different ridge-characteristics. The remaining three groups include denticles that are smooth (grp 7), i.e., without prominent ridges, spiky (grp 9) or highly structured (grp 10). The latter referring to denticles with elaborate structures neither fitting in the groups of ridged or spiky denticles (see Supplementary Fig. 3 for morphological variation within the 10 morphogroups). Ridged dermal denticles are prevalent in the most speciose clade To understand the tempo and mode of the evolution of shark dermal denticles in macroscopic terms, we mapped and analysed the morphology using a recently published phylogenetic tree of shark relatives 11 . This fossil-calibrated phylogeny represents the most taxonomically comprehensive to date, however, we acknowledge that other phylogenies may present alternative hypotheses. The geological dating of fossil specimens and thus the geological timeline referred to in this section is based on the phylogeny by Stein et al. (2018). It has been well established that variation in denticle morphology across a single individual can be enormous 15 , 16 . Dermal denticles on different body regions, i.e., the snout, the flank region above the pectoral fin (F1-body location), the caudal fin tip, etc., can vary as much as denticles between species. In this study, we observed that the denticles of the F1 region were the most variable and thus a good indicator for interspecific variation. Additionally, the flank region above the pectoral fins (F1-body location) is the most commonly sampled in literature for understanding the relationship between dermal denticles and shark lifestyle 15 , 17 – 20 (see Fig. 1b and Supplementary Fig. 2 for position of the F1-body location). For many studies, it may have been the sheer proportional size of the F1-body location that made it ideal for sampling skin, in which a larger body area with similar denticle morphology may indicate a representative morphology for the sharks’ lifestyle. We thus focused on the F1-body location when examining the ancestral states, mapping the ten morphogroups for 73 extant shark species representing seven orders and 23 families, and when calculating the diversification rates of different clades (Figs. 2 and 4 ). We found all ten morphogroups present at the nodes of the most recent common ancestors (MRCA) to the two suborders, Squalomorphii and Galeomorphii, over 250 million years ago (MYA) (Figs. 2 and 3 ). Because all morphogroups are present, it is not possible to determine the ancestral state to the two suborders. The Squalomorph sharks diversified carrying mostly non-ridged denticles (grps 9–10). However, the Galeomorph sharks diversified with a different set of denticles, mainly the ridged and smooth morphogroups (grps 1–8). Two-thirds of the dogfishes (Squaliformes) display the spiky and highly structured denticles at the tips (grps 9–10). The lantern sharks (Etmopteridae) solely display spiky denticles, and the gulper sharks (Centrophoridae) mostly display highly structured denticles. Squalomorph sharks are mainly found in cold, deeper waters, with a variation of pelagic-oceanic, bathydemersal and benthopelagic lifestyles 21 , 22 , however, some have diverged to shelf-related habitats and developed novel lifestyles reflecting these ecological niches. Examples include the dwarf sawshark Pristiophorus nancyae preferring the open ocean and continental slopes, the bluntnose sixgill shark Hexanchus griseus and the spiny dogfish Squalus acanthias , both prevalent on continental and insular shelves and upper slopes, the former being highly migratory (Figs. 2 and 3 ) 21 , 22 . The bullhead sharks (Heterodontiformes), a group of small, benthic dwellers, display a mix of highly structured (grp 10), thick ridge (grp 5) and spiky denticles (grp 9). Around 235 MYA, the Galeomorphii diverged into two major clades, the carpet sharks, Orectolobiformes, and the clade comprising the ground sharks and mackerel sharks, Carcharhiniformes and Lamniformes (Figs. 2 and 3 ). The carpet sharks, mostly exhibiting a reef-associated and benthic lifestyle 23 , 24 , display a variety of ridged and non-ridged denticles including round ridge, robust ridge, thick ridge, catshark ridge, and smooth denticles (grps 3–7). There is a 33% probability that the MRCA to Hemiscyllidae and the group comprising the zebra shark Stegostoma tigrinum and the nurse sharks Pseudoginglymostoma brevicaudatum, Ginglymostoma cirratum , and Nebrius ferrugineus , displayed smooth dermal denticles (grp 7), whereas the MRCA to the blind sharks, Brachaelurus waddi and B. colcloughi , has a high probability of catshark ridge denticles (grp 6 with 48%). The highly migratory mackerel sharks (Lamniformes), including the shortfin mako shark Isurus oxyrinchus , exhibit a mix of ridged denticles, including classic ridge I (grp 1), round ridge (grp 3), and robust ridge denticles (grp 4). The coastal-pelagic ground sharks (Carcharhiniformes) are broadly represented by all seven groups of ridged denticles, with a single representation of highly structured denticles (grp 10) in Scyliorhinus meadi . Finally, within the ground sharks, between 110–80 MYA, the ghost catsharks Apristurus and the catsharks diversified (Figs. 2 and 3 ). These catsharks diversified in the deep sea, diverging from the prevailing, shallower habitats of the clade 25 , carrying irregular ridge (grp 8) and catshark ridge denticles (grp 6), respectively (Figs. 2 and 3 ). The MRCA to the ground sharks has a 37% probability of classic ridge I denticles (grp 1) and 44% probability of catshark ridge (Figs. 2 and 3 ). Relationship between habitat, dermal denticle morphology, and diversification rates Throughout the evolutionary history of sharks, we observed an increased diversification rate approximately 170 MYA in the ground sharks, Carcharhiniformes (Figs. 2 and 4 ), resulting in high taxonomic diversity accounting for 43 of the 50 genera. However, the diversification rates of two other main shark clades—the reef-dwelling carpet sharks, Orectolobiformes, and the deep-water clade, Squaliformes—decreased over time (Fig. 4 ). We further compared the diversification rates of ridged and non-ridged dermal denticles using the binary state speciation and extinction model (BiSSE) 26 implemented in the R package diversitree 27 . We found that clades with ridged dermal denticles generally have a higher diversification rate compared with the non-ridged denticles, although with no significant difference ( P = 0.3026, Supplementary Fig. 5D ). We further examined whether denticle ridges were related to specific ecological niches. To understand the relationship between shark dermal denticles and ecological niches, we conducted a correlation analysis of trait and habitat evolution. Our results show that when analysing all clades together, dermal denticles of sharks in shallow water environments, i.e., species carrying out most of their life in the photic zone above 200 m depth, tend to evolve from non-ridged (grps 7, 9, 10) to ridged ( P &lt; 0.05, grps 1–6, 8, Supplementary Fig. 5A ). However, in deep-sea environments, i.e., species living below 200 m depth, there is no significant evolutionary direction between non-ridged and ridged denticles (Supplementary Fig. 5A ). We also found a higher transition rate from a reef to a non-reef environment for sharks carrying ridged denticles ( P &lt; 0.05, Supplementary Fig. 5B, C ). Our results suggest a strong correlation between denticle type, ridged vs. non-ridged, and the ecological niches of the sharks. While we acknowledge that species within a morphogroup may exhibit differences in material properties despite similarities in external morphology, we believe that our sample size provides valuable insights into the form function relationship (see Supplementary Table 1 for further sampling details). Our sample definition is described as the species plus the body location, i.e., blackbelly lanternshark snout (S) or shortfin mako shark belly (B), and each sample provides numerous data points both for nanoindentation (NI) and for electron probe microanalysis (EPMA). For internal properties, we used NI to measure the enameloid hardness, which is the material’s resistance to localized plastic deformation (H, 137 indents per morphogroup on average), reduced elastic modulus (E r , 137 data points per morphogroup on average), i.e., the material’s ability to withstand elastic deformation when subjected to an applied force, here referred to as the stiffness, and we calculated the H/E r , also known as an indicator of the damage resistance 28 (Fig. 5a , Supplementary Note 1 ). With EPMA we confirm the biomineral of the enameloid as fluorapatite (Ca 5 (PO 4 ) 3 F) from the Ca/P ratio and F wt% (Fig. 5a, b , Supplementary Note 2 ). Additionally, we use the F wt% as an indicator of mineral protection, not to be confused with mineral content (89 data points per morphogroup on average) 29 , 30 . As fluorapatite is commonly known for its protective properties against tooth caries, also known as demineralization, we will use these terms in later sections 31 . We tested for statistical significance of internal properties among the morphogroups using ANOVA (Fig. 5a , see ‘Methods’ for details). Regarding external properties, we further scanned the samples with micro-CT and recreated 3D models of representative morphogroups to analyse their capacity for drag reduction with computational fluid dynamics. We used one representative sample per morphogroup with the exception of spiky and highly structured where two samples were necessary to cover the morphogroups’ morphology (Fig. 5c , Supplementary Figs. 7 and 8 , Supplementary Note 3 , see specific species used in Supplementary Table 1 ). We then used functional trait analysis to integrate the mechanical properties, mineral composition, and fluid dynamic properties of the various denticle morphogroups (see ‘Methods’ section for further detail, Fig. 6 ). The functional trait analysis is essentially a PCA (principal component analysis), which is able to minimize the dimensions of the projected trait data, and test the significance of each dimension in order to illustrate differences and correlations between the traits 32 . We transformed the functional data from Fig. 5a into performance ranks between 0–10, with 0 indicating a low value, in order to standardize the various properties and facilitate comparison across different metrics (Fig. 5d ). This ranking system serves as a method for organizing and comparing relative performance and does not inherently address statistical significance. The Squalomorph sharks mainly inhabiting deep water predominantly carry spiky (grp 9) or highly structured denticles (grp 10). Spiky denticles present a long and slender morphology with a high mineral protection (rank 10) and damage resistance (rank 8.2) (Figs. 5a, d and 6 , Supplementary Fig. 8H–K ). These properties are indicative of a suitable armour of protection, difficult to approach unharmed, and protected against demineralization if damaged. Highly structured denticles are identified by their variable morphologies, including low, irregular-shaped knobs and starshaped, spiky protrusions, and have a high stiffness (rank 7.6) and hardness (rank 8.6) and intermediate levels of damage resistance (rank 6.2), protection (rank 4.8) and drag reduction (rank 5.3) (Figs. 5a, d and 6 , Supplementary Fig. 8 ). Highly structured denticles present versatility in their functional profile, but predominantly indicate a strong and stiff armour able to protect and reduce demineralization if damaged. We also found that smaller, deep-water sharks, vulnerable to predators in both open ocean and shelf-related habitats, e.g., the lantern sharks Etmopteridae, the catsharks Scyliorhinidae, and the dogfishes Squalidae, exhibit catshark ridge, irregular ridge, spiky, or highly structured denticles (grp 6, 8–10). The catshark ridge denticles express high drag reduction (rank 9.9) and intermediate levels of stiffness (rank 6.6), whereas the irregular ridge denticles are more versatile with high hardness (rank 10), stiffness (rank 9.2) and drag reduction (rank 9.4), and intermediate levels of damage resistance (rank 6.7) (Fig. 5d ). We found that deep-water sharks predominantly carry denticles suitable for protection against predators expressed as a combination of their internal properties and external morphologies. However, sharks carrying the catshark denticles appear to be better equipped for improved swimming performance than protective functions (Figs. 5a, d and 6 ). In addition, we found that the carpet sharks, primarily reef-associated and demersal sharks, predominantly exhibit smooth (grp 7), robust ridge (grp 4), and thick ridge denticles (grp 5). Smooth (grp 7) and robust ridge denticles (grp 4) have similar functional profiles with high drag reduction (rank 10, 9.9), protection (rank 7.3, 8.2), and stiffness (rank 7.3, 5.2), but with low damage resistance (rank 0, 0.01) and low hardness (rank 2.5, 0) (Figs. 5a, d and 6 ). Thick ridge denticles (grp 5) have high stiffness (rank 10) and intermediate levels of hardness (rank 4.9) and drag reduction (rank 6.3), but low protection (rank 1.8) and damage resistance (rank 0.3) (Figs. 5a, d and 6 ). In all three morphogroups, the profiles indicate that morphology plays a greater role than internal properties for the function of abrasion strength. However, robust ridge and smooth denticles have an extra layer of protection, i.e., the high fluorine content, to reduce demineralization if damaged. Our results show that the Ca/P ratios of denticle morphogroups range from 1.89 to 2.0, which are close to the ideal stoichiometry of the apatite structure (2.13), and hardness ranges from 4.2 to 5.9 GPa in these morphogroups. Comparatively, shortfin mako shark teeth, with a near-perfect stoichiometric Ca/P ratio (2.14), exhibit the highest hardness values (6.2 to 7.5 GPa), while Port Jackson shark Heterodontus portusjacksoni teeth with the lowest Ca/ P values (1.77–1.82) show the lowest hardness (3.7–4.6 GPa) 33 , 34 , indicating that the hardness of teeth enameloid is directly related to the Ca/P ratio (Supplementary Table 2 ). Furthermore, our samples indicate substantial fluorine content, ranging from approximately 1.82 wt% to 4.42 wt%, suggesting sufficient fluoride ions for the formation of fluorapatite compounds, which may provide an extra layer of protection against demineralization in denticles more prone to damage, as evidenced by the negative correlation between hardness and mineral protection in our functional trait analysis (Fig. 6a, d , see also Supplementary Note 1 for additional discussion). As mentioned above, the coastal-pelagic sharks are mainly composed of ground and mackerel sharks. We found the classic ridge denticles (grp 1) to be highly prevalent among these pelagic-oceanic and highly migratory species. Classic ridge denticles express high damage resistance (rank 10), protection (rank 8.4), and drag reduction (rank 9.8) (Fig. 5a, d ). Referring to previous studies, a ridged denticle crown is highly efficient in reducing skin friction by controlling the boundary layer 35 , 36 . Lastly, we found that round ridge denticles (grp 3) were dispersed across the phylogeny on species exhibiting various lifestyles, such as the highly active bigeye thresher shark Alopias superciliosus , the deep-water little sleeper shark Somniosus rostratus , and the bottom-dwelling spotted wobbegong Orectolobus maculatus . These denticles have high drag reduction (rank 9.8), and intermediate to low levels in all other categories (rank 1.7–4.6) (Fig. 5a, d ). Thus, the round ridge denticles reflect a more versatile functional profile, which concurs with their broad taxonomic distribution. Discussion By utilizing deep learning methods, we classify the complex dermal denticles of sharks into ten major groups based on morphological similarities. We then conducted a series of mechanical, elemental, and fluid dynamics experiments to investigate the characteristics of these different denticle morphologies. Interestingly, we found that sharks inhabiting different environments tend to exhibit distinct denticle shapes: denticles with ridges primarily occur in shallow-water sharks, which generate minimal drag during swimming, thereby facilitating optimized movement. In contrast, irregular-shaped and spiky denticles are mainly found in deep-sea sharks, providing stronger protection and damage resistance, but at the cost of increased hydrodynamic drag. Moreover, smooth-shaped denticles predominantly appear in reef-dwelling sharks, which produce minimal drag and offer substantial protection, albeit with poor damage resistance. These findings suggest that the diverse denticle morphologies contribute to the adaptation of sharks to varying environments. Our results follow the framework by Reif (1982) relating form and ecological function of shark dermal denticles (see Supplementary Note 4 , Supplementary Fig. 9 ). Consequently, we provide a detailed account of the relationships between the denticle morphology, function, and ecological niche of different shark clades in the following sections. The mackerel sharks are mostly composed of highly migratory species of pelagic-oceanic distribution, whereas the ground sharks, the most species-rich order of sharks, include an array of lifestyles and habitats, from benthic and reef-associated to open water migrators 23 . Together, these two clades display all seven morphogroups of ridged denticles (grps 1–6, 8). Animals moving through water have to deal with different kinds of drag. The first to mention is ‘skin friction’ caused by the boundary layer formation, in which many studies have found that ridges on the denticle crown help control this boundary layer 35 , 36 . The presence of ridged denticles among the successful ground sharks and highly active mackerel sharks indicates the importance of boundary-layer control, and the associated optimized energy consumption, during swimming. Further, a stiff structure creates less turbulence compared to a soft structure 37 , however, we found stiffness to be independent of drag reduction in shark denticles (Fig. 6a ). Lastly, although we measured the highest drag reduction for the smooth denticles (grp 7) (Figs. 5d and 6d ), most studies reach the conclusion that specific spacing and depth of denticle ridges affect the ability to interact with the boundary layer and thus provide control while moving through water at different speeds and angles, being more efficient for the shark 5 , 38 – 40 . Among the carpet sharks, which are primarily demersal and reef-associated sharks 23 , we found dermal denticles with low damage resistance and hardness (grps 4, 5, 7). This result disagrees with previous hypotheses suggesting that reef-associated and benthic sharks carry denticles with high damage resistance to prevent abrasions 2 , 41 , 42 . In all three morphogroups, the functional profiles indicate other kinds of adaptations to living on a reef. Robust ridge and smooth denticles have a high fluorine content, to possibly reduce demineralization. This concurs with our functional trait analysis, where we found hardness and mineral protection to be negatively correlated, indicating that denticles more prone to damage have a secondary defence, i.e., high fluorine content (Fig. 6a, d ). Additionally, it is possible that external morphology plays a greater role than internal properties for the function of abrasion strength, with carpet sharks displaying thickened and robust structures. The Squalomorph sharks predominantly evolved in deep water 24 . Among these clades, we found the catshark ridge (grp 6), irregular ridge (8), spiky (grp 9), and highly structured denticles (grp 10), dominated by the latter two (Figs. 2 and 3 ). The deep sea is a relatively uniform ecosystem, where depth defines the ecological boundaries, and the ability to navigate the aphotic zone is essential 43 – 45 . Thus, accommodating photophores on the skin for communication, counter-illumination and camouflage is useful, whereas carrying an armour of hard, protruding denticles for protection against predators can be of vital importance 2 , 46 – 49 . Highly structured and spiky denticles are both associated with bioluminescence, however, for the former, photophores are attached to a thin layer of epidermis on top of the layer of denticles, whereas the latter accommodate photophores in between their elongated shapes to avoid overshadowing the emitted light 46 , 49 . Although the catshark ridge denticles are associated with a deep-water clade (the catsharks; Scyliorhinidae), their high drag reduction indicates that these denticles are better equipped for swimming performance than protective functions. This is further supported by the negative correlation between drag reduction and damage resistance in Fig. 6a . In spite of the different morphologies of morphogroups 6, 8–10, they all indicate a defensive function, whether it being cusps, spikes, or knob-shapes. The dense arrangement of the large, knob-shaped, highly structured denticles have high mineral protection and damage resistance, indicative of a suitable armour protected from demineralization. Spiky denticles exhibit similar morphological strategies and a versatile functional profile indicative of an unapproachable armour, with high stiffness and hardness. Lastly, the irregular ridge denticles exhibit a general functional profile with high drag reduction, hardness, and stiffness, with a morphology of protrusive, spiky structures fitting a protective armour. These denticles can be found on smaller, deep-water species, such as the longfin catshark, the cookie-cutter shark Isistius brasiliensis , and the sailfin roughshark Oxynotus paradoxus , all exposed to larger predators or dangerous prey and thus in need of protective denticles 23 , 50 , 51 . Furthermore, by examining the relationship between the phylogenetic tree and morphological evolution of shark dermal denticles, our study indicates that a rapid species diversification occurred around 170 million years ago (MYA), significantly increasing the taxonomic diversity of the Carcharhiniformes (Fig. 2 ). The species within this diversified clade primarily inhabit shallow waters, are associated with a non-reef environment, and they all carry ridged denticles (Fig. 3 ). We observed all seven ridged morphogroups (grps 1–6, 8) within this clade (Figs. 2 and 3 ). Our correlation analysis of traits and ecological niches also revealed that in shallow-water and non-reef-associated environments, shark denticles are more likely to evolve from non-ridged to ridged forms (Supplementary Fig. 5A–C ). This period around 170 MYA coincides with the breakup of Pangaea and the gradual formation of the Atlantic Ocean. These tectonic events created numerous new shallow-water continental shelf habitats in the marine realm 52 – 55 , providing ecological opportunities. These significant marine ecological opportunities, in combination with existing ridged denticle morphogroups may have been the primary driving force behind the rapid diversification of the Carcharhiniformes, around 170 MYA (Fig. 2 ). The dramatic changes in the marine environment caused by plate tectonics during this time also led to a substantial increase in the taxonomic diversity of corals 56 , 57 and benthic animal groups 58 . The ridged denticles of the Carcharhiniformes are generally optimized for drag reduction (Fig. 6a, d ), among other related functions 59 , and may have provided an advantage for the sharks to explore shallow habitats (Supplementary Fig. 5 A, B). However, the evolution of morphological diversity remains poorly understood. Thus, our study highlights the significant impact of the ecological opportunities created by the breakup of Pangaea and the formation of the Atlantic Ocean on the macroevolution of marine organisms’ morphology and taxonomic diversity, but clearly, more research is needed to fully comprehend these relationships. In addition, we discovered that the main shark denticle morphologies may have already appeared early in the evolutionary history of sharks. According to a recently published study, shark diversity may have been reduced by &gt;70% during the early Miocene epoch (around 19 MYA), greatly diminishing the diversity of denticle shapes seen today 60 . Although ridged denticles are dominant throughout the ground sharks (Galeomorphii suborder), we found many examples of ridged denticles in the older, Squalomorph lineages, e.g., the dwarf sawshark (classic ridge II) and the bluntnose sixgill shark (robust ridge), indicating that ridges may have been present at the common ancestor to the two suborders, or that ridges evolved several times. It was not until millions of years later, when plate tectonics and the emergence of the Atlantic Ocean created a wealth of ecological opportunities of shelf environments, that clades with ridge-shaped denticles adapted for swimming experienced a taxonomic diversity burst. Our results demonstrate that the evolution of shark dermal denticles is a complex process of successful innovation in the marine environment, likely explained by a combination of early trait diversification and long-term delayed ecological opportunities. These findings support recent theories emphasizing the distinction between morphological novelty and innovation in the evolutionary success of a lineage 61 , 62 . Ultimately, our study showcases how integrating deep learning, materials science, and evolutionary analysis provides a comprehensive understanding of how an ancient group, such as sharks, has managed to maintain its dominant position in marine ecosystems. To assess whether body length and sex influence morphogroup classification, we tested both variables using a series of statistical and machine learning approaches. These analyses included linear (logistic regression) and non-linear (random forest) models, as well as ANOVA and importance score assessments. We began by defining a null model that included only an intercept term and no predictors, thereby assuming that morphogroup assignment is entirely random based on the observed class distribution. This serves as a baseline for evaluating whether body length and sex add explanatory power. The ANOVA revealed significant differences in body length across morphogroups (F(9, 1671) = 33.182, p &lt; 0.001, ω² = 0.147; Supplementary Fig. 6A ), and post hoc Tukey’s HSD tests identified which morphogroups differ (Supplementary Fig. 6B ). However, a random forest model assigned 97.7% importance to body length and just 2.3% to sex, suggesting that sex plays a minimal role and was therefore excluded from further analysis. To quantify the predictive value of body length, we trained classification models using a 70:30 train-test split. The logistic regression model performed poorly, with mean accuracy of 28.5%, F1-score of 0.16, and a McFadden’s pseudo R² of 0.062, indicating only a 6.2% improvement over the null model. Additionally, the inclusion of sex did not improve model fit relative to the model using body length alone and resulted in a negligible decrease in McFadden’s pseudo-R² (Δpseudo-R² = −0.000905). The random forest classifier showed improved performance (accuracy = 54.5%, F1 = 0.55, pseudo R² = 0.142), with a 14.2% log-likelihood gain over the null model. This model is capable of capturing non-linear and high-dimensional interactions, so the limited performance is notable. Although body length was assigned a high importance score of 97.7%, the overall model performance suggests that its contribution might not be as impactful as the importance score implies. This indicates a modest overall gain, suggesting that these two predictors offer limited ability to explain morphogroup structure in this dataset. Similarly, the classification accuracy of 54.5%, while better than chance, is still relatively low—particularly considering that a random forest classifier, which is capable of modelling non-linear and complex relationships, was used. If body length and sex were strong determinants of morphogroup identity, we would expect substantially higher performance. To further evaluate the role of body size in the morphometric structure, we performed Pearson correlation and linear regression between body length and the first two UMAP dimensions (Supplementary Fig. 6D ). UMAP1 exhibited a statistically significant but weak positive correlation with body length (r = 0.088, p = 0.0315; R² = 0.008), while UMAP2 showed a stronger negative correlation (r = –0.194, p &lt; 0.0001; R² = 0.038). Although these correlations are statistically significant, the low R² values indicate that body length explains only a small proportion of the variance along each UMAP axis. These trends suggest a limited influence of body size on the embedded morphometric structure. It is important to note that UMAP is a non-linear, non-parametric dimensionality reduction technique. Its axes do not preserve linear relationships from the original high-dimensional data, and correlations with biological variables should be interpreted cautiously. Combined with our classification results—where body length had a high importance score (97.7%) but yielded modest model performance (random forest accuracy = 54.5%, McFadden’s pseudo R² = 0.142)—these findings suggest that body size may exert some weak structure, but it is unlikely to be a dominant factor shaping the morphogroup clustering. Morpho-space construction with deep learning model We use the Variational Sparse Coding (VSC) autoencoder, which is an improved version of the Variational Autoencoder (VAE) 12 , 13 , 63 . The VSC is a self-supervised model, and it can extract visual features of images by reconstructing the inputs with constricted conditions and revising the model without predetermined labelling. We prefer the VSC because it introduces a stronger regularization term in the loss function, which constrains the latent vectors (the underlying numerical representations that encode the essential features of the input data) to approximate a sparse (spike-and-slab) distribution, rather than a normal distribution. The sparse distribution regularization helps improve dimension independence and prevents excessive divergence while still achieving high similarity in image reconstruction. If the latent vectors become too dispersed, similar data points—such as dermal denticles with similar appearances—might not map to nearby points in the lower-dimensional latent space, where the latent vectors are positioned to capture the core structure of the original feature space formed by the raw features of the input data. This would make it difficult, or even meaningless, to effectively analyse and interpret the latent space. Like all other autoencoders, VSC is mainly composed of two modules: the encoder and the decoder. We based the learning phase of the model on 2193 SEM images (input images), including images produced from this study (1586) and acquired from literature (607). To build a standardized augmentation dataset for model training, these images were resized to the same scale and cropped into 100 samples of 256 × 256 sub-images on random positions for each scaled SEM image. Our implementation of the VSC encoder down-sampled the input images of the dataset with 6 layers of residual blocks for extracting features from and across different scales, and encoded each input image of 256 × 256 into a 512-dimension feature vector 64 . In the learning process, the encoder learns to compress visual features of images of dermal denticles into the vectors of much smaller dimension. The decoder, which is symmetrical to the encoder in terms of network architecture, takes the 512-dimension feature vectors as inputs, and learns to decode them back to reconstruct the 256 × 256 images (output images). Comparing the real and reconstructed images iteratively guides the gradients and directions for updating of model parameters with the back-propagation algorithm, which is done by calculating the pixel-level mean squared error (MSE loss) between the input and output images. The feature vectors are regularized in the training process to the spike-and-slab distribution for the model, which increases the latent space sparsity, i.e., the model extracts less numeric features for representing the whole visual feature, and provides better disentanglement performance where each extracted feature is more independent. The whole process resembles a more complex version of PCA (principal component analysis) with non-linear solution algorithms. Our model was built and trained with Python 3.6, PyTorch 1.7.1 and CUDA 9.2, on two GPUs (NVIDIA GeForce 1080 Ti), for 23,310 epochs. Morphogroup formation We tested different combinations of clustering algorithms and group sizes to divide dermal denticle images into different morphogroups and evaluate their performance. We considered two main principles to evaluate the clustering results of different algorithms. The first is natural clustering, which is a preference for a clustering algorithm that better aligns naturally occurring patterns, such as the clades in a phylogenetic tree. The second is a limited number of variants in each clade, thus controlling for visual differences in dermal denticles within a clade. Based on these two principles, we tested four different clustering methods from two categories: distance-based algorithms (BIRCH and K-means) and density-based algorithms (DBSCAN and OPTICS). All these algorithms are well documented and implemented in the scikit-learn package of Python 65 ( https://scikit-learn.org/stable/modules/classes.html#module-sklearn.cluster61 ). The first method we tested was a distance-based algorithm, BIRCH, combined with hierarchical clustering using Birch and AgglomerativeClustering from the scikit-learn package. Birch automatically divided our dataset into 500 seed groups, which were then merged into 15, 20, 25 and 30 groups by AgglomerativeClustering. The group numbers were the only parameters assigned. The second method we tested was K-means using KMeans from scikit-learn, which is also a distance-based algorithm that tends to divide the data points into groups with small variates, which in the case of dermal denticles refers to controlling the visual differences within each group. The controlled group differences make the clustering results easier to interpret. The only parameter assigned is the number of groups K. We tested 20 and 50 groups and found that these also corresponded to the taxonomic groups of the phylogenetic tree. The third and fourth methods we tested were the density-based clustering algorithms DBSCAN and OPTICS using scikit-learn. These methods do not require the assignment of a number of groups. However, they do require information to determine the parameters related to the density and connectivity of the points to form meaningful groups. We used the default parameters provided by scikit-learn. In the case of density-based algorithms, they produce very unbalanced groups, i.e., one large group with a variety of visual features, many small ones, and an addition of groupless outliers. The unbalanced grouping results look random on the phylogenetic tree and can be difficult to explain, e.g., members of a very large group were scattered all over the tree, while smaller groups matched only a few species. Ultimately, we chose K-means because it most closely followed the two principles of natural clustering and controlled for visual differences within groups. K-means produced more visually controlled clustering results, had a simpler implementation for reproducing results, and its result was more consistent with natural clustering, with the alignments being consistent between different numbers of groups. Following the selection of K-means as our clustering algorithm, we conducted the clustering process without incorporating phylogenetic information. First, utilizing the K-means function from scikit-learn, we partitioned the dataset into 20 and 50 clusters. These clusters corresponded with the taxonomic groups delineated by the phylogenetic tree. Second, due to the scarcity of representative samples for F1-body positions, we adopted the k-means clustering result of 20 clusters as our final configuration. Out of the 20 morphogroups, we excluded eight clusters that were represented by fewer than three F1-body position denticle samples to ensure a robust sample size for the subsequent ancestral state reconstruction. Third, from the remaining 12 morphogroups, we further reduced the number to 10 by merging clusters with the lowest pairwise centroid distances, using a dimensionless threshold of 1.6. This step, which only merged the closest groups, was designed to simplify further analyses and did not alter the grouping patterns mapped on the phylogenetic tree. Additionally, merging these morphogroups did not impact the results of the ancestral state reconstruction (Fig. 1a , Supplementary Figs. 4 and 10 ). Computational fluid dynamics modelling for denticles This study employs FEniCS, an open-sourced finite element simulation platform, to perform the computational fluid dynamics (CFD) analysis for each denticle model as well as calculate the drag reduction effect (Fig. 5a, c ) 72 . For CFD modelling of denticles, the Variational Multiscale Incompressible Navier-Stokes Toolkit (VarMINT) was introduced 73 . VarMINT is a python module with reusable functionalities for solving the incompressible Navier-Stokes equations using the variational multiscale (VMS) formulation 74 . The VMS method is proven to be effective in circumventing the numerical difficulty brought by strong advection and can further reproduce dedicated vortex and turbulence in fluid flow modelling 75 , 76 . In the computational simulation, the micro-CT scanned 3D-models of denticles were converted to a FEniCS compatible format for CFD calculation. Then, for each CFD test, we expressed the streamline distribution and the drag reduction for the fluid flow over denticle samples. The streamline and vortices near the denticles can be observed in the provided animation files (Supplementary Movies 1 – 22 via Figshare: doi.org/10.6084/m9.figshare.21229172, and Source Data for scalebar dimensions). We emphasize that these flow conditions are selected based on the examination of the denticle geometry rather than the whole body of the sharks. Adding to this, species-specific environmental factors that may influence the drag conditions, such as water temperature, were not considered. Ancestral state reconstruction (ASR) We performed ASR using the ace function in the R package ape 78 to estimate the character evolution and reconstruct the ancestral states at nodes, using the published phylogenetic tree from Stein et al. (2018). The tree was pruned in R to only include the 73 species of extant sharks represented by the dermal denticle F1-body location. Choosing a single body location for the ASR was necessary for interspecific comparison (Fig. 3 ). The results are displayed as the statistical probability of each character state (morphogroup) in pie charts for each (ancestral) internode. Specifically, we tested the model fits of the following three character-evolution models. ER, equal transition rate among states (lnL = 8.386625 −13 ); SYM, symmetric transition rate between states (lnL = 0.000197); and ARD, all transition rates allowed to vary (lnL = 9.96239 −10 ). The best-fit model, SYM, was determined using likelihood ratio test. Correlated evolution between traits We tested whether the evolution between ridged and non-ridged denticle states is associated with the evolution of different habitat/niche preferences using the fitPagel function 79 implemented in the R package phytools 80 . Specifically, the ridged morphogroups were divided into binary trait states, where a ridged state included morphogroups 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, and 8. The remaining morphogroups, i.e., 7, 9, and 10, were categorized under the non-ridged state. We tested whether ridged versus non-ridged denticles is evolutionarily associated with the evolution of (1) deep versus shallow water habitats, and (2) reef versus non-reef habitats (Supplementary Fig. 5A–C ). We categorized the binary states of deep versus shallow habitats, and reef versus non-reef habitats following the categorization in Fishbase 51 . We use the same vocabulary to describe these binary states, which have been defined based on depth and geographic location of capture 23 , 51 , 81 . We first fit the directional dependency model (e.g., ridged or non-ridged is dependent on deep or shallow habitats, but not the other way around), interdependent evolution, and independent evolution between the binary traits and calculated the likelihood values and AICs for the models. We then chose the best model based on AIC weight 82 . Diversification rate analysis We estimated speciation, extinction and net diversification rates through time for a published 503-species shark phylogeny from Stein et al. (2018) employing both BAMM v. 2.5 11 , 83 , and ClaDS 84 . As BAMM relies on branch lengths to estimate diversification, and has been found to underestimate the number of shifts, we use it to detect major evolutionary changes 84 , 85 . The ClaDS method, however, is able to detect many small rate changes, as well as large changes in diversification 84 . We used the phylogeny from Stein et al. (2018) as it is the most taxon-complete tree to date. The phylogeny was built from the pre-existing Barcode of Life Data System and GenBank records, with 54 novel DNA sequences generated from their study, accounting for 642/1192 chondrichthyan species (54%). The tree was dated using ten fossils, of which one of them met the criteria of exhibiting diagnostic apomorphies with the superorder Squalomorphii, estimating the root node of the chondrichthyans to be 377.98 MYA. They generated 500 ‘treePL’-dated topologies for both fossil temporal calibrations (one fossil and all ten fossils). Subsequently, they manually added all taxa without DNA sequence data to each of the molecular-based 500 ‘treePL’-dated trees and used a polytomy resolver in R for unresolved clades. We used the ten-fossil calibrated tree and pruned it to only include the 503 species of Neoselachii (extant sharks) and assumed a complete sampling of extant taxa. The initial values for rate shift, and speciation and extinction rates were estimated using the setBAMMpriors function in the R package BAMMtools 83 . We performed two bamm runs, the first run with a total of 107 generations and the second run with a total of 28 × 58 generations of Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) searches, both with samples stored every 1000 generations, and a total of 1000 post-burnin samples (50%) were retained. Using the posteriors generated from the MCMC runs, we aimed to verify the diversification rates estimated by Sorensen et al. 2014 and to explore the overall pattern of shark diversification. Using the plot.bammdata function from BAMMtools we visualized the lineage-specific diversification and created time plots of the clade-specific diversification rates with the plotRateThroughTime function (Fig. 4 ). We used the fit_ClaDS function in the RPANDA package for the ClaDS analysis. Specifically, we used the shark phylogeny with 503 tips as the input phylogeny, assuming a complete taxonomic sampling (sample_fraction = 1.0). A MCMC run of 2 × 10 5 generations was launched for the ClaDS analysis. The mixing of parameter estimations was investigated using the plot_ClaDS_chain function and the ClaDS result was visualized using the plot_ClaDS_phylogeny function. Dependency analysis To test whether ridged and non-ridged dermal denticles have different diversification rates, we used the binary state speciation and extinction model (BiSSE) 26 implemented in the R package diversitree 27 . Specifically, we estimated the likelihoods of models that assumes either (1) the speciation rate is the same associated with the different states or (2) the speciation rate differs between states. The best-fit model was determined using likelihood ratio test. Furthermore, we launched an MCMC search for 10 5 generations under the full BiSSE model (6 parameters) to estimate the speciation rates. The model assuming that the speciation rate differs between states, resulted in a similar likelihood value (lnL = −451.89; AIC = 911.79) to the model assuming the same rate (lnL = −452.43; AIC = 910.85). Thus, the MCMC search results showed that the speciation rate associated with ridged denticles is slightly higher than the speciation rate with non-ridged denticles (Supplementary Fig. 5D ), although the results were not statistically significant ( P = 0.3026). Functional trait analysis Principal component analysis (PCA) This technique allows the projection of a set of related variables onto a composite set of variables of lower dimensions. It also allows one to test the significance of the variable dimensions in PCA to visualize differences between traits 32 . We performed PCA on our trait values (stiffness, hardness, mineral protection, damage resistance and drag reduction, see Source data) using the function dudi.pca in ade4 R package 86 . The standardized data were obtained from the default standard methods in dudi.pca. We visualized the space occupied by the traits by two-dimensional PCA axes with following methods. Kernel density estimation We used the two-dimensional kernel density estimation 87 to measure the occurrence probability of likely combinations of our trait values (stiffness, hardness, mineral protection, damage resistance, and drag reduction), and displayed the results in a two-dimensional space defined by PC axes 1 and 2 (Fig. 6 ). The decision of bandwidth used for the smoothing kernel was carried out by unconstrained bandwidth selectors 88 . The contour plot displaying colour gradients from yellow (0.5 quantile), orange (0.95 quantile) to red (0.99 quantile) is used to visualize the occurrence probability of a given trait combination in the PCA space (Fig. 6 ). The quantiles are a measure of the respective probability distribution, presenting the regions of lowest to highest trait occurrence probability. We used the ‘kde’ function for the kernel density estimation and the SAMSE pilot bandwidth selector to estimate the optimal bandwidth for every trait combination, both provided by the ‘ks’ R-package 88 , 89 .
