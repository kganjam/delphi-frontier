Introduction The purpose of this publication is to provide a standardized nomenclature for classifying lesions in the skeletal system of laboratory fishes. Terms of systemic nonproliferative lesions that are not specific to the skeletal system are not included here. Neoplasms that affect skeletal tissues, but do not arise from the skeletal system, are described in other documents. The skeletal tissues described here are bones, fins, cartilage, teeth, notochord/vertebrae, and joints. Scales are described further in the integumentary system document, and intramembranous bones (pin bones) are included in the soft tissue system document. Application of nomenclature to other specialized elements of the exoskeleton (e.g., odontodes, denticles, dermal plates, scutes, etc.) are not specifically addressed; adaptation using bone, teeth, and scale terminology is advised. The skeleton of fishes consists of two major categories: the endoskeleton and the dermal skeleton (exoskeleton) 1 . The endoskeleton includes bony and cartilaginous elements that develop in the deeper tissues and are what we commonly refer to as the “skeleton” of an animal. The dermal skeleton comprises the hard tissues which form within the dermis without a cartilaginous template (e.g., dermal bone, teeth, odontodes, scales, scutes, fin spines, and fin rays) 2 . The major categories of skeletal tissues (e.g., bone, cartilage, dentin, enamel) and their resident cell types (e.g., bone lining cells, osteoblasts/osteocytes/osteoclasts, chondroblasts/chondrocytes/chondroclasts, odontoblasts, and ameloblasts) are present in both teleosts and mammals. It is important to recognize that, unlike mammals, fish normally exhibit a range of intermediate tissue types as part of their mature skeleton. Among these is enameloid, an intermediate tissue sharing features of enamel and dentine, which coats the teeth. In addition, chondroid/hyaline-cell cartilage (tissue with features of both cartilage and connective tissue) and chondroid bone (tissue with features intermediate between cartilage and bone) are commonly present 3 ( Figs. 12.1 and 12.2 Fig. 12.1. At least 15 categories of cartilage have been described, some of which are not restricted to the endoskeleton, but develop within the dermal skeleton 4 . Skeletal regeneration is almost entirely restricted to the dermal skeleton (scales, fin rays, fin spines). Teleosts never stop growing, and thus all skeletal tissues continue to change throughout life whether by differentiation, mineralization, remodeling, or even metaplasia. Fluctuations in bone growth are typical and are often brought about by changes in temperature, light, diet, and other environmental cues. In some cases, bone growth may be delayed or even cease. These changes may leave defined ‘annual rings’, most evident in scales, opercula, and vertebrae 5 . The skeleton of fish is highly adaptive; altered feeding conditions and food types can alter cranial bone morphology, for example. Vertebral malformations, a relatively well-known occurrence, may develop from altered mechanical loads. In some species, remarkable changes in the skull, vertebrae, and scales can occur normally in association with migration/spawning. The ability of the skeleton to respond to mechanical changes in the environment, which in mammals is via the mechanosensory osteocyte and canalicular network, is significantly different in fishes. The canalicular network and connectivity of fish osteocytes are questionable; it is likely that osteoblasts, chondrocytes, and bone surface cells/osteocytes function as localized mechanosensory circuits 6 . Many of the characteristics of skeletal tissues are conserved among the vertebrates, and this includes cytokines and molecular factors familiar to those who work with mammals. Scant information is available concerning joint specific pathology in fish and thus this topic will not be discussed in detail; lesions involving the joints are generally not seen in toxicologic studies. However, zebrafish knockout strains used for osteoarthritis modelling do exhibit changes similar to those in mammals 7 - 9 . Intervertebral discs are described in the following notochord section, and for further comment on disease, see Deformation, Bone, Vertebra, Notochord, and Joint, Degenerative Joint Disease. Necropsy and Trimming The skeletal tissues are generally incorporated into the sectioning of fish tissues, particularly in small species where the entire fish is examined on a standard glass slide. Individual bones may be cleaned of soft tissue, fixed by immersion in neutral buffered formalin (10%), decalcified, embedded in paraffin, sectioned at 4–8 μm, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&amp;E). In young fish decalcification is often not necessary. Small laboratory fish may also be fixed in Davidson’s solution, which has mild decalcifying properties. Fish skeletal tissues are not as densely mineralized as mammalian bone, and decalcification with gentle acid solutions (such as Kristensen’s) or EDTA can be achieved relatively rapidly. Specialized investigations may require examination of undecalcified sections; the use of H&amp;E in addition to histochemical stains such as von Kossa and Goldner’s trichrome can be used. For decalcified sections, H&amp;E, Masson’s trichrome, Toluidine blue, Alizarin red, and Alcian blue stains have been successfully used to delineate fine change in various fish skeletal tissue types 4 , 10 , 11 . An array of clinical and basic science tools has been used to examine fish skeletal tissues, including computed tomography, electron microscopy, and mechanical testing by various methods 12 , 13 , 14 . Morphology, Physiology, Nomenclature, Diagnostic Criteria, and Differential Diagnosis 1. Bone In general, bone in fish is similar to that in mammals 15 . Specific terminology (described below) is used in the literature that the reader may find confusing as it applies to location, origin, mode of development and structural properties 16 . Endo- and exoskeleton refer to the location of the tissue; exoskeletal tissues form in contact with the epithelial ectoderm (scales, fin rays, teeth) while endoskeleton does not (endochondral bone). Dermal skeleton (exoskeleton) refers to teeth, scales, fin rays, and dermal bones that derive from the interaction of epidermis and mesenchyme. The dermal skeleton typically develops by membranous ossification, while endoskeleton is largely endochondral. Chondroid bone and cartilage in the dermal skeleton are secondary tissues. In a structural context, dermal skeletal and endoskeletal tissues may be woven or lamellar. Furthermore, bone may be compact or cancellous, and occasional osteons may be formed. Compact bone is characterized by tightly packed layers of mineralized matrix, while cancellous (spongy) bone is arranged in trabecular plates with intervening soft tissue cavities. Type I collagen is the major organic component; however, type II collagen can be present 16 . A recent bone tissue classification scheme has been published, which may prove useful in further harmonizing terminology among specialist groups 17 . Unmineralized bone matrix (osteoid) is deposited by osteoblasts directly into connective tissue (dermal/membranous), or onto/within cartilage (perichondral/endochondral). Also, portions of tendons in the muscle bodies may mineralize, forming sesamoids or “pin” bones. In teleosts, woven bone develops first, and is subsequently remodeled into lamellar, and even osteonal bone. Like mammals, bone may contain encased osteocytes but, in many species, it is acellular (more properly termed “anosteocytic”), lacking obvious cell processes within the matrix (like acellular cementum in mammals and crocodiles, and first-generation teeth in teleosts), which distinguishes it from typical dentine 3 , 18 . In some species, osteocytes remain at the surface but send cytoplasmic processes into the matrix (primary canaliculated, or tubular acellular bone) 19 . The classic paradigm that acellular bone is formed through polarized secretion of matrix without entrapment of osteocytes, has been recently challenged; authors propose that entrapped osteocytes undergo apoptosis and mineralization 20 . Unlike mammals, laboratory fish may have a poorly developed osteocyte canalicular system (zebrafish-cellular bone), or none at all (medaka-acellular bone) 21 . Acellular bone, like cellular bone, is capable of remodeling and can be a source for calcium mobilization. Early cellular bone may initially be acellular or sparsely populated as to mimic acellular bone. Usually, the presence of cellular or acellular bone is uniform within the endoskeleton for any given fish, but endoskeletal and exoskeletal bone types may differ within a species. In adult zebrafish, bone is cellular and cancellous, while in the medaka, it is anosteocytic 22 . However, early stages of bone in zebrafish are anosteocytic 23 . Bone turnover estimates are lacking in teleosts, but reports in salmon indicate that the processes of resorption and remodeling are regular. In farming/laboratory conditions where growth is rapid compared to the wild, fast bone growth may lead to the production of abundant non-mineralized matrix, which in time mineralizes sufficiently 24 . Calcium phosphate, in the form of hydroxyapatite, forms the mineral component. Mineralization occurs through inotropic calcification (crystal deposition along collagen fibrils). It is important to recognize that bone formation and bone mineralization are not the same; matrix is produced and mineralization occurs later. In zebrafish, mineralization of osteoid occurs within 6 hours of deposition, which is much faster than that reported in humans- several days 25 . Evidence in fish suggests that while osteoblasts produce the organic matrix, they may not be actively involved in mineralization 26 . In fish lacking osteocytes, other mesenchymal cells may assume osteocyte regulatory functions, similar to those of mammalian osteocytes 27 . Fish also have what is termed chondroid bone (CB), which is an intermediate tissue with characteristics of both cartilage and bone; it has a basophilic mineralized/bony collagenous matrix with nests of chondroid cells within lacunae, which may be immediately surrounded by cartilage matrix 28 , 29 . It may be highly cellular or poorly cellular, blends into other tissue types, and is found in joints, interfaces between cartilage and bone, and areas of increased stress. Formation of chondroid bone can have both type I and II collagen (typical of bone and cartilage, respectively) and can be identified with Trichrome staining, particularly in areas of growth 30 , 31 . It should be kept in mind, however, that other types of skeletal tissue, especially cartilage, may have both type I and II collagen 32 . Other histochemical stains may be used to better define the tissue and differentiate it from true cartilage 33 . Chondroid bone can be a stable tissue or appear in pathological processes such as metaplasia or neoplasia 3 , 34 . In zebrafish jawbone repair, chondrocyte-like cells produce both cartilage (Sox9a, Col2a1a) and bone (Runx2b, Col1a1a) associated proteins, which mineralize 35 . These chondrocytic cells derive from the periosteum or osteoblasts. In sea bream gills, chondroid bone chondrocytes lose collagen II expression with maturity, but instead have collagen I and osteonectin expression 36 . Thus, a population of mixed cartilage-bone cells can normally be present in a rapidly growing tissue, with or without subsequent differentiation into osteoblasts. As a result, bone repair in zebrafish can be a mixture of membranous and chondroid bone 35 . Chondroid bone is to be distinguished from secondary cartilage which, in addition to lacking bone matrix features, is formed by periosteum/endosteum on membrane bone during normal development 37 . Two types of CB are described, according to different ontogenetic pathways. Type 1 CB develops in multipotential periostea; osteoblasts secrete osteoid which surrounds the cells and begins to calcify; acid carbohydrates then increase within the matrix, and cells then acquire a chondrocyte morphology, even losing cell contacts. Ultrastructurally, the cells have characteristics of chondrocytes and matrix has woven bone properties 38 . In this way, young CB resembles cellular bone and is overlaid by a fibroblastic/mesenchymal layer. Type 2 CB is the equivalent of calcified hyaline cartilage or fibrocartilage 4 , 39 , 40 . Several characteristics of skeletal remodeling in teleosts distinguish it from that in mammals 1 : • Fish lack hematopoietic tissues in bone cavities; the dynamics between mammalian marrow and bone, which regulate osteoblast and osteoclast activities, are thus segregated in fish. • Osteoclasts in many species are small, mononucleated cells that resorb bone in non-lacunar fashion. • Teleosts obtain and release calcium through the gills (freshwater) or intestine (marine), and thus the endoskeleton is not usually a source or depot for calcium. Teleost bone metabolism is not primarily calcium driven, which is a common source of misinterpretation. • If minerals are needed by the fish, mobilization occurs in the exoskeleton first (scales). • The exoskeleton has a high capacity for regeneration, more than the endoskeleton. • In teleosts, growth and remodeling are continuous throughout life, with the development of species-specific structures (the kype structure on the head of salmon) occurring well into adulthood. Bone formation is typically perichondral, intramembranous, endochondral, or parachondral; the latter being characterized by the separation of osteogenesis from the cartilage by a few cell layers of connective tissue 23 , 41 . Perichondral bone is very common and predominates in young fish; osteoid is laid down in contact with the hyaline cartilage. This type of formation commonly persists as a thin layer of bone, without further ossification. As such, teleost long bones often present as a persistent rod of cartilage with a thin bony encasement, and cartilage condyles at both ends 42 . As the first matrix to be laid down, compact woven bone fibers lack orientation but are remodeled into parallel-fibered and lamellar bone, both of which form the majority of teleost bone. In the case of lamellar bone, oriented fibrils are deposited on the inner aspect of lamellae that surround vascular canals (osteon formation in some species). Subsequent lamellae may not be concentric, but are arranged in piled “plywood stacks”, forming lamellated bone. Remodeling can occur repeatedly at the same site, in the form of localized resorption and filling that is seemingly unrelated to bone growth 22 . Sharpey’s fibers (collagen bundles connecting soft tissue to bone) may be present, particularly in growing intramembranous dermal bone. Due to the abundance of these fibers in some species, the term “Sharpey fiber bone” has been applied 43 . Additionally, the presence of micron-sized tubules (termed “canals of Williamson”) within anosteocytic bone has led to some debate as to their content, whether cell processes (osteoblasts, cells of Williamson), hypomineralized collagen, or fluid 19 , 44 . In endochondral ossification, cartilage may be resorbed along a complete front, often without a spongiosa (zebrafish and medaka); adipose tissue fills the space 23 , 30 , 45 . In long bones, epiphysis-like maturation gradients may exist in the cartilage at either end, as well as in the diaphysis, where a bi-directional gradient can occur 46 , 47 . The removal of the cartilage may be incomplete, and cartilage may persist. Enlargement of bone cavities occurs by endosteal resorption and periosteal deposition. In bones that do not develop a central cavity, the cartilage model proceeds through a transition stage, where the matrix loses histochemical features of cartilage and assumes bone morphology and staining. The chondrocytes themselves also exhibit alterations with loss of collagen II production and upregulation of collagen X, osteonectin, and collagen I 36 . Osteoblasts can assume different morphologies depending on their level of activity. They may be pyriform, spindled, or cuboidal and can be indistinguishable from mononuclear osteoclasts 30 . Actively secreting osteoblasts exhibit polarization with increased basophilia and are positive for alkaline phosphatase 42 . Osteoclasts in fish may either be large multinucleated cells, such as those seen in mammals, or small mononuclear cells; both can, but do not necessarily have, ruffled borders. In medaka, osteoclasts in the early stages of resorption are mononucleated, with multinucleated forms appearing secondarily 48 . In zebrafish, the appearance of osteoclast types depends on age and the bone studied 22 . Therefore, proper visualization of remodelling activity may require staining of tissues for tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase (TRAP). Preosteoclasts also express TRAP, while osteoblasts express alkaline phosphatase. Osteoclasts are able to resorb calcified cartilage (chondroclasts) and uncalcified tissues associated with bone resorption areas 49 , 50 . Mononuclear osteoclasts are often flat/squamous, similar to other bone lining cells. In this manner, such osteoclasts form a smooth, non-lacunar type of bone resorption, and such cells may predominate in the scales. Aside from osteoclasts, bone mineral resorption may occur by osteocytic osteolysis and halastasis (removal of calcium phosphate without matrix lysis) 1 , 51 , 52 , 53 . The regulation of calcium in teleosts is primarily through the gill, secondarily in the intestine, and a minor component in the opercular epithelium. The kidneys maintain internal calcium levels through excretion of excess. Hormones involved in calcium homeostasis include stanniocalcin, calcitonin, estrogen, melatonin, calcitriol, retinoic acid, cortisol, growth hormone, prolactin, parathyroid hormone and parathyroid hormone related protein (PTHrP). Fibroblast growth factor 23 in fish may function in a similar manner to mammals 1 , 51 , 52 , 53 , 54 , 55 , 56 , 57 , 58 . Stress, circadian rhythms, vitellogenesis, smolting (the physiological and anatomical metamorphic adaptations observed with changes in environmental salinity), and osmoregulation may lead to changes in these hormones, effectively suppressing osteoblast or osteoclast activity 1 , 59 . Calcium deficiency is uncommon in teleosts, and the role of parathormone (PTH) in fish is uncertain. A PTHrP is present in some species which may function as a hypercalcemic factor, counteracting the effects of stanniocalcin. In teleosts, dietary phosphorus is a requirement; thus, inadequate phosphorus intake or starvation may lead to insufficient bone mineralization or resorption 60 . Phosphorus uptake in fish, like mammals, is vitamin D dependent. Vitamin D is stored in the liver of fish. Vertebrae form around the notochord, which is a mesodermally derived rod of large cartilage-like cells, constrained by a thick sheath, situated between the spinal cord and coelom; it serves as the major skeletal element for locomotion as well as for developmental fate information 61 . In elasmonbranchs the notochord persists throughout life. In teleosts it is retained in a reduced form, largely between vertebra, and functions like the mammalian nucleus pulposus. A single layer of chordoblasts, in a stack-of-coins formation, divides into chordocytes which then enlarge and become vacuolated 62 . Vacuolar content is constrained by thick basal laminar sheaths, which allow cells to exert pressure on the tissue walls 63 . Notochord cells throughout are joined together by junctional complexes, contain keratin, and secrete a sparse chondroid matrix 4 . The developed notochord is delimited by a complex sheath rich in type II collagen, proteoglycans and elastin. The chordoblasts in Atlantic salmon express alkaline phosphatase (ALP) at the time of sheath mineralization, similar to pre-osteoblasts and osteoblasts, suggesting that chordacentra formation is related to chordoblast function 65 . However, in the medaka and zebrafish, ALP expression was found only in the perichondral osteoblasts, suggesting a different method of chordacentra formation 66 . In some species it is reported that the chordacentra are transitory structures that degenerate during development 67 . The intervertebral portions of the sheath then thicken and remain unmineralized, forming the internal intervertebral ligaments (notochord sheath and outer elastic membrane) which are lined internally by the internal elastic membrane (in some species) and chordoblasts. Subsequently, a continuous layer of collagen (external collagenous ligament) is formed by the fibroblasts surrounding the sheath 68 . Thus, the growth plate zone is composed of several layers, from internal to external: chordoblasts, intervertebral ligament/notochord sheath, an external elastic membrane, an external intervertebral ligament (with an external layer of fibroblasts) delimited by the osteogenic zone/bone rims, a layer of osteoblasts abutting the bone, and an investment of loose vascular connective tissue 69 . At the same time, paired cartilages (arcualia), both dorsal and ventral to the notochord, fuse to one another and to the vertebra, forming the neural and hemal arches of the spine, respectively. The basal portions of the arcualia rest on the vertebra or are incorporated into it (arch centers, or archocentra). The arcualia undergo endochondral ossification. The third component (auto centers, or autocentra) is membranous bone that develops outside the collagenous investment of the chordacentra 26 , 70 . Studies in medaka have reported conflicting results 66 , 71 , 72 . Osteoblasts deposit osteoid, which is modelled into lamellar bone along the anterior and posterior endplate rims 68 . When combined with the remnants of the arcualia, the autocentra may developed elaborate ornamentations (maltese cross appearance), particularly when viewed in cross section. Vertebral growth occurs at the anterior and posterior endplates in progressively wider rings, forming an hourglass/biconoid shape (amphicoelous) with notochord tissue between. The outer rim of the autocentra is the growth zone; chordacentra do not grow appreciably and are subsequently incarcerated within the vertebra 67 , 73 . Within each vertebra, the two cones are composed of compact bone while the remaining autocentra bony tissues are cancellous. Osteoclasts are not necessary for vertebral development since mineralization occurs through the notochord sheath and intervertebral ligament. As such, remnants of the notochord are retained between vertebral bodies. Within the notochord tissue of salmon there may be remarkable reorganization as the fish develop, with the formation of large toroid chambers filled with clear content within the central axis, which are interrupted by central strands and intervertebral septa 62 . In this process, the cells lose their vacuoles and accumulate intermediate filaments. Remarkably, at sexual maturity these changes disappear and the chordocytes become secondarily vacuolated, much as before. In this process, chordocytes progress through various morphologies, including squamous, acanthocytic, finely vacuolated, and large vacuolated forms. Such changes can be seen in other fish species; however, they have not been investigated. Alterations in the vertebrae are commonly seen; exercise can increase bone formation and vertebral length significantly 73 . In aging fish, vertebral body resorption may be increased. Spinal deformities are commonly encountered in fish, particularly salmon, and are due to varied causes 74 . Vertebrae may be compressed, enlarged, fused, or dysplastic due to a combination of increased or decreased bone, collagen, cartilage, and mineral. Changes are also evident in the intervertebral discs. Under pathological conditions, the vertebral bone or notochord tissues may exhibit cartilage metaplasia 26 , 75 . 2. Fins Fish fins are skin folds supported by endoskeletal endochondral bone, and rays that derive from the dermis. Most fish have two sets of paired fins, the pectoral and pelvic, and three median fins 76 . The paired fins are attached to the pectoral and pelvic girdles, which are endoskeletal and do not regenerate. Fin rays (exoskeleton) are composed of either segmented (soft rays) or fused (spines) bone. Each ray, or lepidotrich, is composed of two symmetrical bony halves (hemitrichs), which are fused to varying degrees by ligaments; they are present in all bony fish and are derived from modified scales. Soft ray hemitrichs are composed of multiple uniform bony segments with intervening fibrous joints, allowing flexibility. Between each hemitrich pair is a layer of connective tissue, vessels, and nerves. When injured, scleroblasts associated with the epithelium synthesize the osteoid for bone production. Lepidotrichia regenerate from proximal to distal, successively adding new segments. Actinotrichia are fine cords of connective tissue that attach to the hemitrichs, generally at the distal tips; they are made of collagen-like elastoidin 77 . For many years the scales and fin rays of fish have been regarded as neural crest derivatives. Recently however it has been demonstrated that these tissues have a mesodermal origin in the medaka and zebrafish 78 . Methods for studying fin and scale regeneration are published 79 . In broader context, the homology between skeletal elements (bone, cartilage, fins, and scales) and teeth in fish may have bearing on the interpretation of chondro-osseous elements found in cases of chronic injury, as well as in sarcomas of the jaw, fins, and skin. Bone – Congenital/Developmental Lesions Congenital lesions involving piscine skeletal tissues are not frequently observed in small fish species used in toxicological studies but are well known in salmonids. However, zebrafish exposed to TCDD developed craniofacial deformities, in addition to other system deformities 80 . Malformations occur with low prevalence and are attributed to genetic factors, hypoxic episodes, nutritional imbalances, rearing density, infections, trauma, and many more 16 , 81 . Mortality in deformed fry is high. In general, skeletal anomalies are conspicuous; vertebral anomalies are well described. Vertebral malformations have been observed in zebrafish 82 . In routine studies, a provisional diagnosis of increased osteoid can be made from decalcified H&amp;E sections based on the presence of clearly demarcated zones of pale staining bone matrix associated with plump osteoblasts. Osteoid is finely fibrillar/amorphous, is intimately associated with or entraps osteoblasts, and becomes mineralized; features which differentiate it from fibrous stromal collagen. With trichrome stains in decalcified sections, osteoid seams will stain pale green or blue (when compared to to the red, more mature and presumably well mineralized bony tissues), adjoined by regimented osteoblasts situated between fine collagen bundles. Osteoid is composed of collagen and is therefore birefringent (unlike fibrin). The use of picrosirius red collagen staining has been reported as a method to discriminate osteoid from bone by the relative quality of birefringence and collagen type 120 . However, the ability of picrosirius red to differentiate types of collagen is disputed 121 , 122 . Histochemical staining generally does not allow reliable differentiation of osteoid from fibrous stromal collagen when evaluating intercellular matrices in osteogenic neoplasia 123 . However, methods have been published using human tissues that make a strong case for their discriminatory ability 124 . Nevertheless, validation of such mammalian methods must be done for any fish species in question, since the staining characteristics of the various skeletal tissues are likely to be significantly different. In areas of active membranous bone formation, the detection of osteoid may be straightforward 43 . Studies with undecalcified sections would be required to confirm the presence of osteoid. It is important to realize that fish, depending on circumstances, can have severe hypomineralization of bone without identifiable changes histologically 26 . As such, the changes inherent to bone maturation that allow histochemical discrimination may not be present or may be altered in the population studied. Minor variations in the tinctorial qualities of the matrix in standard H&amp;E sections (decalcified) should not be interpreted as definitive evidence of variations in osteoid. Various osteosarcoma subtypes are recognized in mammals, and cartilage or fibrous tissues are a common component (chondroblastic, fibroblastic osteosarcoma); however, subtyping of these tumors is not conducted in toxicity studies. The various presentations are not surprising, as the progenitor cells of bone can produce bone, cartilage, and fibrous tissue as a reparative response. In addition, the commonality of chondroid bone or mixed bone types in fish may lead to additional osteosarcoma subtypes specific to fish. Bone tumors of scales (lepidocytomas/lepidosarcomas) are described in the Integument chapter. Reported tumors are single, lack metastasis, and do not arise from osteomas 128 , 133 , 134 . Cartilage Cartilaginous tissues figure prominently in the skeleton, not only during development, but as persistent tissues in the adult. The blastema that becomes cartilage begins as a condensation of mesenchymal cells that develop into chondroblasts and eventually become separated by secretion of extracellular matrix (differentiation). These blastemas may be very small and have very little matrix; this may be problematic, as fully differentiated cartilages in fish may look similar. Hyaline cartilages are generally similar to that seen in mammals and often merge with other cartilage types. A simplified classification system highlights the common hyaline cartilage modifications seen, which generally fall into the following: addition of matrix fibers (collagen, elastin), calcified cartilage, chondroid bone, and transformation/resorption (replacement/infiltration by adipose, endochondral bone formation, connective tissue metaplasia) 4 , 136 . These tissue types can be recognized and discriminated from bone using H&amp;E, trichrome, toluidine blue, PAS, Alcian blue, safranin O/fast green, and elastin stains 4 , 31 . The term ‘chondroid’ has historically been used to describe tissue types which have properties of both of cartilage and fibrous tissue, but has also been more specifically used to describe hyaline-cell cartilage, which is composed of large ovoid cells separated by scant matrix (as defined in the classification system cited above; it is distinct from matrix-rich hyaline cartilage which is typical of mammalian hyaline cartilage) 3 , 5 , 137 . The use of the term ‘chondroid’ as a tissue category should be avoided, as it is often used non-specifically as a descriptive term and more precise terms are available. In some cases, hyaline cartilage exhibits a maturation gradient, producing a physis with resting, proliferating, and hypertrophied zones. Cartilage growth can occur at the periphery (perichondrium) as well as interstitially. Resorption of cartilage, developmentally, does not primarily occur by macrophage ingestion, but rather may involve transdifferentiation/metaplasia of cartilage into other connective tissues 138 . The major collagen in fish is type II. Chondroid bone is described further in the Bone section of this chapter. Endochondral ossification, in the mammalian sense, exists in teleosts (salmon, carp) but is generally absent from smaller species such as zebrafish and medaka. In these species the replacement of cartilage by bone occurs by the perichondral deposition of a bony collar. Chondrocytes can undergo hypertrophy, but the cartilage is removed from the diaphyseal body and is not replaced by bone. The resulting space (sometimes referred to as marrow spaces, although fish do not deposit hematopoietic marrow in bone, but rather in the kidneys) is filled with adipose. Physeal structures are encountered in various locations in some species (such as carp), resembling epiphyseal growth plates in mammals 31 . Recent investigations in experimental osteoarthritis in the jaw of zebrafish elucidate the regenerative capacity of cartilage 8 , 9 . Calcified cartilage is a permanent feature in the skeleton of many species; hydroxyapatite is identified by the presence of globules of spheritic calcification (described in the Teeth section) with the matrix, forming concentric rings of Liesegang; groups may be further surrounded by waves of Liesegang 139 . The calcified globules exhibit metachromasia with histochemical staining 37 . Mucochondroid is a special cartilage-type tissue in the skull that contains fibroblasts or hyaline cells in a mucinous matrix (mucous connective tissue) 140 ; it is described further in the Soft Tissues manuscript. Tooth Aside from edentulous species, fish characteristically have continuous life-long formation and replacement of teeth. They may be present throughout the mouth, on the branchial arches, and/or in the caudal pharynx 1 . Teeth are present in a broad spectrum of forms, and bear similarities to integumentary structures (dermal skeleton) such as placoid scales and denticles (odontodes) 2 . In most cases, teeth are peg-like or conical. Cypriniforms, including the zebrafish, lack oral teeth and have only pharyngeal teeth. The medaka has both an oral and pharyngeal dentition, with as many as 1000 erupted pharyngeal teeth alone 148 . Oral and pharyngeal teeth also develop differently, in terms of germ layer contribution; oral teeth derive from the ectoderm, while pharyngeal teeth derive from endoderm 149 . Many species of fish (including zebrafish) have pharyngeal jaws which have robust musculature 3 . Pharyngeal teeth are arranged in rows that are constantly replaced, though not necessarily in synchronous fashion. The frequency of replacement depends on relative attrition, diet, and time of year (which may all be related) 5 . The first generation of teeth in teleosts forms outside the jaw bones, within the dermis (extramedullary), while replacement teeth may develop in similar fashion (as in zebrafish), or within the jaw (intramedullary). The ability of fish to have multiple replacement cycles allows for phenotypic plasticity, which may occur in single or successive generations 150 . Variation in tooth shape, number, and tooth rows are associated with pathology, diet, attrition, intraspecific variation, and development 3 , 151 . Dental laminae can be characterized as (1) continuous or discontinuous, and (2) permanent or non-permanent 163 . The origin location of the tooth germ can be independent of development location (i.e., extramedullary or intramedullary) 158 , 164 . The formation of a dental lamina in fish is less obvious than in mammals, and in some cases can be arguably absent; small regions of cells may be arranged in isolated units within the mesenchyme, rather than in a continuous fashion. In fish with intramedullary dentition, epithelial downgrowths must pass through replacement pores within the bone, which may be re-established many times for the replacement teeth 3 . In the zebrafish, all functional teeth are associated from the beginning with a non-permanent, discontinuous epithelial downgrowth (successional laminae). The enamel organ of first-generation teeth develops directly from the oropharyngeal epithelium, without a lamina 165 . The first indication of tooth formation is a focal thickening of the epithelium (basal layer or entire thickness), with eventual accumulation of mesenchymal cells in the subjacent stroma. The invagination of the oral epithelium into the mesenchyme results in a dental organ and papilla 2 . In salmon the opposite occurs; it is the mesenchyme that evaginates into the epithelium 166 . Mesenchymal cells subjacent to the IDE differentiate into pre-odontoblasts; immediately prior to enameloid formation they mature into odontoblasts. Enameloid is very similar to enamel, but with a less orderly crystalline arrangement; the collagen component is deposited by odontoblasts with contribution of matrix by ameloblasts 167 . The first deposits of enameloid are in the matrix surrounding odontoblast processes, which themselves may extend to the basal lamina of the ameloblast layer. The basal lamina between the ameloblasts and enameloid is subsequently removed, and mineralization begins in the enameloid. At this point, pre-dentine is already present and mineralization begins at the tooth tip. The dentine-enameloid junction, which may be irregular, marks the original location for the basal lamina 5 . Centripetal mineralization of the pre-dentine, toward the odontoblast layer (tip-down, outward-in), marks the formation of dentine. Odontoblast processes are not generally observed, or are relatively short, in first generation teeth. The dental papilla in first generation teeth is often composed entirely of odontoblasts or even a single odontoblast, lacking capillaries and nerves. Subsequent dentitions, and in particular larger teeth, may have larger and more developed pulp 158 . Dentine deposition can continue after tooth eruption 168 . Enameloid maturation begins with increased mineralization and progressive loss of organic matrix, at a time just prior to tooth attachment. The bone of attachment forms at the point of dentinal proximity, with or without an underlying supporting bone. The dentin links to the bone by ligaments that may or may not mineralize 2 . The cells producing the collagen, dentin, and attachment bone are often difficult to discriminate from one another, as they derive from the same mesenchymal population 168 , 169 . In zebrafish, fusion of dentine with attachment bone occurs (in decalcified sections toluidine blue staining can differentiate the tissues) 170 . Hertwig’s root sheath does not disintegrate in fishes 171 , 172 . Replacement : New teeth form beneath or next to the older teeth, being arranged in generational rows connected via an epidermal lamina that buds from the previous tooth. In zebrafish and most species studied, functional teeth are associated with epithelial downgrowths termed ‘successional laminae’, from which replacement teeth develop. Such laminae may remain quiescent, only to develop upon stimulation 165 , 173 . Replacement teeth may differ significantly from first generation teeth in terms of size, shape, skeletal support, and epithelial connections 154 . As the dentin grows, constriction of the pulp vessels and nerves may lead to pulp atrophy. Shedding is an active process, not a result of wear or damage, and proceeds in most cases from base to apex 158 . Osteoclasts/odontoclasts remove the bony attachments, and teeth are lost or resorbed 5 , 174 . In zebrafish, tooth movement does not occur, but rather epithelial remodeling and retraction leads to dental exposure 165 . 2) Tissue types Cementum is a “bone-like mineralized tissue secreted by cementoblasts on the surface of root dentine or, in some animals, crown enamel” 171 . Acellular and cellular cementum (primary and secondary, respectively) are characterized by laminations, a lower degree of mineralization compared to bone and dentine, and many coarse fibers (Sharpey’s fibers) running through it 175 . The organic matrix of cementum is similar to bone and dentine, but differs in that it lacks innervation and vascularization 167 . Cementum is conspicuously present in thecodont vertebrates (typical of mammals), which have a discrete periodontium, as opposed to most fishes 3 . In some species, particularly with robust dentitions ( Anarhichas , wolffish), tissue resembling cementum is evident, but in most species a clearly defined cementum is absent 5 , 172 , 175 . The “bone of attachment” in many teleost teeth is derived from odontogenic cells and is a type of dentine rather than cementum or true bone 3 , 167 . In bony fish, teeth are generally attached to the jaw by ligaments. Dentine is a mineralized collagenous tissue surrounding a mesenchymal papilla which, at some stage in development, possesses cells with long processes that penetrate the tissue. Generally, dentine lacks cell bodies enclosed within the matrix; however, occasional cells can become entrapped within complex teeth. Calcification of the matrix may occur in two ways: inotropic and spheritic calcification. Inotropic, or homogeneous calcification develops from seeding centers associated with collagen bundles. Spheritic calcification, on the other hand, leads to the formation of globular lime bodies where hydroxyapatite crystallites radiate from the center, without regard to collagen. Spheritic calcification is also present in scales (corpuscles of Mandl), calcified cartilage, and bone in some cases 139 . More than one type of dentine may be present, depending on tooth type, however orthodentine is predominantly present, as seen in mammals. The dentine of fishes is of an intermediate type, which contains regular tubules running from the pulp (containing odontoblast processes- Tomes fibers) orthogonally across the entire thickness of the dentin, and well into the enameloid 176 . The first-generation teeth (and sometimes adult teeth, as well as vasodentine) may lack dentinal tubules (and thus, Tomes fibers), forming an acellular, atubular tissue that is not readily distinguishable from acellular bone 3 , 166 . However, even without tubules, dentine should not contain Sharpey’s fibers (thick collagenous fiber bundles that extend from mesenchymal tissue to bone/cementum) 176 . The structural markings identified in mammalian (particularly human) orthodentine, such as Schreger lines and incremental lines, have been identified in fishes 176 , 177 , 178 . Other types of dentine, including vasodentine (Gadidae), osteodentine (pike), and plicidentine (gar) are remarkably different from typical orthodentine, and may be encountered in some species, particularly in larger teeth 5 , 139 , 176 . Enameloid is a hypermineralized, non-prismatic, fibrous tissue that coats the teeth (cap and collar enameloid) and is composed of fluoridated carbonate-apatites 179 . It forms by the combined action of the ameloblasts and odontoblasts, unlike enamel 34 . Enameloid appears prior to dentine development, contains many collagen fibers, and lacks growth lines since mineralization occurs in one event 163 . Odontoblast processes are often present in enameloid, and thus it may be considered a form of modified dentine 179 . Unlike enamel, enameloid forms in a centripetal manner; the external shape of the tooth is therefore similar to the boundary between the IDE and dental papilla at the initiation of matrix deposition. In some species, the first-generation teeth enameloid may not be distinguishable, or it may be entirely lacking 158 . In demineralized sections, enameloid usually lacks any apparent matrix, but in some species the Tomes fibers from the dentinal tubules may persist 175 . Amelogenin is the major matrix protein. The etiology of these tumors is unknown, however environmental contamination and viral infection are suspected. A wide variety of histologic patterns have been described 186 . Some ameloblastomas in Chinook salmon ( Oncorhynchus tshawytscha ) appeared to originate in extra-oral locations such as the gill arch and fin 184 . A final note concerning tumors composed of a mixture of odontogenic epithelium and mesenchyme (ameloblastic fibroma/fibro-odontoma). In domestic animals such neoplasms present along a continuum of inductive differentiation, spanning from ameloblastic fibroma (least differentiated) to compound odontoma (highly differentiated) 187 . Typically, mesenchymal components comprise the bulk of the lesion. Consensus regarding nomenclature of mixed dental tumors in animal species has not been reached; it can hardly be done for fish, where few cases are available to examine. The term ameloblastic odontoma is not favored 186 , 187 . Currently there are too few cases available to address the diagnosis of odontoameloblastoma in fishes at this time 188 . Ameloblastic fibromas and fibro-odontomas are described as exophytic, often densely cellular masses; although numerous denticles can be present, careful examination may be required to reveal hard tissue elements 189 . Hamartomatous malformations are also possible, which would be present early in development, however injury to developing teeth in adult fish could also lead to malformed teeth that may present as grossly evident nodules. The occurrence of a true neoplasm that produces dental hard tissues therefore requires unequivocal evidence of neoplastic tissue; haphazard odontogenic mesenchyme and tooth-like elements with some primitive features 183 , 192 . Few cases are described in fishes; they are often considered to be malformations (hamartomas) rather than true neoplasms, however local bone invasion has been reported 126 , 189 , 193 , 194 . Angelfish ( Pterophyllum scalare ) are particularly well known to produce lip nodules (fibromas or fibrous hyperplasia) that contain denticles. Typically, they are pedunculated lip masses composed of spongy fibrous tissue containing bone and well-formed teeth in various stages of development, all radiating from the point of attachment and oriented perpendicular to the epithelial surface; such features suggest reactive hyperplasia rather than neoplasia 183 . Depending on the cell of origin, these may be diagnosed as foci of Fibroplasia, soft tissues, or Hyperplasia, fibrous connective tissue, tooth. Significant bone may be produced within an odontogenic mass (possibly a result of repeated trauma), obscuring the presence of abnormal tooth elements; careful examination of the tumor is necessary to differentiate this from osteoma 192 .
