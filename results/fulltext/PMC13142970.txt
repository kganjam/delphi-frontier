Predator–prey interactions can be viewed as a highly conserved evolutionary game, played out by a remarkably diverse set of species. Whether it is dragonflies hunting flies ( 1 ), cheetahs chasing steenboks ( 2 ), or killer whales pursuing bluefin tuna ( 3 ), the fundamental objective remains the same: predators must come into close proximity with their prey to capture them, while prey must avoid this condition. Despite the conserved nature of predator–prey interactions, the biomechanical traits that shape their outcome, such as speed and maneuverability, vary widely across species. This variability is largely determined by body size and by the physical properties of the medium through which animals move (air, land, or water) ( 4 – 6 ). As a result, the relevant biomechanical traits of predators and prey can differ by several orders of magnitude. An open question, then, is what the consequences of such variability are for the dynamics of predator–prey interactions across different predator–prey pairs? For instance, what are the characteristic spatial and temporal scales of predator–prey interactions across systems? Do certain combinations of body size and domain consistently favor predators or prey? Are there distinct regimes in which particular biomechanical traits become especially important in shaping the outcome of pursuit? Addressing these questions requires establishing a link between the biomechanical traits of predators and prey and the outcomes of their interactions. We here focus on a widespread class of interactions, pursuit predation, in which a predator actively chases a fleeing prey, as opposed to ambush predation or ballistic interception ( 7 – 9 ). Predators are generally larger than their prey and, as a result, tend to be faster ( 5 , 10 ). This speed advantage allows them to close the relative distance during a pursuit, potentially leading to a straightforward capture if the prey continues along an unchanging path. However, because maneuverability tends to decrease with size ( 4 ), smaller prey are typically capable of sharper turns, which may allow prey to outmaneuver their pursuer ( 11 ). This trade-off between speed and maneuverability is formalized in a geometric model known as the turning gambit ( 11 ). In the turning gambit model, a faster predator approaches a slower prey in a head-to-tail alignment. Once the distance between them falls below a threshold X 0 , the prey initiates a sharp evasive turn while maintaining constant speed V e and turning radius R e . The predator, moving at speed V p with minimum turning radius R p , attempts to respond. The model evaluates whether the prey can outmaneuver the predator and reach a region that is kinematically inaccessible to the predator due to its maneuverability constraints ( Fig. 1 A ). A central prediction of the model is that escape is possible when the prey-to-predator speed ratio exceeds the square root of the ratio of their turning radii ( Fig. 1 B ). Fig. 1. While the turning gambit has been applied to individual species ( 11 – 13 ) or specific domains ( 14 ), it has not yet been used to examine differences across a broad spectrum of body sizes and domains, nor has its capacity to explain cross-domain (aquatic, terrestrial, aerial) variation in capture outcomes been systematically tested. In this study, we synthesize previously published data on the allometric scaling of maximum speed and minimum turning radius ( 4 – 6 , 13 , 15 ) with turning gambit theory to examine how body size and locomotor domain influence pursuit–evasion outcomes, and compare these predictions to empirical capture-success rates reported across aquatic, aerial, and terrestrial systems. We then revisit a key assumption of the turning gambit model: that predators can respond instantaneously to prey maneuvers. In reality, all animals experience sensory–motor delays, due to the time required for perception, neural processing, and motor execution ( 16 ). By incorporating empirically grounded estimates of these delays, we show that even modest delays can substantially alter the turning gambits’ predictions, shifting the advantage from predators to prey and better aligning theoretical predictions with the low capture success rates observed in nature. Moreover, these delays interact with domain-specific biomechanics to generate distinct pursuit–evasion regimes in which different traits are more important for the outcome of pursuit encounters. Together, our findings reveal how the interplay of biomechanics and sensory-motor delays shapes pursuit dynamics across air, land, and water. The Allometric Scaling of the Turning Gambit Scaling of Maximum Speed. The turning gambit is characterized by two biomechanical traits: maximum speed and minimum turning radius. Across animals that fly, run, or swim, maximum speed scales with body size in a hump-shaped manner, with intermediate-sized animals achieving the highest velocities across all three domains. Hirt et al. captured this with a phenomenological relationship ( 5 ): [1] V max = a M b 1 − e − h M k . Here, M denotes body mass, V max the maximum speed, and a , b , h , and k are empirically fitted parameters based on data from 394 species ( 5 ). For small body sizes, the relationship approximates a simple power law. The term 1 − e − h M k accounts for the observed decline in relative speed at larger body sizes, deviating from the power-law trend. The derived equations for aquatic and aerial locomotion share a similar structure: both include the density of the surrounding medium, a surface area-related coefficient, a (lateral) lift term, and they scale with body mass to the power of M 1 / 3 . These similarities are intuitive, as turning in both domains requires generating lateral forces through interaction with the surrounding medium. A key difference, however, lies in the medium’s density. Water is approximately 1,000 times denser than air. Since the other relevant parameters are of comparable magnitude across domains, we predict that aquatic animals should exhibit turning radii roughly three orders of magnitude smaller than their aerial counterparts. This prediction is consistent with empirical observations, as shown in Fig. 2 B . Importantly, the predicted turning radii in the figure are not fitted to the data but are derived from independently estimated parameter values (see SI Appendix for the derivations). Moreover, empirical studies confirm that in aquatic domains, turning radius is largely independent of speed ( 6 , 17 ). Locomotion in the terrestrial domain differs fundamentally from that in aquatic and aerial systems. On land, the minimum turning radius is constrained either by the grip provided by the feet or by the maximum force the limbs can withstand. Tan and Wilson identified these two limiting regimes in horses ( 18 ). In the grip-limited case—which applies when friction with the surface is the limiting factor—the turning radius scales with the square of the animal’s speed. This scaling has been empirically validated in species such as northern quolls, cheetahs, lions, impalas, and zebras ( 15 , 19 ). In this paper, we focus solely on the grip-limited case as even relatively large terrestrial animals such as cheetahs, lions, impalas, and zebras appear to be grip-limited ( 2 ). As the transition between the two domains is a function of mass ( 18 ), we assume that all lighter animals will also be grip-limited. This includes most of the predator–prey pairs in our analysis. The downturn of the terrestrial turning-radius curve at large body masses arises because the terrestrial turning radius scales with V 2 , while maximum speed peaks and subsequently declines at high body mass. As a result, reductions in speed in very large animals lead to smaller predicted turning radii. Empirical data for large terrestrial animals are currently insufficient to confirm or refute this prediction. Our analysis highlights substantial differences in locomotory capabilities across domains, driven largely by medium density. Aerial animals achieve the highest speeds, whereas aquatic animals can turn the sharpest, with turning radii often orders of magnitude smaller than those in air or on land. To investigate how these biomechanical constraints shape predator–prey interactions, we integrate them into the turning gambit framework and examine their implications for the dynamics of pursuit predation. The turning gambit. The turning gambit framework delineates two distinct regions: one in which the prey can successfully outmaneuver the predator and reach a “safe zone” that is unreachable to the predator due to its own turning limitation (the escape region), and another in which the predator is biomechanically capable of intercepting prey before they can reach this zone (the capture region). Whether a given predator–prey pair falls into the escape or capture region is determined by two key ratios: the ratio of their maximum speeds ( v = V e V p ) and the ratio of their minimum turning radii ( r = R e R p ). The condition under which prey can evade capture was analytically derived by Howland ( 11 ), yielding the following relationship: [2] v &gt; r . We evaluated this condition using empirical predator–prey mass pairs from vertebrate species that operate in the same locomotor medium (aquatic, aerial, or terrestrial). We focus on vertebrates because several assumptions of the turning-gambit framework and the locomotory scaling laws are less applicable to invertebrates. Small aquatic invertebrates typically operate at low Reynolds numbers and rely on intermittent burst–coast or jump-like motion rather than sustained, constant-speed swimming ( 20 , 21 ). Flying insects use unsteady, flapping-wing aerodynamics that do not conform to the fixed-wing mechanics used to derive turning-radius scaling ( 22 ). Likewise, the terrestrial friction coefficients underlying our turning-radius estimates are parameterized from four-legged mammals and may not generalize to arthropods with different limb morphologies. For each predator–prey pair, we computed the speed ratio: [3] v = M e M p b 1 − e − h M e i 1 − e − h M p i = m b γ M e , M p , where m = M e M p is the prey-to-predator mass ratio, and γ ( M e , M p ) captures the non-power-law deviation introduced by the hump-shaped scaling of maximum speed. The turning radius ratio, r , follows different scaling relations across domains (derivation in SI Appendix ): [4] r = v 2 for terrestrial animals m 1 / 3 for aerial and aquatic animals . In the terrestrial domain, substituting r = v 2 into Eq. 2 , shows that predator and prey lie exactly on the boundary. Thus neither has a fundamental biomechanical advantage, and outcomes hinge on species-specific deviations in speed or maneuverability. In contrast, the aerial and aquatic domains yield a different outcome. Substituting r = m 1 / 3 into Eq. 2 , we find that prey can evade if: [5] m 1 6 − b &lt; γ M e , M p . At small masses where γ ≈ 1 , prey escape depends primarily on the speed-scaling exponent b . In both aquatic (b = 0.37) and aerial (b = 0.18) domains, b exceeds 1 6 , indicating that predators can catch prey as long as the prey is smaller ( SI Appendix ). At large predator masses, however, the hump-shaped speed curve reduces predator advantage, creating a mixed region in which predators capture smaller prey but fail against larger ones. While body size explains a substantial proportion of the variation in speed and maneuverability across species ( Fig. 2 A and B ), animals of similar size can still differ markedly in these traits. As a result, the outcomes of specific predator–prey interactions may deviate from the predictions of our model, which reflects average allometric scaling relationships. To quantify how such deviations influence pursuit outcomes, we calculate the number of SD by which a prey or predator must change their speed to shift from the capture region to the escape region of the turning gambit (or vice versa). In the aerial domain, most prey would need only modest increases in speed—on average, 0.1 SD ( Fig. 2 C ) to shift into the escape region. This suggests that while most predators tend to hold a slight biomechanical advantage, relatively small species-specific deviations in locomotory traits may enable prey to evade capture. In aquatic systems, however, prey would require much larger deviations (often 1 to 4 SD), reflecting the stronger allometric advantage of aquatic predators arising from steeper speed–mass scaling. Taken together, these results suggest that in terrestrial systems, the ability to escape or capture is primarily governed by species-specific deviations in locomotory traits from the average. In aerial and aquatic domains, the turning gambit model predicts a general predator advantage over small prey, with this advantage being much more pronounced in aquatic systems. As a result, one would expect the highest capture success rates in aquatic pursuit predation, followed by aerial and then terrestrial systems. Yet empirical capture rates show the opposite pattern: pursuit success is lowest in aquatic systems and highest in terrestrial systems, although the difference in capture success between aquatic and aerial predators was not statistically significant ( Fig. 2 D and SI Appendix ). A striking example is provided by field observations of Caranx melampygus and Aphareus furca hunting small prey fish at spawning sites, where only 4% of attacks resulted in successful capture ( 30 ). This low success rate contrasts sharply with model predictions, which, given prey masses on the order of grams and predator masses in the kilogram range, classify these encounters as highly favorable for the predator. The Turning Gambit with Sensory-Motor Delay In the original formulation of the turning gambit, predators are assumed to respond instantaneously to prey maneuvers. In reality, all animals experience sensory–motor delays arising from the time required for sensory processing, neural transmission, and motor execution. Consequently, predators can only respond to prey maneuvers after a delay ( 69 , 70 ). Even brief delays can substantially alter the geometry of pursuit, as the predator begins turning only after the prey has already changed course. The extent to which the prey can change its heading during this delay can be approximated by the angular displacement ω e τ p , where ω e = V e R e is the prey’s turning rate and τ p is the predator’s sensory–motor delay. To estimate how much rotation a prey can achieve during a predator’s sensory–motor delay across different predator–prey pairs, we apply the previously introduced allometric scaling relationships for maximum speed (Eq. 1 ) and minimum turning radius (equations in Table 1 ), and additionally introduce a scaling law for visual sensory–motor delays, given by τ p = 0.1 M 0.14 ( 70 ). Strikingly, most aquatic prey are predicted to be able to complete a full 180 ° turn within the predator’s delay window, a consequence of the much smaller turning radii afforded by locomotion in the denser aquatic medium. In contrast, most aerial and terrestrial prey fall well below 45 ° , with median angular displacements around 10 ° . These differences suggest that aquatic prey benefit from a larger head start in their maneuver before their predators begin to respond. To further explore the role of sensory–motor delays in shaping pursuit and evasion, we extend the turning gambit framework to explicitly account for the predator’s sensory-motor delay, as illustrated in Fig. 3 A . A mathematical description is provided in Materials and Methods . In this formulation, we neglect the prey’s sensory–motor delay, based on the assumption that it initiates the evasive maneuver and can compensate for its own delay by adjusting the timing of its response ( 70 ). The prey begins its turn at a distance X 0 from the predator, executing the maneuver with turning rate ω e and turning radius R e . In contrast to the original model, the predator does not respond immediately: it continues in a straight line at speed V p for a duration τ p , corresponding to its sensory–motor delay. Only after this delay does the predator begin to turn, with turning rate ω p and turning radius R p . To evaluate how delays influence the timing of escape, we introduce the concept of the escape window ( Fig. 3 B ). Since the prey can choose when to initiate its evasive turn, we evaluate the outcome for different values of X 0 . The escape window, denoted Δ X , is defined as the range of initiation distances from which the prey can successfully evade capture. Because the significance of this spatial range depends on the animals’ speeds—covering one meter at 1 cm/s is not equivalent to doing so at 10 m/s —we convert it into a temporal window. This yields the escape window: [6] Δ t = Δ X V p − V e , which expresses the time window within which the prey must initiate its maneuver to stay out of reach of the predator. Fig. 3 D shows the escape windows for the same set of predator–prey mass pairs analyzed previously, now evaluated under predator sensory–motor delays of 0, 50, and 100 ms. In the absence of delay, only a few prey are predicted to escape (same data points as in the green shaded area of Fig. 2 ), and the median escape window across all domains is zero. However, once sensory–motor delays are introduced, the model’s predictions shift dramatically: all prey are now predicted to evade, shifting the advantage from predator to prey. Comparing the three domains, the escape window is, on average, about an order of magnitude larger in terrestrial and aerial systems than in aquatic ones. Consequently, successful evasion requires far more precise timing for aquatic prey. Furthermore, we observe substantial variation in escape window size as a function of body mass within all three domains, with lighter individuals consistently predicted to require more precise timing of their maneuvers than larger ones ( Fig. 3 D ). In summary, although the original formulation of the turning gambit posits that prey escape predators by outmaneuvering them, the scaling of locomotory traits across aquatic, aerial, and terrestrial domains suggests that most prey should, in fact, be unable to do so. Incorporating sensory–motor delays into the model fundamentally alters this prediction, shifting the advantage toward prey by giving them a critical head start in their escape maneuver. Furthermore, introducing sensory–motor delays leads to the prediction that aquatic prey must time their evasive maneuvers with roughly an order of magnitude greater precision than prey in the other two domains. Distinct Maneuverability Regimes of Pursuit Beyond shifting the advantage from predator to prey, the introduction of sensory–motor delays also generates qualitative differences in how pursuit outcomes depend on maneuverability. In terrestrial and aerial domains, decreasing maneuverability (i.e., increasing R e and thus lowering ω e ) leads to a rapid decline in escape performance ( Fig. 4 A ). On average, doubling R e reduces miss distance by roughly eightfold in aerial predator–prey pairs and by about 16-fold in terrestrial ones. In aquatic systems, the pattern reverses: miss distance increases with increasing R e . While the magnitude of this effect varies among aquatic predator–prey pairs, on average, aquatic prey could nearly double their miss distance by turning with radii three to four times greater than their predicted minimum. To understand these contrasting patterns, we examined how miss distance varies with prey turning radius across domains ( Fig. 4 B ). In nearly all aquatic predator–prey pairs, the miss distance equals 2 R e , the maximum possible separation predicted by the turning-gambit framework. In contrast, in terrestrial and aerial systems it remains substantially below this limit. This difference arises because most aquatic prey can complete a full 180° turn before their predators begin to respond ( ω e τ p &gt; π ), whereas terrestrial and aerial prey cannot. This threshold divides pursuit–evasion dynamics into two maneuverability regimes: a high-maneuverability regime, in which prey achieve the theoretical maximum miss distance, and a low-maneuverability regime, in which predators begin responding before the prey completes its turn ( Fig. 4 C ). As long as ω e τ p &gt; π , prey can further increase their miss distance by widening their turns up to the point at which they can no longer complete the full rotation before the predator reacts. Even after prey can no longer complete a 180° turn within the predator’s delay ( ω e τ p &lt; π ), miss distance can continue to increase slightly with further increases in R e because a wider turn carries the prey farther away from the predator’s initial trajectory before the predator begins to turn ( Fig. 4 D ). In this range, the lateral displacement gained from a wider turn initially outweighs the cost of giving the predator more time to respond. With further increases in R e , however, the predator’s response increasingly erodes this advantage, and miss distance begins to decline ( Fig. 4 D ). Thus most aquatic prey operate in a high-maneuverability regime, where further increases in maneuverability do not improve escape performance. Instead, aquatic prey can benefit by turning less sharply than they are capable of. Terrestrial and aerial prey, by contrast, occupy the low-maneuverability regime, where predators respond before the prey completes its turn and escape performance is strongly limited by maneuverability. Discussion Our analysis reveals that the original formulation of the turning gambit, when combined with allometric scaling relationships, fails to capture key patterns observed in empirical data on capture success rates. While the model predicts a consistent predator advantage across domains, empirical data show that prey, on average, escape more often than they are captured. Moreover, the model suggests that capture should be easiest for aquatic predators, followed by aerial and then terrestrial predators, whereas empirical data show the exact opposite pattern. These discrepancies suggest that the biomechanical trade-off between turning radius and speed alone, as formalized in the turning gambit framework, is insufficient to explain pursuit–evasion dynamics. A key assumption of the original turning gambit is that predators can respond instantaneously to prey maneuvers. In reality, neural processing and transmission delays—known as sensory–motor delays—mean that predators can only perceive and respond to prey maneuvers after a delay ( 16 , 70 – 74 ). Although these delays are relatively short, on the order of 20 to 200 ms, they have profound consequences for pursuit–evasion interactions ( 70 ). Across all three domains, incorporating predator sensory–motor delays into the model consistently shifts the advantage toward the prey, a prediction more consistent with the generally low capture success observed across predator–prey pairs. Beyond tipping the scales in favor of prey, sensory–motor delays interact with domain-specific differences in maneuverability to create distinct pursuit–evasion regimes. Because water is roughly three orders of magnitude denser than air, aquatic animals tend to have lower speeds but can execute the sharpest turns, making them the most maneuverable across all domains. Most prey in this domain are predicted to be able to complete a full 180° turn within the predator’s sensory–motor delay, enabling large directional changes before the predator can react. By contrast, aerial and terrestrial prey typically rotate less than 45° during the same interval, with a median of about 10°. Although our analysis focuses on vertebrates, many flying insects achieve turning rates comparable to those of aquatic animals and may therefore operate within or near the high-maneuverability regime ( 12 , 75 ). Pursuit–evasion dynamics among flying insects may thus resemble those of aquatic predators and prey more closely than those of vertebrates in air or on land. The substantial domain-specific variation in maneuverability, in combination with predator sensory-motor delays, leads to three major consequences. First, because most aquatic predators are highly maneuverable themselves, they can rapidly adjust their course once the delay has elapsed, leaving prey with much narrower time windows in which they can outmaneuver them ( 6 ). Under a 50 ms predator delay, the model predicts that most aquatic prey have escape windows shorter than 100 ms (and the smallest prey around 50 ms) compared with median windows of ∼ 1 s in terrestrial and aerial systems. Consequently, aquatic prey must time their evasive maneuvers with roughly an order of magnitude greater precision than prey in the other two domains. Consistent with this requirement, many teleost fishes possess a specialized escape circuit (the Mauthner cell system) that can initiate responses with exceptionally fast and precise timing ( 76 , 77 ). This suggests that aquatic prey may indeed be capable of triggering escapes with the predicted precision necessary to evade predators despite their narrow escape windows. Second, in both aerial and terrestrial systems, escape outcomes are highly sensitive to differences in maneuverability: small reductions in turning performance lead to large reductions in miss distance. In the aquatic domain, the opposite holds true. Most aquatic prey already reach the theoretical maximum miss distance of twice their turning radius by completing a full turn during the predator’s delay. Sharper turns reduce this distance, whereas wider evasive turns can increase it. This counterintuitive effect leads to a clear, testable prediction: in aquatic systems, prey should employ wider evasive turns than their biomechanical limits would otherwise allow. This is especially important because, although aquatic prey can execute tight turns, sufficiently large turning radii may be required to generate the lateral displacement needed to flank the mouth of an approaching predator. Finally, the high maneuverability of aquatic prey affects not only the geometric feasibility of interception, but also the predator’s ability to generate a controlled interception response. The turning gambit evaluates only whether capture is biomechanically possible (whether the prey can escape the set of all feasible predator trajectories), whereas real predators must commit to a single interception path. To guide these interception paths, predators across a broad range of taxa appear to rely on simple control strategies that use the perceived relative position and motion of the prey to guide their turns ( 78 – 81 ). In aquatic systems, rapid prey turns force predators into equally rapid course changes; when combined with sensory–motor delays, these changes can destabilize feedback control and lead to interception failure ( 70 , 74 ). Such instability is a general property of feedback-controlled systems facing rapidly changing but delayed inputs ( 82 ), and has been demonstrated in models of predator–prey interactions parameterized with empirical aquatic locomotion data ( 70 ). In contrast, our model predicts that vertebrate prey in terrestrial and aerial domains undergo far smaller directional changes during the predator’s delay, suggesting that predators in these domains are less susceptible to delay-induced instability. As a result, compensating for delays, for example, by predicting a prey’s current position from delayed sensory input, may be especially important for aquatic predators tracking highly maneuverable prey ( 74 ). Beyond sensory–motor constraints, the turning gambit simplifies pursuit dynamics by representing animals as point masses that move at constant speed and execute turns with a fixed minimum radius. Real predators and prey can violate these assumptions in several ways. They can bend their bodies, reorient control surfaces, and accelerate or decelerate during maneuvers ( 15 , 25 , 83 – 86 ). In aquatic and aerial systems, rapid turning often incurs speed loss due to increased drag, whereas in terrestrial systems turning radius depends directly on velocity, creating an intrinsic speed–maneuverability trade-off ( 87 ). Prey may also violate the assumption of planar motion by executing fully three-dimensional turns, in which direction-dependent differences in maneuverability can confer advantages along different axes of motion ( 88 ). In addition to these biomechanical deviations, predators and prey may employ behavioral strategies that alter pursuit outcomes beyond the geometric framework considered here, including cooperative hunting, endurance-based chases, or gravity-assisted maneuvers ( 3 , 13 , 89 – 94 ). Moreover, predators may pursue prey that move in a different locomotor domain, such as aerial predators attacking terrestrial prey ( 95 , 96 ). In these cross-domain interactions, predator and prey no longer share the same allometric scaling of biomechanical constraints and may move in different planes, fundamentally altering the geometry of interception. Extending the turning gambit to such scenarios, therefore, requires a distinct treatment and represents an important direction for future work. Despite these departures from the model’s assumptions, limited empirical evidence suggests that the turning gambit still captures first-order determinants of escape success. In bat–moth interactions, for example, once sensory–motor delays and empirically measured average turning speeds were incorporated, the model discriminated between successful and unsuccessful escapes even under clear violations of constant-speed and planar-motion assumptions ( 12 ). This suggests that, although simplified, the turning gambit may still serve as a useful first-order approximation for understanding pursuit–evasion outcomes. In conclusion, although pursuit and evasion dynamics are governed by conserved underlying principles, they unfold within distinct biomechanical regimes where different traits become more or less influential, and across spatial and temporal scales that differ by orders of magnitude. Counterintuitively, while aquatic animals are the slowest among the three domains, their high maneuverability causes interactions to unfold over the shortest timescales, creating especially challenging conditions for both predator and prey. Our model provides a step toward explaining the striking variation in capture success observed both among and within domains. Integrating this framework with high-resolution data on natural encounters will enable direct tests of its predictions and advance a predictive theory of pursuit and escape in nature. Materials and Methods The Turning Gambit Model. Original model. In the original formulation of the turning–gambit model ( 11 ), the predator and prey both travel at constant speed and with fixed turning radii. The prey begins its evasive turn at an initiation distance X 0 directly in front of the predator. Its trajectory is given by [7] X → e ( t ) = X 0 + R e sin ( ω e t ) R e − R e cos ( ω e t ) , where R e is the prey’s turning radius and ω e = V e / R e its turning rate with speed V e . The predator immediately turns toward the prey with a turning radius R p , speed V p , and the resulting turning rate ω p = V p / R p . Its trajectory is given by [8] X → p ( t ) = R p sin ( ω p t ) R p − R p cos ( ω p t ) . Adjusted model. We modify the original model to prevent the prey from unrealistically continuing to rotate indefinitely. Specifically, once the prey completes a 180 ° turn, we constrain it to continue in a straight line at constant speed V e . Thus, for t &lt; π / ω e the prey follows a circular arc of radius R e ; for t ≥ π / ω e it moves straight in the direction opposite its initial heading. The prey’s trajectory becomes [9] X → e ( t ) = ( X 0 + R e sin ( ω e t ) ) 1 1 t &lt; π ω e + ( X 0 − V e ( t − π ω e ) ) 1 1 t ≥ π ω e ( R e − R e cos ( ω e t ) ) 1 1 t &lt; π ω e + 2 R e 1 1 t ≥ π ω e , where 1 denotes the indicator function, which is equal to 1 when the condition in the subscript is met, otherwise it is zero. To incorporate the predator’s sensory–motor delay, we assume that the predator continues to travel in a straight line at speed V p for a duration τ p . After this delay, it begins its turn with a turning radius R p and a turning rate of ω p = V p / R p again. The predator’s trajectory becomes [10] X → p ( t ) = V p t 1 t &lt; τ p + V p τ p + R p sin ( ω p ( t − τ p ) ) 1 t ≥ τ p R p − R p cos ( ω p ( t − τ p ) ) 1 t ≥ τ p . Computation of the miss distance. To compute the miss distance, we use numerical optimization to find the values of R p and V p that minimize the minimum distance between the predator and prey during evasion. We define the predator-to-prey distance as [11] D ( t ) = ‖ X → p ( t ) − X → e ( t ) ‖ 2 . For a given initiation distance X 0 , we allow the predator to increase its turning radius, reduce its speed, or do both. We then determine the combination that minimizes the minimal predator–prey separation: [12] arg min R p , V p min t ∈ [ 0 , T max ] D ( t ) subject to R p ≥ R p 0 , V p ≤ V p 0 , X e x ( 0 ) &gt; X p x ( 0 ) , where R p 0 and V p 0 denote the predator’s minimum turning radius and maximum speed, respectively. The constraint X e x ( 0 ) &gt; X p x ( 0 ) makes sure that the prey starts in front of the predator. We define T max as the time at which the prey and predator have the same x-position, X e x ( t ) = X p x ( t ) . This ensures that we evaluate whether the prey can reach the safe zone—i.e., pass into the region where X e x ( t ) &lt; X p x ( t ) —without being intercepted. The minimal predator–prey separation over this interval is taken as the miss distance. A miss distance of zero indicates that the predator successfully intercepts the prey.
