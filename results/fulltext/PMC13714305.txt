Introduction Fishes are the most diverse vertebrate group, occupying numerous aquatic niches that vary in temperature, pressure, salinity, oxygen and light intensity 1 . Discrete aquatic light habitats include the epipelagic (sunlight) zone with intense, broad-spectrum light, the mesopelagic (twilight) zone with low-intensity, medium-wavelength light, and the abyssopelagic (abyssal) zone devoid of sunlight 2 . Consequently, vision in fish has evolved under strong selective pressures, yielding various anatomical innovations tailored to specific light regimes 3 . Many visual adaptations relate to the retina, including ultraviolet-sensitive photoreceptors in many reef-associated lineages 4 , specialisations for aerial vision in various surface-dwelling species 5 and a multibank retina and retinal diverticula in numerous deep-sea species 6 , 7 . Sharks (Selachii) have only one type of cone photoreceptor and are considered colour blind 8 , with many showing adaptations that maximise visual sensitivity (i.e. the ability to detect light) and contrast (i.e. the ability to detect differences in brightness). By comparison, bony fishes (Osteichthyes) can possess up to four cone types and vary widely in visual acuity (i.e. the sharpness of visual detail) and sensitivity 9 . Light modulation mechanisms also differ among major taxonomic groups. Cartilaginous fish (Chondrichthyes) predominantly employ pupillary light reflexes (PLRs, i.e. changes in pupil size), whereas ray-finned fish (Actinopterygii) typically rely on ocular filters 10 and retinomotor movements (RMMs, i.e. active repositioning of photoreceptors and pigment within the retina) 11 . RMMs protect photoreceptors from excess light exposure under changing ambient illumination 12 , consistent with an alternative evolutionary solution to PLRs in fluctuating light environments and underscoring divergent visual adaptations in Actinopterygii and Chondrichthyes. Complementing these retinal mechanisms is melanin 13 , a widespread pigment packaged into organelles called melanosomes. In skin and internal tissues, melanin functions in photoprotection 14 , immunity 15 and metal homeostasis 16 , while in eye tissues it also reduces light scattering and shields photoreceptors from excess illumination 17 , 18 . Melanosome geometry varies among tissues 19 ; the functional implications of this variation are only partially resolved, and the physiological and ecological controls on melanosome geometry in fish have not been systematically investigated. To address this, we examine melanosomes from the eye, skin and internal tissues of jawless (Agnatha), cartilaginous (Chondrichthyes), ray-finned (Actinopterygii) and lobe-finned (Sarcopterygii) fishes (Supplementary Tables 1 and 2 ). Our data reveal lineage- and tissue-specific variation in melanosome size and shape reflecting ecological adaptations and functional specialisations (Fig. 1 and Supplementary Fig. 1 ). By integrating these data with melanosome measurements from fossil specimens (Supplementary Fig. 2 and Supplementary Table 3 ), we demonstrate that patterns of variation in melanosome geometry in extant fish have deep evolutionary origins. Results Anatomical distribution of melanosomes in extant fishes Enzymatic digestion of various internal and external tissues of extant fishes yielded black to dark brown melanosome extracts. The eyes and skin are melanised in all species, but none show melanosomes in the brain, gonads or heart (Fig. 1 and Supplementary Fig. 1 , Supplementary Table 2 ). The European river lamprey (hereafter river lamprey, Agnatha) exhibits the fewest melanosome-bearing tissue extracts, with melanosomes observed only in the eyes, skin and olfactory sac. The West African lungfish (hereafter African lungfish, Sarcopterygii) is the most heavily melanised taxon, with melanosomes in the gills, eyes, kidney, liver, lungs, peritoneum, skin, spleen and gut. In the studied Actinopterygii (Atlantic mackerel, European seabass and gilthead seabream, hereafter referred to as mackerel, seabass and seabream, as well as American eel and whiting), melanosomes consistently occur in the eyes, skin, spleen, kidney and, except in seabass, the peritoneum (Fig. 1 and Supplementary Fig. 1 , Supplementary Table 2 ). In contrast, the studied Chondrichthyes (spotted skate, cuckoo skate, blackmouth catshark, small-spotted catshark and rabbitfish) show consistent melanisation only in the eyes and skin. In Chondrichthyes, melanosomes also occur in internal tissues, but their distribution varies among species: they are common in the gills, liver and peritoneum, rare in the gut and spleen, and absent in the kidney. Exceptions include the small-spotted catshark, which lacks melanosomes in the gills and peritoneum, the rabbitfish and blackmouth catshark, which lack melanosomes in the liver, and the spotted skate, which lacks melanosomes in the peritoneum. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images of the spleen, liver and kidney reveal that melanosomes are organised into discrete spheroidal aggregates within melanomacrophage centres (MMCs), with individual aggregates containing tens to tens of thousands of melanosomes (Supplementary Fig. 3 ). MMC melanosomes are spheroidal (spherical to slightly prolate) with minimal variation in morphology. In bony fishes (Osteichthyes; Sarcopterygii and Actinopterygii), cylindrical melanosomes (elongate with rounded ends) are present in the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE), where they are oriented parallel to the photoreceptors (Supplementary Figs. 4 – 6 ). In contrast, cylindrical melanosomes are absent in chondrichthyans and agnathans (Supplementary Figs. 4 , 6 ). Notably, melanosomes within the RPE of Chondrichthyes are substantially reduced or absent. In all groups, spheroidal melanosomes occur in the choroid and iris, but in Chondrichthyes and Sarcopterygii, melanosomes in these tissues are typically stratified into size-specific layers, with larger melanosomes positioned on the vitreal (inner-facing) side. Intraspecific trends in melanosome geometry in extant fishes We quantified intraspecific variation in melanosomes across extant fishes using standard descriptors of melanosome geometry (length, width and aspect ratio) and assessed the significance of differences among tissues using two-sided analysis of variance (ANOVA) and Tukey-adjusted post hoc pairwise comparisons. We scrutinised pairwise comparisons further using Bhattacharyya coefficients (BCs), which quantify overlap between the distributions for each pair. Higher values indicate greater overlap. BCs were assigned to three categories: low (0–0.33), moderate (0.34–0.66), and high (0.67–1) to facilitate interpretation of morphological differentiation among tissues. Within individual fish species, melanosome length and width differ significantly among tissues (ANOVA, p &lt; 0.05; Supplementary Table 4 ). Post-hoc comparisons show that these differences are primarily associated with comparisons involving the eyes (Tukey-adjusted p &lt; 0.05, Fig. 1 and Supplementary Fig. 1 , Supplementary Table 5 ). Among the skin and internal tissues (gills, kidney, liver, peritoneum, spleen and gut), however, pairwise comparisons are almost all non-significant and BCs are high (Tukey-adjusted p &gt; 0.05, BCs = 0.70–0.94; Supplementary Fig. 7 and Supplementary Table 5 ). Exceptions include the blackmouth catshark and seabream, where comparisons of the peritoneum and gills with other tissues are significant (Tukey-adjusted p &lt; 0.05; Supplementary Table 5 ). Despite differences in these taxa, BCs remain moderate to high (BCs = 0.45–0.89; Supplementary Fig. 7 and Supplementary Table 5 ), indicating overlapping morphological distributions and suggesting that differences reflect shifts in the central tendency rather than clear morphological separation. The African lungfish is the most striking exception: melanosome length and width in the skin and lung differ markedly from other tissues (i.e., gills, liver, kidney and peritoneum; Tukey-adjusted p &lt; 0.05; Supplementary Table 5 ). BC values for these comparisons are low to moderate, indicating limited morphological overlap (BCs = 0.31–0.65; Supplementary Fig. 7 and Supplementary Table 5 ). African lungfish melanosomes are smaller in the skin and lung than in other tissues (Supplementary Tables 6 , 7 ). Melanosome aspect ratio also differs significantly among tissues within individual fish species (ANOVA, p &lt; 0.05; Supplementary Table 4 ). Again, post-hoc comparisons reveal that these differences are primarily associated with comparisons involving the eyes (Tukey-adjusted p &lt; 0.05, Fig. 1 , Supplementary Fig. 1 and Supplementary Table 5 ). Pairwise comparisons of melanised tissues in the river lamprey (agnathan) are non-significant and show high BC values, indicating high morphological overlap (Tukey-adjusted p &gt; 0.05, BCs = 0.93–0.96; Supplementary Fig. 7 and Supplementary Table 5 ). In the African lungfish (sarcopterygian), only eye melanosomes differ significantly in aspect ratio from those in other tissues; BCs are low, indicating low morphological overlap (Tukey-adjusted p &lt; 0.05, BCs = 0.18–0.31; Supplementary Table 5 ). Within the examined actinopterygians and chondrichthyans, only eye melanosomes show significant differences in aspect ratio from melanosomes in the skin and internal tissues; BCs are low to moderate, consistent with limited overlap in melanosome geometry between eyes and other tissues (Tukey-adjusted p &lt; 0.05, BCs = 0.11–0.66; Supplementary Tables 4 , 5 ). The blackmouth catshark shows additional significant differences in aspect ratio comparisons involving the gills and peritoneum, while the seabream shows additional significant differences in aspect ratio comparisons involving the peritoneum (Tukey-adjusted p &lt; 0.05; Supplementary Table 5 ). Differences in aspect ratio for the skin and internal tissues (among gills, liver, kidney, spleen and gut) are not significant for Actinopterygii and Chondrichthyes; BCs are typically high, indicating pronounced morphological overlap (Tukey-adjusted p &gt; 0.05, BCs = 0.74–0.98; Supplementary Fig. 7 and Supplementary Table 5 ). Discussion Our data reveal major trends in fish melanosome geometry that relate to tissue type and phylogeny. Collectively, these trends confirm step changes in melanosome geometry at key physiological, anatomical and ecological transitions of the fish phylogeny, discussed below for the major tissue types analysed. To date, analyses of fish tissues have only provided direct evidence for eumelanin 21 , 22 , while reports of pheomelanin 23 – 27 have been debated in the absence of direct chemical evidence 28 . Melanin composition was not analysed here, but the dark colour of melanosome extracts in our dataset is consistent with eumelanin-rich melanosomes. These observations are not diagnostic and thus melanosome chemistry is not discussed further. Previous ToF-SIMS analyses of melanosomes from Mazon Creek fossils are consistent with eumelanin 29 . Melanosome geometry in the internal tissues of extant fish Internal tissues in all extant species studied here are characterised by small, spheroidal (spherical to slightly prolate) melanosomes (Figs. 1 , 2 and Supplementary Figs. 1 , 7 , 14 ). This may reflect optimisation of melanosome geometry under strong functional constraints during early fish evolution, with this geometry retained as a plesiomorphic feature in all groups. Internal melanin in vertebrates is linked to metal sequestration and scavenging of harmful reactive oxygen species (ROS) 30 . In fish, melanin accumulates within the melanomacrophage centres (MMCs) of the liver, spleen, kidney and gills (Supplementary Fig. 3 ) in response to stress-related stimuli 31 ; such functions likely benefit from the high surface-area-to-volume ratio of small, spheroidal melanosomes. Whether these melanosomes accumulate in MMCs through phagocytosis or are synthesised in situ as part of a regulatory response is debated 15 . African lungfish (Sarcopterygii) show melanisation in more internal tissues and greater variability in melanosome geometry than all other species studied (Fig. 2 and Supplementary Figs. 7 , 14 ). Extensive internal pigmentation has also been reported for Protopterus aethiopicus , another species of African lungfish 32 . Given that internal organs in lungfish are largely shielded from direct ambient light, expanded melanosome morphospace in African lungfish internal tissues is more consistent with pervasive differences in physiology rather than differences in light exposure 33 . Further, the high abundance of internal melanin in lungfish is unexpected: in deep-sea fishes, gut and peritoneal melanin is suggested to mask light from ingested bioluminescent prey as camouflage 34 , whereas the shallow freshwater ecology of lungfish suggests an alternative function. We propose that the strong internal tissue melanisation in African lungfish relates to aestivation, which in lungfish involves terrestrialisation and prolonged metabolic depression 35 . Upon arousal, reoxygenation generates large amounts of ROS that can cause oxidative stress and tissue damage 35 . African lungfish exhibit unique immunological adaptations to counteract this physiological stress, including large granulocyte populations 36 (which scavenge ROS) 14 , 15 and, during aestivation, upregulation of antioxidant defence 37 and increased tissue pigmentation 36 . Extensive internal melanisation in lungfish may therefore buffer tissues against oxidative stress, raising the intriguing possibility that melanisation of internal organs, by facilitating aestivation, may have played a crucial role in enabling vertebrates to withstand the physiological challenges of transitioning onto land. Expanded melanosome morphospace in lungfish may be a pleiotropic effect of the above and other changes in the melanocortin system 19 . The role of internal melanin in physiological stress responses is conserved across ectothermic vertebrates 38 . There is also evidence that internal melanin is associated with adaptations to seasonal temperature variation. In some amphibians, increased liver melanisation during seasonal dormancy mitigates low environmental temperatures by leveraging the ability of melanin to absorb energy from photons and convert it to heat 14 , 39 , 40 . In African lungfish, however, aestivation occurs in response to drought conditions driven by high temperatures 35 . The extensive internal melanisation in African lungfish is therefore most plausibly explained by ROS-related physiological stress and not as an adaptation to low ambient temperatures. The lack of internal melanisation in sarcopterygian fossils, relative to other groups (Fig. 5 and Supplementary Figs. 11 – 13 ), indicates little pigmentation in vivo, likely reflecting the estuarine setting 41 . In these permanently aquatic habitats, selective pressure for aestivation would have been lower than in extant sarcopterygians inhabiting seasonally dry environments. Limited sampling and preservation bias in the available fossil material, however, cannot be ruled out. Melanosome geometry in the skin of extant fish Superimposed on these broad taxonomic trends are tissue-specific variations in melanosome geometry. In African lungfish, despite pronounced variation in internal tissue melanosome morphology relative to other taxa, skin melanosomes exhibit comparatively limited geometric variation. (Supplementary Fig. 7 ). This indicates tight functional constraints in skin tissues, likely driven by light-related selection pressures. Further, skin melanosomes are more spherical in Sarcopterygii and Actinopterygii than in Chondrichthyes and Agnatha (Supplementary Table 7 ). This, in addition to the size 42 and packing density of melanosomes in skin tissues 43 , provides additional evidence that melanosome size and shape modulate light absorbance and scattering in fish, optimising the optical properties of skin in different species for different lighting regimes. There is no obvious link between the aspect ratio of skin melanosomes and physiological colour change 44 , 45 ; evidence for the latter in several of the studied taxa is limited, preventing assessment of this in our dataset. In humans, there is evidence that the size of hair melanosomes increases with age, reflecting turnover and replacement by progressively larger melanosomes 46 . There is no evidence for such age-related changes in melanosome size in any tissue in fish. All specimens examined here are sexually mature, minimising potential age-related variation among taxa. Further, to our knowledge, no studies have reported systematic changes in melanosome geometry in response to environmental pH or temperature. As the reported patterns are consistent across groups, these reflect true taxonomic signals rather than a methodological or sampling bias. Melanosome geometry in the eyes of extant fish Our data show strong lineage-specific patterns in fish eye melanosome geometry (Figs. 1 – 4 ). In actinopterygians and sarcopterygians (bony fish), the eye contains two distinct melanosome populations discriminated by aspect ratio: cylindrical and spheroidal. In contrast, chondrichthyans and agnathans possess only spheroidal melanosomes (Fig. 4 and Supplementary Figs. 4 , 8 ). These taxonomic differences reflect group-specific visual adaptations. Vertebrates exhibit diverse retinal architectures that balance sensitivity and acuity to meet specific ecological demands, with optimisation of one aspect of visual performance often resulting in trade-offs for others 3 . Species adapted to low light levels typically prioritise sensitivity over acuity, using low ganglion cell densities, high summation (convergence) ratios, high rod-to-cone ratios and/or a tapetum lucidum to maximise photon capture and light detection 3 . We propose that melanosome geometry plays a key role in regulating light and enhancing visual performance, particularly across variable light environments. In such environments, dynamic control of retinal light absorption relies on pupillary light reflexes (PLR) and retinomotor movements (RMM) 11 . RMMs involve positional adjustment of photoreceptors and melanosomes within the RPE in response to ambient light levels 12 . In bright conditions, rod inner segments extend, pushing the light-sensitive rod outer segments deep into the RPE, while melanosomes migrate apically to surround and shield the outer segments from excess light. Simultaneously, cone inner segments retract, pulling cone outer segments away from the RPE for optimal visual acuity and photoprotection of rods. Under low light, these processes reverse, maximising sensitivity 3 , 12 (Supplementary Fig. 6 ). Among extant vertebrates, bony fish display the most extensive and widely distributed RMMs 12 , with RMMs in teleosts thought to compensate for the absence of a PLR 47 ; these light regulation mechanisms are not mutually exclusive 11 , 47 . PLR-bearing teleosts are typically bottom-dwellers that use PLRs for camouflage rather than visual adaptation when immersed in the substrate to reduce the contrast between the pupil and surrounding tissue/sediment 11 . RMMs are inherently slow (typically lasting several hours), limiting their effectiveness under rapidly changing light conditions 48 , 49 . PLRs, however, require only milliseconds to seconds in certain taxa 11 . Further, PLRs are often coupled with dynamic pupil shapes, enabling control of directional light and depth of field adjustments 50 , while most bony fishes retain near-round pupils that constrict less efficiently than slit- or crescent-shaped pupils 11 . In Actinopterygii, melanosome geometry has been linked to RMM function 17 , whereby only cylindrical melanosomes enter the apical processes of RPE cells 17 (Supplementary Figs. 4 – 6 ). The controls on the formation of elongate melanosomes are thus of particular interest. PMEL (premelanosome protein) is a highly conserved component of melanosome biogenesis in vertebrates and plays a key structural role in the formation of an organised fibrillar scaffold that is, in turn, required for melanosome elongation 51 – 53 . Inactivation of PMEL in vertebrates prevents the formation of elongate melanosomes 52 . Further, knockdown of PMELa in zebrafish eye tissues results in the loss of melanosome transport into apical processes 17 . The high surface-area-to-volume ratio and reduced cross-sectional area of cylindrical melanosomes are thus thought to enhance melanosome transport by molecular motors through the narrow (&lt;1 µm wide) apical projections 17 . Additionally, the orientation of cylindrical melanosomes parallel to photoreceptors appears to optimise incident light capture and minimise lateral scattering, enhancing overall visual contrast and acuity 17 , 18 , 42 . RMMs have not been reported in Chondrichthyes, consistent with their well-developed PLRs (excluding some deep-sea sharks 8 ). The absence of cylindrical melanosomes in Chondrichthyes and the reduction/loss of melanosomes within the RPE (Supplementary Fig. 4 ) 8 , 54 – 56 , therefore, provide new evidence that RMMs are reduced or absent in this group. Given that cylindrical melanosomes may reduce lateral light scatter and enhance acuity 17 , their absence in the retina of Chondrichthyes may be a low-light adaptation, supporting the hypothesis that eye melanosome geometry is linked to lineage-specific visual adaptations. These retinal melanosomes would occlude the tapetum lucidum, impeding light reflection back to photoreceptors and thereby reducing visual sensitivity in these taxa. Certain chondrichthyans regulate incident light via migration of choroidal melanosomes. Despite this, they retain spheroidal melanosomes. In bright conditions, melanosomes can migrate through the dendritic processes of choroidal melanocytes to occlude the tapetum lucidum and reduce intraocular light scatter 57 – 59 . Conversely, in dim light, melanosomes retract toward the cell body, exposing the reflective tapetum and enhancing photon capture by retinal photoreceptors 57 , 58 . Notably, this occurs in few species adapted to brighter conditions; the chondrichthyans studied here are not known to exhibit choroidal melanosome migration, reflecting nocturnal and deep-water behaviour, where visual sensitivity is prioritised 8 . Since the choroid lies sclerad (outward) to the photoreceptors and thus cannot shield them from incoming light, selective pressure to minimise lateral scatter is likely weaker than in the RPE. Additionally, the spacing of guanine crystals may be less tightly constrained than photoreceptor organisation within the retina, imposing fewer geometric constraints on melanosome packing. Consequently, choroidal melanosomes are presumably less constrained by the selective pressures that favour melanosome elongation in the RPE. Lampreys exhibit RMMs and a PLR 60 , 61 , although both are considered rudimentary. These RMMs appear to lack melanosome migration and involve myoid elongation in only one photoreceptor type (rod-like short photoreceptor cells 60 ), unlike Osteichthyes, where both rod and cone photoreceptors participate 12 . Additionally, the lamprey PLR is mediated solely by melanopsin, independent of photoreceptor input, representing a simpler visual system that predates more complex gnathostome vision 61 . In combination with nocturnality 62 , 63 and a reliance on electroreception 64 , lamprey vision appears biased towards sensitivity rather than acuity. Taken together, this rudimentary visual physiology is inferred to have imposed minimal selective pressure for melanosome elongation, explaining the simple spherical to slightly prolate geometry of lamprey eye melanosomes, similar to those within their internal tissues (Figs. 1 – 3 ). Previous studies of the vertebrate eye have indicated that iris pigment epithelium (IPE) and choroidal melanosomes are typically small, spheroidal 65 and confined to a single layer. Our data, however, reveal that in the examined Chondrichthyes and Sarcopterygii 66 , melanosomes in choroidal and IPE tissues are typically stratified into immobile, size-specific layers, with larger melanosomes positioned on the vitreal (inner-facing) side (Supplementary Fig. 4 ). This size stratification may modulate light entry and/or intraocular scattering: optimal melanosome sizes for maximum light absorption have been characterised in deep-sea fishes 43 and numerical modelling indicates that variation in melanosome morphology can alter light scattering and absorption properties 42 . Given the high metabolic and oxidative activity of the choroid 67 , it is likely that choroidal melanosomes contribute to ROS scavenging, but it is not clear how size-based stratification would directly enhance this function. Melanosome geometry in the eyes of fossil fish We analysed eye melanosomes preserved in a range of fossil fishes from the Mazon Creek biota (Carboniferous, USA; Fig. 5 and Supplementary Figs. 2 , 11 – 13 ). Remarkably, trends in melanosome geometry in the fossil specimens closely mirror those in extant fishes (Figs. 3 and 4 , Supplementary Fig. 10 ). As in extant agnathans 20 , the fossil agnathans possess spheroidal melanosomes with minimal variation in geometry. The fossil chondrichthyans show spheroidal melanosomes of varying size, consistent with the eye melanosome size-based stratification in extant chondrichthyans. The fossil sarcopterygians and actinopterygians show spheroidal to highly elongate, cylindrical melanosomes. These data confirm that the broad taxonomic trends in melanosome geometry identified in extant fishes are deeply conserved and originated at least 306 million years ago. This, in turn, indicates strong conservation of melanosome function across the major fish groups examined. As ocular melanosomes have been identified in early vertebrate eye fossils dating back to the Cambrian 68 , these data provide an avenue for extending phylogenetic analyses deeper into vertebrate evolutionary history. The lack of significant differences in melanosome size in all fossil groups relative to their extant counterparts (Fig. 3 and Supplementary Fig. 10 ) is unexpected, given that melanosomes have been shown to shrink during thermal maturation 69 . This raises the possibility that fossil melanosomes were larger in vivo. In humans, eye melanosomes persist throughout life with little to no regeneration 70 ; although melanin content decreases with age 71 , melanosome geometry does not change 72 . No comparable data exist for extant fish, but ontogenetic differences are unlikely to explain the possibility of larger in vivo melanosome sizes in the Mazon Creek fishes, as this would require a consistent age bias between fossil and extant taxa, for which there is no ecological or taphonomic basis. If Mazon Creek eye melanosomes were indeed larger in vivo, this may reflect experimentation in early gnathostome visual systems, with subsequent reductions in melanosome size potentially reflecting optimisation for visual function, although the mechanistic basis for such changes remains unresolved. This dataset provides a framework for classifying fossil fishes that preserve melanosomes and for evolutionary reconstructions of early vertebrate visual ecology. Building on previous qualitative observations of cylindrical and spheroidal eye melanosomes 29 , our approach establishes quantitative analyses of melanosome geometry as a source of taxonomic and ecological information that complements, and is particularly useful, in the absence of traditional anatomical characters. Evolutionary Implications Melanosome geometry in the internal tissues of fishes is highly constrained. With the exception of African lungfish, the internal tissues of fishes from all major groups examined consistently show spheroidal melanosomes with limited variation, indicating that melanosome form is inherently stable across major fish groups in the absence of external light-related selective pressures (e.g., ultraviolet radiation or fluctuating light; Fig. 2 , Supplementary Figs. 1 , 3 and 7 ). This strongly conserved geometry suggests an ancestral vertebrate condition, providing a baseline against which differences in geometry can be assessed. In tissues exposed to light, i.e. eyes and skin 43 , melanosome geometry is more variable among species and eye melanosomes show a strong taxonomic signature for major fish groups (Figs. 1 – 3 ). This taxonomic control is best explained by broad ecological differences among major taxonomic groups and indicates adaptations of melanosome geometry in response to selective pressures linked to ambient light levels. Consideration of the ancestral state for vertebrate vision provides critical context for interpreting the evolution of eye melanosomes 73 . The lamprey eye demonstrates that camera-style vision predates the agnathan-gnathostome split 74 , 75 . Slight variation in melanosome geometry from spherical to prolate spheroids 20 (Fig. 3 ), together with the presence of pmel orthologs in agnathans 51 , suggests that experimentation in melanosome elongation began in early vertebrate evolution. The absence of more elongate cylindrical melanosomes in both fossil and extant agnathans (Figs. 3 , 4 ), however, indicates that these more extreme melanosome geometries evolved later, likely driven by ecological demand for enhanced visual performance 20 . The low diversity of melanosome morphologies in agnathans is therefore considered to represent an ancestral condition, consistent with the simplified light-adaptation strategies of the agnathan eye 60 , 61 . Our data indicate that cylindrical melanosomes most parsimoniously represent an ancestral condition of gnathostomes, present in actinopterygians, sarcopterygians and stem chondrichthyans (acanthodians), but subsequently lost in crown chondrichthyans (Supplementary Fig. 10 ). The latter transition may reflect a shift towards visual sensitivity and alternative light-regulatory mechanisms, particularly PLRs (a faster, more flexible light-regulation mechanism than RMMs). These findings imply that complex retinal adaptations (i.e., PLRs and RMMs) were already established by 306 Ma and that RMMs may have been present in the gnathostome ancestor in the Late Ordovician (ca. 445 Ma) 76 , 77 . When cylindrical melanosomes were lost in Chondrichthyes is less clear, but their absence in all three major extant groups (chimaeras, sharks and rays/skates) suggests a single event at, or close to, the origin of Chondrichthyes in the Early Silurian. Collectively, these findings indicate that taxonomic trends in melanosome geometry in fish have deep evolutionary origins. The widespread nature of cylindrical melanosomes in fossil Actinopterygii, Sarcopterygii and stem Chondrichthyes (Acanthodii) suggests that RMMs and acuity-related adaptations were the dominant light-regulatory strategies of early gnathostomes. In crown chondrichthyans, however, RMMs were likely reduced or lost as faster, more versatile PLR mechanisms evolved for light regulation 11 , alongside a reduced reliance on melanosomes for intracellular light shielding and increased dependence on sensitivity-enhancing adaptations such as the tapetum lucidum 8 , 56 , 78 . This may reflect behavioural changes favouring visual sensitivity and/or ecological expansion into habitats with steep light gradients, in which frequent and rapid vertical movements favoured faster retinal adjustment. In contrast, continued use of RMMs in Actinopterygii is consistent with a greater reliance on acuity-based vision and adaptation to more predictable or gradual changes in ambient light, such as diel transitions, vertical migrations, seasonal changes and occupation of shallow-water environments 79 . This is supported by the high behavioural plasticity (especially in diel activity patterns) of some extant teleosts in response to increased predation 80 . RMMs and PLRs thus represent alternative visual strategies that balance visual sensitivity, acuity and response speed to changing ambient light levels under selective pressures including social signalling, prey detection and mate recognition 81 . The replacement of RMMs with PLRs in Chondrichthyes represents a pivotal shift from slower, cellular modes of photoprotection to rapid, neural-based reflexes, a transition that likely occurred convergently in multiple vertebrate lineages and that underpins the sophisticated visual systems in modern species. Methods Modern specimens In order to test for variation in melanosome geometry across the phylogenetic tree of fish, we identified the following major groupings: Agnatha (jawless fish), Chondrichthyes (cartilaginous fish), Actinopterygii (ray-finned fish) and Sarcopterygii (lobe-finned fish). Groups were selected to correspond with the major phylogenetic groups represented by the fossil taxa. Within these groups, extant fish species were selected to capture representation across different orders, although sampling was constrained by species availability. The taxon phylogeny (Fig. 1a ) was constructed following the relationships described in the current literature 82 – 84 . Thirty-six sexually mature fish specimens (three biological replicates of each of 12 species) were used for enzymatic melanin extraction (Supplementary Table 1 ). Specimens of Chimaera monstrosa (rabbit fish; Chondrichthyes), Galeus melastomus (blackmouth catshark; Chondrichthyes), Raja montagui (spotted skate; Chondrichthyes) and Leucoraja naevus (cuckoo skate; Chondrichthyes) were caught by the Irish Marine Institute in International Council for the Exploration of the Sea (ICES) subareas 6 (division a, in waters south of 58°N) and 7 (divisions b, c, j and k, in waters west of 8°W). Specimens were frozen at sea, defrosted immediately after transport to the laboratory, and dissected. Lampetra fluviatilis (European river lamprey; Agnatha), Scyliorhinus canicula (small-spotted catshark; Chondrichthyes) and Anguilla rostrata (American eel; Actinopterygii) were purchased online from UK-based commercial suppliers (Gerry’s Fishing, Darwin Biological and Bradley’s Fish, respectively). Specimens were supplied frozen and dissected immediately after defrosting. Merlangius merlangus (whiting; Actinopterygii), Scomber scombrus (Atlantic mackerel; Actinopterygii), Dicentrarchus labrax (European seabass; Actinopterygii) and Sparus aurata (gilthead seabream; Actinopterygii) were purchased freshly deceased from local suppliers (K O’Connell’s Fishmongers, English Market, Cork). Specimens were dissected within 24 h after death. Specimens of Protopterus annectens (West African lungfish, Sarcopterygii) were supplied live by Sims Tropical Fish (UK), transported to Ireland by car, euthanised by cranial concussion on arrival at the laboratory and dissected within 12 h. All research reported here was conducted in accordance with the relevant ethical regulations and guidelines of University College Cork (UCC). African lungfish specimens were imported to Ireland from the UK for scientific purposes under a Movement Approval Notice issued by the Marine Institute Fish Health Unit (OIMP-3346), with entry documented by a Common Health Entry Document for Animals (CHED-A; CHEDA.IE.2023.0004216). Euthanasia was approved by the UCC Animal Experimentation Ethics Committee (approval number: UCC-AEEC-23/001; amendment 10/07/2023) and was performed by Luke Harman (School of BEES; HPRA authorisation AE19130/I087). Wild-caught fish specimens were provided to D.C. by the Marine Institute following collection during the 2023 Irish Anglerfish and Megrim Survey (IAMS; cruise CE23003) 85 , conducted by the Marine Institute in fulfilment of Ireland’s obligations under the Common Fisheries Policy (CFP). Fossil specimens Fossil specimens from different localities inherently differ in their taphonomic history. To minimise potential taphonomic bias among fossil biotas, such as potential melanosome shrinkage during thermal maturation 69 , we limited our fossil dataset to the Mazon Creek Lagerstätte (~306–311 Ma; Moscovian stage, late Carboniferous 86 ). This biota was selected as it includes representatives of all four major groups of fish examined in our study (Agnatha, Chondrichthyes, Sarcopterygii and Actinopterygii), plus the extinct stem chondrichthyan group Acanthodii. We examined melanosomes preserved in the eye region of twenty fossil specimens from Mazon Creek, housed in the collections of the Field Museum of Natural History (FMNH), Chicago, Illinois (Supplementary Table 3 ). Fossil specimens were accessed through a formal loan from the FMNH (2 May 2025; FMNH invoice no. G-55337), approved by William Simpson and authorised by Arjan Mann. Specimens were temporarily imported to Ireland from the US for study. No import or export permits were required. Four specimens were studied per taxonomic group. Taxonomic identification follows museum designations. Agnathan specimens include three identified as Mayomyzon pieckoensis (PF17992, PF5688, PF10788) and one identified as Gilpichthys greenei (PF8448). All acanthodian specimens (PF16201, PF5754, PF16263, PF11509) are of undetermined affinity. Sarcopterygian specimens are designated as Esconichthys apopyris (PF8719, PF9740, PF9868, PF16850), however, new evidence suggests that they may represent early life stages of other sarcopterygian species 87 . Three chondrichthyans are identified as Bandringa rayi (PF10265, PF15328, PF3197) and one as Bandringa sp. (PF16367). Among the actinopterygians, two are assigned to Elonichthys peltigerus (PF7487, PF7499), and two are Elonichthys sp. (PF16084, PF16083). Digital images of specimens were taken using a Nikon D3100 digital SLR camera. Specimens from the Mazon Creek biota are preserved as well-defined two-dimensional impressions within siderite concretions. The studied specimens locally show a thin coating of white kaolinite, which represents a secondary diagenetic precipitate 20 . All examined dark regions within the fossils correspond to locations of melanosome preservation. Several specimens also exhibit a pale circular structure at the centre of each eye, interpreted as the lens. Recent analyses of the Mazon Creek deposits, integrating sedimentology 86 and statistical assessment of over 300,000 siderite concretions 41 , conclude that the site represents a river delta system delivering freshwater and sediment into a shallow, low-energy, brackish marine basin. Within this framework, three distinct biotas have been recognised, each corresponding to a different paleoenvironmental setting: the Braidwood biota is dominated by terrestrial washout flora and fauna in a brackish estuarine environment; the Will-Essex biota supports abundant benthic taxa in a shallow nearshore marine environment; and the Kankakee-Essex biota is inhabited primarily by pelagic and fully marine organisms in a more distal offshore environment 41 . Specimen PF15382 ( Bandringa rayi ) was recovered from Essex deposits of the Vermilion Shaft Mine in Will County. The exact recovery location of PF10788 ( Mayomyzon pieckoensis ) is unknown. All other specimens in this study (Fig. 5 , Supplementary Figs. 11 – 13 ) were recovered from Pit 11 in Will County, which contains predominantly marine fauna of the Essex Formation, with benthic organisms more abundant to the north and pelagic organisms more abundant to the south 41 . While all sarcopterygian fossils included in the study were collected from Pit 11 and thus belong to the marine fauna of the Essex Formation, lungfish scales and teeth are also reported from the estuarine Braidwood Formation 41 . The aquatic species of the Braidwood assemblage are considered to have lived predominantly in brackish estuarine habitats, while their presence in the Essex assemblage represents the seaward limit of their living ranges 41 . Enzymatic melanin extraction Eyes, skin, peritoneum, spleen, kidney, liver, gills, gut, gonads, brain and heart were dissected from each fish where possible. The lung was dissected only in African lungfish and the olfactory sac only in river lamprey. This yielded a total of 432 tissue samples across 12 taxa, with three biological replicates per taxon (Supplementary Table 1 ). Tissues were processed immediately or stored for up to a month at −20 °C prior to further treatment. Melanosomes were extracted using a modified version of Novellino et al. 88 , summarised below. For each dissected tissue sample, 100 mg of tissue was added to 6 mg of collagenase and 1.5 ml of phosphate buffer solution (pH 7.4) prior to incubation for 20 h at 37 °C and 200 rpm. Samples were then centrifuged at 9000 g for 4 min and washed once with 1 ml acetone and three times with 1 ml DDI water, centrifuging at 9000 g between each wash. After a second treatment of collagenase and washing, 1 mg DL-dithiothreitol (DTT), 5 mg proteinase K and 1.5 ml of 0.1 M phosphate buffer were added to each sample prior to incubation for 20 h at 37 °C and 200 rpm. Then, 80 µl Triton X-100 was added and vortexed for 10 s prior to incubation for 4 h at room temperature (15–20 °C) at 200 rpm. Samples were then centrifuged at 9000 g for 4 min and washed once with 1 ml acetone and three times with 1 ml DDI water, centrifuging at 9000 g between each wash. Samples were treated four more times with proteinase K and DTT, with washes between each treatment. Then, 1 ml of 36% HCl was added, and the samples were shaken at 200 rpm for 20 h at room temperature. Samples were washed successively with 1 ml each of ethanol, heptane, and acetone (three times each) and vortexed for 10 s. Samples were centrifuged at 9000 × g for 4 min after each wash before adding 1 ml of acetone and centrifuging at 21,000 × g for 20 min to pelletise the solid residue. The remaining residue was dried overnight in a fume hood and then transferred to glass vials for storage. Scanning electron microscopy Melanosome extracts were fixed onto aluminium stubs with carbon tape and sputter-coated with Au for 50 s using an Agar Manual Sputter Coater. Fossil specimens were analysed uncoated and were wrapped in foil, leaving only the area of analysis exposed to facilitate imaging under high vacuum. All fossil specimens were examined in their entirety as destructive sampling and gold sputter coating were not permitted. Extracts and fossils were examined with a JEOL JSM-IT100 variable-pressure scanning electron microscope at an accelerating voltage of 15 kV and a probe current of 25 nA. The contrast and brightness of SEM images were adjusted in Adobe Photoshop where necessary. Histology Skin and internal tissues (approximately 3 × 3 mm) were fixed in a solution of 2% glutaraldehyde in 0.1 M phosphate buffer (pH 7.4) for 3 h at room temperature (23 °C). For eye tissues, a 2 × 2 mm window was cut in the eye wall prior to fixation to facilitate penetration 89 . Eyes were fixed in a solution of 1% formaldehyde and 1.25% glutaraldehyde in 0.1 M phosphate buffer for 36 h at room temperature 90 . After fixation, eyes were cut into three pieces 91 and processed for paraffin embedding using a Shandon Citadel 1000 automated embedder. Tissues were dehydrated through a graded ethanol series (30%, 50%, 70%, 80% and 2 × 95%; 6 h total), followed by three 2 h washes in 100% ethanol and two 2 h washes in butanol, before infiltration with paraffin (three × 3 h treatments). Paraffin blocks were prepared with a Tissue-Tek TEC 5 console and sectioned at 6 µm using a Leica RM2235 microtome. To facilitate mounting, wax sections were floated on a heated water bath at 40 °C to flatten the section prior to collection on glass slides. Sections were deparaffinised with two 5 m washes in xylene. Sections were left uncovered and imaged using SEM. Data processing and statistical analysis Where possible, the length and width of 100 melanosomes were measured for each sample (totalling 300 across three biological replicates) from digital images using the Fiji distribution package 92 in ImageJ2 93 (Supplementary Data 1 ). For samples with markedly variable melanosome geometries (eye samples), 200 melanosomes were measured to better characterise the full range of morphological variation (totalling 600 across three biological replicates). For tissue yielding fewer than 100 observable melanosomes, all visible melanosomes were measured. Although melanosomes were observed in the dark, carbonaceous eye region of all twenty Mazon Creek fossils analysed, three specimens lacked sufficient melanosomes for statistical analyses (Supplementary Table 2 ): one chondrichthyan (PF15382) and two acanthodians (PF16263, PF11509). In fossils with both cylindrical and spheroidal eye melanosomes, melanosomes are often preserved as two distinct layers, each with size-specific melanosome geometry. These layers were treated as representing the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) and choroid/iris, which are superimposed during fossilisation 20 . A total of 100 melanosomes were measured from each layer (totalling 200 melanosomes per specimen; Supplementary Table 12 ). Measurements of melanosome length and width from extant fishes were performed on melanosomes isolated from tissue by enzymatic extraction. Since melanosomes were removed from their in situ cellular context, their orientations on SEM stubs are effectively random rather than constrained by cellular architecture. Likewise, decay and early diagenetic processes in fossils can disrupt original cellular arrangements, leaving preserved melanosomes similarly disordered. SEM images of the melanosome extracts thus contain melanosomes in multiple orientations, reducing the bias inherent to two-dimensional measurements made on TEM sections of in situ tissue. Melanosomes are typically three-dimensional organelles (spheroidal/cylindrical), as demonstrated by electron tomography 94 , and measurements of their length and width therefore provide a consistent approximation of the principal geometric axes used here for comparative morphometrics. All statistical analyses were conducted in R (version 4.5.0) 95 , with statistical significance assessed using two-sided tests at a threshold of p &lt; 0.05. Statistical analyses are reported in Supplementary Tables 4 – 12 . Data manipulation and visualisation were performed with base R, the tidyverse package (version 2.0.0), 96 and Adobe Illustrator (version 30.8.1). To reduce pseudoreplication, melanosome measurements were first averaged within each biological replicate for each tissue. Differences in melanosome morphology among tissues within species and among species within tissues were assessed using two-sided one-way analyses of variance (ANOVA), with tissue or species, respectively, included as the fixed effect. Model assumptions were assessed using Shapiro–Wilk tests of residual normality, residual diagnostics, and Levene’s tests for homogeneity of variance (car package version 3.1.3 97 ). Where significant differences were detected, estimated marginal means were calculated and pairwise comparisons performed with Tukey adjustment for multiple comparisons (emmeans package version 2.0.4 98 ). Mixture modelling was performed to assess the distribution of eye melanosome aspect ratios among species and taxonomic groups (Supplementary Data 1 ). Gaussian and t -mixture models were applied to identify finite mixtures of Gaussian and multivariate t -distributions, respectively, and to characterise subpopulations with distinct melanosome geometries (mclust version 6.1.3 99 and teigen version 2.2.2 100 packages, respectively). While t -mixture models are better suited to heavy-tailed data and outliers, both t -mixture and Gaussian mixture models produced nearly identical results. Gaussian models are presented here (Supplementary Figs. 8 – 10 ), as mclust in R provides functionality for extracting soft cluster probabilities. The optimal number of populations was determined using the Integrated Completed Likelihood (ICL) criterion. Melanosomes in internal tissues and skin were collectively described here as spheroidal to encompass variation from near-spherical to slightly prolate geometries. Mixture modelling using the ICL criterion did not resolve these subtle geometric differences into discrete populations, and thus they were treated collectively here. For comparisons of eye melanosome populations identified by Gaussian mixture modelling, melanosome measurements were averaged for each population within each biological replicate. These values were analysed using linear mixed-effects models (nlme package version 3.1-171 101 , 102 ). Observed combinations of taxonomic group, preservation state (extant or fossil) and melanosome population were modelled as the fixed effect, with species included as a random effect to account for variation among species. Models allowing residual variance to differ between melanosome populations 1 and 2 were compared with common-variance models using likelihood-ratio tests. As residual variance differed significantly between populations for length and width, these models were fitted allowing population-specific residual variances; this was not required for aspect ratio. Final models were fitted using restricted maximum likelihood, and the significance of fixed effects was assessed with ANOVA. Model assumptions were evaluated using Shapiro–Wilk tests of residual normality, residual diagnostics, and Levene’s tests for homogeneity of variance (car package version 3.1.3 97 ). Where significant differences were detected, estimated marginal means were calculated and pairwise comparisons performed with Tukey adjustment for multiple comparisons (emmeans package version 2.0.4 98 ). To quantify overlap between melanosome population distributions, we computed multivariate Bhattacharyya coefficients (BCs) from the mean vectors and covariance matrices of length, width, and aspect ratio. BC values range from 0 (no overlap) to 1 (complete overlap), providing a quantitative measure of similarity between distributions. Bhattacharyya coefficients (BCs) were assigned to three categories: low (0–0.33), moderate (0.34–0.66) and high (0.67–1), to facilitate interpretation of morphological differentiation among tissues, species and melanosome populations.
