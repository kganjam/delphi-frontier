Introduction Human tissues and organs, such as bones and smooth muscles, have limited regeneration capacity after injury, whereas the myocardium, eye lens, skeletal muscle, and nerves lack regenerative capacity [ 1 ]. Each year, millions of people worldwide die from tissue or organ failure, imposing a substantial medical and economic burden on society and patients [ 2 ]. Autografting, allografting, and surgical repair are considered the gold-standard treatments in clinical practice for tissue damage or trauma. However, their widespread applications have certain limitations, including invasive harvesting, scarcity of sources, impaired integration, immune rejection, severe complications, and potential transmission of hematologic disorders [ 3 – 6 ]. Hence, tissue engineering (TE) and regenerative medicine alternatives are required to promote tissue regeneration in situ. TE has emerged as an approach to achieve tissue regeneration or replacement in situ through a combination of cells, biomaterial scaffolding, and signaling molecules [ 7 , 8 ]. The basic principle of TE is to create a microenvironment that promotes tissue regeneration [ 9 , 10 ]. Scaffold design is the most critical aspect of TE, as it can recapitulate the physiology of the extracellular matrix (ECM) of native tissue in terms of structure, morphology, and mechanical properties [ 11 ]. Electrospun fiber (ESF) scaffolds possess a high-surface-area-to-volume ratio, excellent porosity, controllable fiber diameter, diverse loading components, controlled release of drugs, flexibility, and ease of functionalization with bioactive molecules [ 12 , 13 ]. Micro/nanoscale ESFs mimic the ECM, which allows the diffusion of nutrients and waste products, supports cell adhesion, proliferation, and differentiation, and creates a suitable environment for maintaining cellular metabolism [ 13 , 14 ]. In addition, electrospinning is a simple, cost-effective, and versatile manufacturing technique [ 13 , 15 ]. Therefore, ESF scaffolds are widely applied in TE. Cells can sense the morphological characteristics of the matrix, and the morphological characteristics of the extracellular microenvironment regulate cell signaling, gene expression, cell adhesion, morphology, proliferation, migration, and differentiation through contact guidance [ 16 ]. As for TE, the microstructure of anisotropic scaffolds guides cell orientation and elongation, whereas the nanostructure affects cell adhesion and differentiation [ 17 ]. The anisotropic structure of skeletal muscle, tendons, ligaments, and nerve tissues is the key to their effective physiological function [ 18 – 20 ]. Compared with isotropic random ESF (RESF) scaffolds, anisotropic aligned ESF (AESF) scaffolds are particularly advantageous for these tissues [ 21 , 22 ]. AESFs significantly expedite the wound healing process by modulating cellular behavior and immune responses, demonstrating the potential advantages of AESFs in anisotropic tissue regeneration [ 23 ]. Cells are also crucial in TE. Permanently differentiated cells exhibit decreased metabolic activity and loss of specific markers over the course of continued passage, limiting their utility in TE applications [ 24 ]. In contrast, mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) have emerged as ideal candidates for TE seed cells due to their multidirectional differentiation, immunomodulation, and paracrine cytokine production abilities [ 25 – 27 ]. MSCs can be readily obtained from multiple tissue sources, including bone marrow, adipose tissue, and placenta, all of which have been utilized in TE [ 13 , 19 , 26 ]. The TE scaffold promotes the spontaneous differentiation of endogenous and/or exogenous MSCs, thereby facilitating tissue regeneration [ 28 ]. Moreover, homing MSCs can secrete chemotactic factors, cytokines, and growth factors conducive to tissue regeneration [ 26 ]. By combining multifunctional scaffolds, tissue-specific cells, and growth factors, biochemical and mechanical stimulation, a functional microenvironment is created to stimulate the formation of engineered tissues and achieve tissue regeneration. Herein, we review and summarize representative research in the stem cell-assisted AESF scaffold field between 2023 and 2025, following the logic of “scaffolds-stem cells-application.” First, we review the principles and strategies of aligned electrospinning and briefly describe commonly used polymer materials. In addition, we emphasize the types of stem cells that are widely used in the TE field, summarizing their incorporation methods, and introducing the principles of AESF scaffolds regulating cellular behavior. Subsequently, applications of stem cell-assisted AESF scaffolds in TE are explored. Finally, the drawbacks of this technology are summarized, and the possible future development direction explored, aiming to provide researchers with constructive exploration directions and promote the development of related disciplines. Aligned electrospun nanofiber scaffold preparation Principles and parameters Electrospinning creates ultrafine fibers by applying a high-voltage, low-current electric field to a polymer solution or melt. Electrospray technology originated from Rayleigh’s discovery in 1897 [ 29 ]. He discovered that highly charged droplets are unstable and tend to break down into smaller droplets when passing through a voltage gradient; this property is known as “Rayleigh instability” [ 30 ]. Electrospinning is an extension of electrospraying. J.F. Cooley first patented the electrospinning technology in 1902 as an “apparatus for electrically distributing fibers” in the US [ 31 ], providing the first documented observation of the electrospinning process [ 32 ]. In 1914, Zeleny conducted in-depth research on electrospraying [ 33 ]. Formhals published the first ever patent on electrospun yarn in 1934 [ 34 ]. G.I. Taylor proposed the concept of the Taylor cone in 1964, which provided a fundamental understanding of the electrospinning mechanism [ 35 ]. The popularity of electrospinning grew in the late 20th century [ 32 ]. Subsequent advancements in material discovery and process optimization have led to the development of functional scaffold structures. The electrospinning apparatus is mainly composed of a high-voltage power supply, an injection system, a nozzle, and a collection device [ 36 – 39 ]. Electrospinning technology uses electrostatic forces generated by a high voltage to charge and deform polymer solutions or melts, forming pendant conical droplets at the nozzle tip [ 40 ]. When the electrostatic repulsion at the droplet surface exceeds its surface tension, high-speed flying jets are ejected from the droplet surface [ 41 ]. These jets are stretched by the electric field, undergo solvent evaporation, and the polymer is solidified to rapidly form fibers (Fig. 1 A). The stability of the electrospinning process and quality of the final fiber products (e.g., morphology, diameter, and uniformity) are jointly controlled by a set of key parameters [ 42 ], which can be systematically categorized into three major types: solution, equipment, and environmental parameters. Among these, solution parameters focus on the intrinsic properties of polymer solutions (or melts), including polymer molecular weight (directly affecting solution viscosity [ 43 ] and the degree of chain entanglement [ 44 ]), solution conductivity (determining charge density and jet stretching capacity) [ 45 ], surface tension (influencing critical conditions for Taylor cone formation) [ 46 ], solution viscosity (regulating jet stability [ 47 ] and fiber continuity [ 48 ]), and solvent volatility (directly related to the subsequent fiber solidification rate) [ 49 ]. Process parameters refer to the operating conditions of the electrospinning apparatus, and mainly cover the output voltage of the high-voltage power supply (determining the magnitude of the jet stretching force) [ 50 ], the distance between the nozzle and collection device (i.e., “spinning distance,” influencing solvent evaporation time and jet stretching distance) [ 51 ], the flow rate of the polymer solution (controlling the rate of droplet formation at the nozzle tip), nozzle inner diameter (affecting the initial droplet volume and the initial morphology of the jet) [ 52 ], and collector surface topography and rotational speed (for drum-type collectors, regulating the alignment orientation of fibers). Key environmental parameters include ambient temperature (affecting solvent evaporation rate [ 53 ] and solution viscosity stability [ 54 ]), relative humidity (affecting solvent evaporation and porous structures) [ 55 ]. These three types of parameters interact [ 56 , 57 ]. Their combined action directly determines the diameter distribution, surface smoothness, pore structure, and mechanical properties of the nanofibers, as summarized in Table 1 . Through precise regulation of individual or combined parameters, targeted design of the morphology and functionality of nanofibers can be achieved to meet the requirements of different applications of TE scaffolds. [ 60 , 62 , 63 ] Conductivity The conductivity is positively correlated with charge density. The conductivity must be within a “reasonable range” to maintain jet stability; both excessively high and excessively low conductivities can disrupt the continuity of the spinning process. [ 51 , 60 , 64 – 66 ] Solvent volatility Slow evaporation may cause fiber fusion; solvent volatility must match the jet flight time; excessive speed or slowness may cause electrospinning interruption. [ 51 , 62 – 64 ] Dielectric constant Higher dielectric constants of solvents are associated with decreased fiber diameters. Lower dielectric constants often lead to the formation of beaded fibers. [ 63 , 67 ] Molecular weight of polymer At low molecular weights of polymers, jet breakup occurs, leading to the formation of short fibers or beads; with a gradual increase in molecular weight, the fiber diameter increases correspondingly due to elevated solution viscosity and surface tension. [ 33 , 47 , 68 , 69 ] equipment parameters Solution flow rate Under constant other parameters, flow rate exhibits a positive correlation with fiber diameter. Excessively high rates may promote bead formation. [ 29 , 70 , 71 ] Applied voltage Higher voltages intensify the electric field, facilitating the production of finer fibers. Insufficient voltage prevents the formation of a complete Taylor cone, resulting in only a “hemispherical droplet” or even “dropping phenomenon”. Excessively high voltages can induce jet instability and secondary branching, leading to broader diameter distribution. [ 46 , 72 ] Spinning distance Appropriately increasing the spinning distance may reduce the fiber diameter. When the spinning distance far exceeds the “critical stable distance,” the stability of the jet and the deposition concentration are disrupted, leading to a decrease in fiber deposition efficiency. An excessively short spinning distance can easily cause fiber fusion. Both excessively short and excessively long spinning distances exacerbate diameter non-uniformity. [ 38 , 41 , 72 , 73 ] Nozzle inner diameter The nozzle inner diameter determines the size of the initial droplets. Although a smaller nozzle inner diameter helps form finer fibers, it also increases the risk of needle clogging; in addition, a higher applied voltage is required to form a stable jet. A larger nozzle inner diameter results in larger initial droplets, and a higher flow rate is needed to maintain a stable jet. [ 52 ] Collector’s properties The topography of the collector surface (e.g., flat, patterned, or 3D-structured) influences fiber alignment and spatial deposition. 3D collectors facilitate controlled spatial architecture of the deposited fibers. The rotational speed of the collector directly affects fiber orientation; higher linear surface speeds promote highly aligned nanofiber assembly. [ 26 , 51 , 72 , 74 ] Environmental parameters Temperature Reasonably elevated temperature accelerates solvent evaporation, favoring the formation of finer fibers with smoother surfaces. [ 62 , 75 ] Relative humidity High humidity retards solvent evaporation. Under the premise of using highly volatile solvents, high humidity delays solvent evaporation, maintaining the fibers in a semi-cured state, and further promoting the condensation of water vapor from the air within the fibers and inducing phase separation, ultimately leading to the formation of porous structures. Conversely, low humidity facilitates rapid evaporation, yielding smooth morphologies. Key scaffold characteristics (such as the fiber diameter, geometry, topography, porosity, pore size, mechanical properties, degradation rate, polymer composition, and surface chemistry) are critical factors influencing the behavior of immune cells and stem cells [ 77 , 78 ]. By modulating these parameters, scaffolds can be engineered to support cell infiltration, specific cell types and direct desired differentiation outcomes. Strategy for aligned electrospinning AESF scaffolds can be produced by multiple strategies, such as the high-speed roll collector [ 80 ], wet spinning method [ 16 ], and stable jet electrospinning (SJES) [ 81 ] (Fig. 1 B). In the early 2000s, AESF fabrication advanced significantly with the development of rotating drum collectors [ 35 ]. Highly aligned nanofibers can be fabricated by precisely increasing the rotational speed of the collector [ 82 ]. The rotating collector operates at speeds of &lt; 500 rpm and produces isotropic (random) fibers. Increasing collector velocity from 2500 rpm to 4000 rpm leads to the formation of high anisotropy and thin-diameter fibrous structures [ 51 ]. Furthermore, the speed of the rotating drum is between 1000 and 3000 rpm [ 5 , 18 , 24 , 83 , 84 ]. For instance, Xue et al. produced an AESF scaffold at a rotational drum speed of 1800 rpm, incorporating basic fibroblast growth factor (bFGF)-bovine serum albumin (BSA) nanoparticles (NPs). After seeding human amniotic MSCs (hAMSCs) onto scaffolds, the AESF scaffold was implanted into the Achilles tendon defect model and showed a satisfactory repair effect (Fig. 2 A) [ 24 ]. Furthermore, a tubular scaffold can be formed for neuro and vascular reconstruction by using a tubular collector or folding or curling the AESF membrane along the longitudinal direction [ 45 , 85 ]. The collector of the wet spinning equipment is a grounded liquid bath, which is loaded with different solutions according to the needs of electrospinning. To fabricate a triple-layer bionic vascular scaffold, Wang et al. combined the electrospinning technique and Wet Vertical Magnetic Rod Electrospinning (WVMRE) to accumulate different layers. In the WVMRE technique, a roller collector with a 62% ethanol solution is driven by magnetic stirring to collect well-aligned poly-ε-caprolactone (PCL) fibers to simulate the vascular middle layer (Fig. 2 B) [ 86 ]. Aligned poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA)/ strontium-doped α-calcium sulfate hemihydrate (SrCSH) fibrous scaffolds can be fabricated by wet spinning. The osteogenic differentiation of umbilical cord MSCs (UCMSCs) was synergistically enhanced by the aligned PLGA/SrCSH fibrous scaffolds, which was related to the regulation of cell arrangement and expression of osteogenic genes [ 16 ]. The wet spinning technique can also produce aligned hydrogel fibers. The alginate/gelatin methacryloyl (GelMA) solution and the human bone marrow MSCs (hBMSCs) solution were extruded into a calcium chloride solution via a microfluidic coaxial needle extrusion system, resulting in immediate fiber crosslinking [ 11 ]. A vortex-assisted electrospinning system can produce drug-loaded AESF by adding drugs to the liquid. For instance, the vortex-assisted electrospinning system in combination with ibuprofen (IBU)-in-water emulsion was used to fabricate IBU-loaded PLLA ESF membranes [ 87 ]. SJES is a simplified yet effective aligned electrospinning method that does not require complex collectors or electric field treatments. The key step in SJES is to add high molecular weight poly(ethylene oxide) (PEO) to the spinning dope [ 88 ]. Because PEO has a non-ionic nature, it exhibits high spinnability and can effectively eliminate the jet-whipping motion in conventional electrospinning, allowing for easy collection of highly aligned fibers [ 57 ]. Zhang et al. produced an aligned poly(L-lactic acid) (PLLA)- poly(3-hydroxybutyrate-co-3-hydroxyvalerate) (PHBV) fiber scaffold with temperature memory effect, and regulated osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs utilizing aligned fibers [ 81 ]. For nerve repair, the aligned poly(pyrrole) (PPy)/ polydopamine (PDA)/ PLLA ultrafine fibrous neural scaffold was prepared by the SJES technique and in situ polymerization. After seeding induced Schwann-like cells (iSCs) differentiated from MSCs into the scaffold, their promoting neural injury repair effect was underscored by the sciatic nerve defect rat model (Fig. 2 C) [ 25 ]. AESF scaffolds constructed from electrospun nanofiber yarns are excellent for anisotropic tissue regeneration, such as nerve and tendon repair [ 89 – 91 ]. The nanofiber buncher for electrospun yarns consists of a neutral metal funnel and a core yarn passing through the hollow metal funnel [ 92 ]. During the formation process of the core-sheath yarns, the funnel rotates to make the as-collected nanofibers form a stable spinning triangle cone wrapping the core yarn (Fig. 2 D) [ 93 , 94 ]. A dynamic liquid system is also used to fabricate the aligned nanofiber yarn scaffold. Specifically, ESFs are twisted and organized into yarns through a water vortex and collected on a mandrel [ 95 , 96 ]. Wu et al. produced the decellularized meniscus extracellular matrix (dmECM)/ poly (lactide-co-caprolactone) (PLLA-CL) ESF yarns using the dynamic liquid system and promoted the chondrogenic differentiation of BMSCs and adipose tissue-derived stem cells (ADSCs) in this yarn scaffold [ 96 ]. The above-mentioned AESF scaffold preparation methods are relatively mature and are currently the most commonly used AESF preparation methods. However, in recent years, stem cell-assisted AESF scaffold preparation has shown a trend of finely controlling the fiber structure or combining with various biological scaffold preparation technologies to adapt to more complex regeneration scenarios. Electrode-assisted electrospinning represents an alternative strategy for achieving fiber alignment. The combination of an electrical field enhancer and a cylinder drum collector can actively and finely control fiber orientation by supplementing the electrode [ 97 ]. Designing patterned collectors, such as meshlike collectors, improves ESF alignment [ 98 ]. Trench-guided electrospinning can fabricate AESF scaffolds. The technology, combined with direct ink writing (DIW), directly fabricates 3D aligned nanofiber scaffolds, which results in the alignment of human-induced pluripotent stem cell-derived cardiomyocytes (hiPSC-CMs) and their 3D distribution (Fig. 2 E) [ 99 ]. Furthermore, mechanical stretching as a post-electrospinning treatment is an effective method to convert random fibers into aligned fibers [ 100 ]. Copyright 2024, American Chemical Society Materials for stem cell-assisted aligned electrospun nanofiber scaffolds Material selection is a central consideration in designing the AESF scaffold for TE. Biocompatibility and biodegradability are the foremost features of materials [ 1 ]. Presently, &gt; 100 kinds of polymers, including natural polymers and synthetic polymers, have been successfully prepared as ESFs [ 101 ]. The natural polymers and synthetic polymers have their own features and functions (Table 2 ). Natural materials are proteins, DNA, and polysaccharides derived from plants, microorganisms, animals, or human tissues [ 12 , 102 ]. Common examples of natural biomaterials for stem cell-assisted AESF scaffolds include collagen [ 103 , 104 ], gelatin (Gel) [ 105 – 107 ], decellularized extracellular matrix [ 108 ], hyaluronic acid [ 4 ], chitosan (CS) [ 109 ], silk fibroin (SF) [ 26 ], keratin [ 80 ], and alginate [ 110 ]. The natural polymers have abundant biological binding sites, offering the benefits of enhanced biocompatibility and lower cytotoxicity than synthetic polymers [ 110 ]. Moreover, the native signaling molecules of natural polymers facilitate cell adhesion, proliferation, migration, and differentiation [ 96 , 108 ]. For example, gel, a hydrolysate of collagen, contains inherent RGD sequences and matrix metalloproteinase (MMP) cleavage sites, which respectively promote cell adhesion and cell remodeling [ 28 ]. Keratin is rich in the cysteine amino acid, which enhances cell attachment and growth [ 80 ]. However, the spinnability and mechanical properties of single natural polymers are poor and vary by batch [ 101 ]. Some examples of synthetic polymers used for stem cell-assisted AESF scaffolds are PCL [ 5 , 111 – 113 ], PLLA [ 25 , 87 , 114 – 117 ], poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA) [ 16 , 97 , 118 ], PEO [ 114 ], polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) [ 80 , 109 , 119 ], and polyurethane (PU) [ 13 , 120 , 121 ]. These polymers have standard synthesis processes and the characteristics of low cost, controllable mechanical strength, stable physical properties, and excellent spinnability [ 18 , 122 ]. However, these polymers lack bioactive signaling molecules, which require a natural polymer combination or bioactive factor addition [ 102 ]. For instance, PCL, a type of aliphatic poly (α-hydroxy esters), has tailorable mechanical properties and adaptable degradability. However, the hydrophobicity of PCL prevents it from providing the biochemical cues for cell adhesion. CS [ 109 ], Gel [ 106 ], SF [ 45 ], and other natural polymers are combined with PCL to increase its biocompatibility. Comparatively, PLLA is more conducive to cell proliferation than PCL [ 123 ]. Electrical stimulation (ES) can enhance the transmission of electrical signals between cells and the proliferation, adhesion, growth, and differentiation of cells. Conductive synthetic polymers such as polyaniline [ 83 ], PPy [ 19 , 25 , 124 ], and polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) [ 125 ] have been frequently utilized in stem cell-assisted AESF scaffolds for nerve, bone, and tendon regeneration. However, these polymers may have unsatisfactory mechanical properties. As such, mixing with other polymers with high tensile strength, like PU, is necessary [ 126 ]. Stem cells that have been used to make AESF scaffolds for TE include embryonic stem cells (ESCs) [ 80 ], adult stem cells, and induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) [ 97 , 154 ] (Fig. 3 ). Compared to ESCs, which can differentiate into cells of the three germ layers, adult stem cells can only differentiate into a limited range of cell types specific to the tissue of origin. In contrast, adult stem cells are less ethically controversial and have a more abundant source than ESCs [ 191 ]. iPSCs avoid ethical concerns and supply limitations because they are generated from reprogrammed adult somatic cells [ 97 ]. Original figure by the authors MSCs are the most commonly used adult stem cells in the stem cell-assisted AESF TE scaffold field. The variability of MSC treatment outcomes is influenced by batch effects, inherent heterogeneity, and ECM. Specifically, batch effects on the consistency of MSC treatment stem from differences in preparation and culture techniques or variations between passages. The number of passages is crucial in MSC culture and has a significant impact on therapeutics. Intrinsic factors such as cell donors, age variation, and location of origin, as well as heterogeneity caused by the microenvironment, also significantly affect the stability of MSC efficacy [ 192 ]. BMSCs, ADSCs [ 26 , 193 ], endodontic-derived stem/progenitor cells [ 57 ], and periodontal ligament stem cells (PDLSCs) [ 56 , 71 ], as adult MSCs, have been used to enhance the tissue regeneration effect of AESF scaffolds. Since 1991, BMSCs have been the gold standard in bone TE [ 183 ]. BMSCs are mechanosensory cells, and their responses are triggered by externally stimulated cell deformation [ 125 ]. Microgroove topography with a rigid substrate can stimulate the differentiation of MSCs into smooth muscle cells [ 110 ]. AESF scaffold-incorporated BMSCs with piezoelectric properties are beneficial for bone reconstruction. Both aligned and random annealed PVDF ESF membranes present high β -phase fractions. Under ES, BMSCs seeded on these membranes show enhanced osteogenic ability, calcium activity, and F-actin distribution [ 125 ]. In addition, the role of BMSCs in neural TE [ 25 , 194 ], tendon TE [ 11 ], and cartilage TE [ 96 ] cannot be ignored. For maxillofacial bone regeneration, the dental pulp stem cells (DPSCs) play an important role [ 63 ]. ADSCs are relatively easy to collect, abundant, minimally invasive, and have a low immunogenic profile, making them suitable for TE [ 103 , 194 ]. Haghighipour et al. constructed a polyacrylonitrile/polyaniline/carbon nanotubes (CNTs) aligned conductive ESF scaffold and seeded human ADSCs into the scaffold. The hADSCs cultured on the prepared scaffolds expressed Nestin, β-tubulin III, MAP2, and other neural markers after using the administered ES [ 83 ]. ADSCs can also be seeded on the PLLA-CL/ porcine menisci dmECM electrospun yarn scaffold to improve cartilage regeneration [ 96 ]. The growth differentiation factor 11 (GDF11) seems to promote myogenic differentiation of ADSCs on aligned PCL/collagen I ESF scaffolds [ 104 ]. Tendon-derived stem cells (TDSCs) are commonly used in tendon TE [ 114 ]. Tendon progenitor cells (TPCs) can contribute to both tenocyte and fibrocyte populations. Functionalized aligned dextran vinyl sulfone (DexVS) ESF scaffolds provide physical cues to TPCs and promote complete tendon differentiation of TPCs in response to transforming growth factor-β3 (TGF-β3) [ 195 ]. The PDLSCs are commonly used in TE repair of the periodontal ligament [ 113 , 196 ]. Neural stem cells (NSCs) are considered a promising cell source for neural TE due to their ability to differentiate into the three main neural cell types [ 197 ]. AESF scaffolds can support the differentiation of NSCs into the neuronal lineage, accelerate synapse formation, and enhance neurite extension [ 13 , 198 , 199 ]. Marques et al. constructed aligned PCL/Gel ESF scaffolds with superparamagnetic iron oxide NPs, and promoted neural differentiation of NSCs on the magnetic responsive scaffold [ 198 ]. Amniotic epithelial stem cells (AECs) [ 200 ], hAMSCs [ 24 ], and UCMSCs [ 201 ], kinds of perinatal stem cells, lack tumorigenicity, have low immunogenicity, multi-lineage differentiation potential, and can undergo a gradual transition from epithelial to mesenchymal phenotype in vitro and in vivo [ 202 ] and have been loaded on AESF scaffolds in tendon TE and bone TE fields. The iPSCs have multidirectional differentiation capabilities, and their derived cardiomyocytes (iPSC-CMs) have been incorporated into the AESF scaffolds for myocardial TE. The iPSC-CMs grown on traditional planar substrates often exhibit random distribution, inefficient calcium ion (Ca 2+ ) handling, short sarcomeres, and poorly developed excitation-contraction coupling [ 112 , 154 ]. In contrast, the iPSC-CMs grown on AESF scaffolds elongate along the long axis of the fibers, exhibit a mature myocardial phenotype, and can undergo highly synchronized contractions [ 112 ]. Strategies for incorporating stem cells into nanofibers Nowadays, seeding stem cells onto the prepared AESF scaffold is the most commonly used stem cell incorporation strategy. Stem cells can be seeded onto the scaffold before implantation, or injected in situ simultaneously with scaffold implantation, combining stem cell therapy with scaffold-assisted nerve regeneration. An aligned fibrin nanofiber hydrogel modified with N-Cadherin-Fc (AFGN) made by the wet spinning method and loaded with NSCs in vitro, was implanted into a rat model of 2 mm-long complete transected spinal cord injury (SCI). The NSC-loaded AESF scaffold significantly promoted grafted NSC retention, immunomodulation, neuronal differentiation, and in vivo integration with inherent neurons, thus finally achieving renaissance neural relay formation and encouraging locomotor functional recovery (Fig. 4 A) [ 203 ]. Sohn et al. implanted an aligned PLGA-gold NPs (GNPs) ESF scaffold and neural progenitor cell (NPC) transplantation simultaneously into the SCI site of Sprague–Dawley (SD) rats. The results showed that the survival outcomes of transplanted NPCs were noticeably increased with the aligned PLGA-GNP scaffold (Fig. 4 B) [ 118 ]. The 3D nanofiber-assisted embedded bioprinting (3D-NFEP) is also a strategy for making cell-loaded AESF scaffolds for TE, where hiPSC-CMs were added to low-viscosity GelMA bioinks. After fabricating the aligned PCL fiber scaffold, the cells were printed on the scaffold. Following the hydrogel degradation, hiPSC-CMs were structurally induced to mature cardiomyocytes (Fig. 4 C) [ 204 ]. Cell electrospinning (CE) is an electrospinning-based technique that can generate fibers embedded with living cells while controlling the formation of micropatterns. CE was first proposed by Jayasinghe et al. in 2006 to demonstrate the feasibility of developing active bioscaffolds using a modified electrospinning process [ 205 ]. This technique improves nutrient accessibility and cell distribution within the scaffold. For esophageal reconstruction, MSC-derived smooth muscle cells (SMCs) were added to alginate/PEO solution to prepare the cell-laden bioink. Following, the cell-laden bioink was supplied at 0.25 mL/h, electrospun under a 10.5 kV high voltage, and deposited on a PCL strut to fabricate the patches. After placement of the patches over the esophageal wound in SD rats, they guided highly elongated and neatly aligned cell morphology and accelerated the MSC-derived SMCs differentiation, contributing to an effective strategy for reconstructing smooth muscle tissue and treating esophageal perforation (Fig. 4 D) [ 110 ]. While seeding stem cells onto the surface of AESF scaffolds in vivo and in vitro is the most common method for stem cell loading, it cannot guarantee uniform distribution of stem cells within the scaffold, and data on the number of stem cells at the center of the scaffold are lacking. 3D-NFEP and CE can more conveniently integrate stem cells into the scaffold, and by resuspending the stem cells in bio-ink, uniform distribution within the scaffold can be ensured. Copyright 2023, Elsevier Mechanisms of aligned electrospun nanofiber scaffolds regulating stem cell behavior AESFs can regulate stem cell function and improve their differentiation through mechanical, chemical signals, and biosignals, such as the topology and stiffness of scaffolds, ES, MS, and bioactive factors, thereby repairing tissue injury. Mechanical signals Topology An advantage of ESF scaffolds is that the structure of ESF is similar to that of ECM collagen fibers. Fibers with diameters between 350 and 1100 nm promote cell proliferation, thereby forming a uniformly distributed cell layer [ 12 ]. Nanotopographical cues have a more profound impact on hMSCs than biochemical cues and are nanotopographically dependent. Nanotopography can modulate cell behavior by mechanically coupling the cell membrane and nuclear envelope, deforming the nucleus at the nanoscale via the cytoskeleton [ 206 ]. The AESF membranes can guide cell migration, regulate the immune microenvironment, and promote angiogenesis and differentiation of cells [ 6 , 198 , 207 ]. Activation of PIEZO1 in hADSCs cultured on aligned substrates results in a 30–40% decrease in cell extension area and increased cell elongation [ 208 ]. For bone TE, RESF nanofeatures promote osteogenesis. The BMSCs on RESFs have a large adhesion area and a stable microenvironment, leading to active calcium dynamics, which promote better differentiation of BMSCs on RESFs than on AESFs [ 125 ]. Yes-associated protein (YAP) has been identified as a mechanoresponsive factor whose activation is mediated by substrate nanotopography and biochemical cues. YAP nucleocytoplasmic localization can regulate stem cell proliferation and differentiation [ 206 ]. When hMSCs spread on a large adhesion area, YAP/TAZ localization in the nucleus increases and enhances stem cell osteogenic differentiation [ 209 ]. The rough surface morphology of the scaffold is also conducive to the adhesion of growth factors, indirectly regulating cell behavior [ 210 ]. Stiffness Stiffness, commonly known as rigidity, is a mechanical property measuring the resistance of the material to deformation. Matrix stiffness influences cell-matrix signaling and cell fate [ 211 ]. Scaffolds of different stiffness guide stem cells to differentiate into different cell lineages. Hard matrices, which are in the GPa range, promote osteogenic differentiation of stem cells [ 12 , 212 ]. A medium-stiffness matrix in the MPa range can promote the myogenic differentiation of stem cells [ 18 , 122 ]. A soft matrix in the kPa range promotes the neural differentiation of stem cells [ 9 , 28 ]. For in vitro TE, when the fibers were pinned around a substrate, the cells sensed the stiffness of the plastic substrate (approximately 2 GPa), with the fiber orientation structure providing only contact guidance. However, when the fibers were suspended from a support frame, the cells sensed the stiffness of the support frame connecting the aligned fibers, which enhanced the regulatory ability of the scaffold on stem cells [ 213 ]. As early as 2007, Tuan et al. demonstrated that the tensile modulus of AESF scaffolds in the fiber direction was about five times that of RESF scaffolds [ 214 ]. The increased tensile modulus is beneficial for guiding cells. TSCs cultured on scaffolds with a medium modulus (1429 MPa) showed upregulation of tendon-specific genes ( Col-I , Tnmd , SCX , and TNC ) [ 215 ]. ECM stiffness also affects cell differentiation through the activation of YAP/TAZ, which requires tension in the actin cytoskeleton and Rho [ 216 , 217 ]. When the YAP/TAZ pathway is inhibited, stiffness-mediated regulation of hMSC differentiation is abolished [ 209 ]. Electrical stimulation Bioelectricity is an important feature of many body tissues, such as bones, muscles, nerves, and sensory organs of the inner ear. These tissues and organs exhibit significant piezoelectric behavior, where mechanical stress can ultimately induce or activate changes in cell surface charge. Piezoelectric polymers can also generate transient surface charges under mechanical deformation [ 218 ], known as piezoelectric response, providing electrical stimulation to cells and promoting tissue formation [ 51 ]. The activity of cells on piezoelectric materials can also generate spontaneous electrical potentials to regulate stem cell differentiation [ 125 ]. All cells can be broadly divided into three categories: electrically excitable cells, electrically responsive (but non-excitable) cells, and electrically insensitive cells. Electrically excitable cells are characterized by their ability to rapidly respond to electrical stimulation through specialized ion channels and generate action potentials that propagate across their membranes [ 116 ]. Neurons, cardiac muscle cells, and skeletal muscle cells are typical electrically excitable cells. ES promotes iPSC-CM promotion [ 154 ]. ES of appropriate intensity can promote neurite extension and neural differentiation [ 219 , 220 ]. Osteoblasts and fibroblasts are electrically responsive cells. The skin has trans-epidermal potentials that exist as an epidermal battery. Damaged skin exhibits a negative electrical potential, generating transient currents that promote the aggregation of epidermal cells and fibroblasts [ 221 ]. ES can also promote the osteogenic differentiation of stem cells. The specific mechanism is that ES changes the membrane potential and activates voltage-gated calcium channels, allowing extracellular calcium ions to flow into the cell, activating calmodulin and consuming adenosine triphosphate (ATP). In addition, ES directly enhances ATP synthesis, reorganizing the depolymerized fibrous actin (F-actin) into new F-actin, which proves that stem cells have the potential for osteogenic differentiation [ 125 ]. Because of the presence of DC voltage (endogenous electric field) in biological tissues, DC voltage is often used for ES [ 28 ]. Conductive materials can enhance intercellular charge transport and electrical signal transmission, thereby enhancing the adhesion, proliferation, infiltration, and migration of cells [ 219 ]. In addition to directly making AESFs of conductive polymers, such as PANI [ 83 ], and PPy [ 222 ] spinning scaffolds, CNTs [ 28 , 223 ], metal NPs [ 224 ], and GO [ 122 ] are also commonly used to increase the conductivity of the scaffold. Increasing the β phase of conductive polymers is an effective way to improve the conductivity of polymers [ 125 ]. Magnetic stimulation Magnetic stimulation (MS) is a method for stimulating cells/tissues by applying static or dynamic magnetic fields (MFs), which may generate current, tension, or heat in situ [ 222 ]. Compared to electrical or photostimulation, MS is a painless, non-invasive, and safe technology that requires no implantable devices and can deeply penetrate tissues such as muscle and bone, offering significant advantages in non-invasive treatment [ 225 ]. In particular, in bone and nerve TE, MS is a hot research topic [ 124 , 225 , 226 ]. The addition of magnetic NPs (MNPs), such as superparamagnetic iron oxide, to nanofibers not only increases the controllability of fiber morphology [ 198 , 207 ] but also makes it possible for stem cells to obtain guidance from external MS through endocytosis of MNPs at the injury site [ 227 ]. Specifically, magnetic fields and MNPs may trigger alterations in focal adhesions, leading to elongation of cytoskeletal fibers, activation of linkers in the nucleoskeleton-cytoskeleton (LINC) complex, and deformation of the nuclear envelope, ultimately leading to mechanical regulation of chromatin conformational changes [ 226 ]. MS may affect the rate of cellular chemical reactions, free radical concentrations, and reactive oxygen species (ROS)-mediated mitosis in cellular systems, and may be associated with nerve regeneration through signaling pathway activation, ion transport, and growth factor secretion [ 225 , 228 , 229 ]. Since biological systems are inevitably affected by prolonged exposure to the geomagnetic field (GF) and ambient electromagnetic radiation, the MS-promoted tissue regeneration requires the addition of an external magnetic field in a specific direction [ 124 , 225 ]. An external pulsed electromagnetic field (PEMF) around the cell culture dish can potentially enhance the osteogenesis and neurogenesis of DPSCs on aligned PCL/reduced graphene oxide (rGO) ESFs, whose result was the synergistic effects of electric currents generated by the irradiation of PEMF, rGO, and PEMF [ 124 ]. In the in vivo experiment, neodymium magnets were fixed on both sides of the rat cage to generate a static magnetic field inside the cage, and the magnetic field strength at different positions in the cage was measured using a gaussmeter [ 225 ]. In summary, the regulation of stem cell differentiation by physical signals is diverse and interconnected (Fig. 5 ). Scaffold topology and stiffness both generally regulate cell differentiation by modulating cell morphology, and this process is mainly achieved through the YAP/TAZ signaling pathway. Stem cells on RESF scaffolds and high-rigidity substrates exhibit larger adhesion cell areas and higher YAP nuclear localization, promoting osteogenic differentiation [ 209 , 216 , 217 ]. Electrical signals mainly guide stem cell differentiation by influencing cell surface ion channels; simultaneously, ion channel activity is also affected by scaffold topology [ 125 ]. For magnetic stimulation, its regulatory effect on cell morphology is similar to that of topology, both altering the cytoskeleton structure through focal adhesion, leading to nuclear membrane deformation and ultimately changes in chromatin conformation that affect cell differentiation [ 125 , 226 ]. Original figure by the authors Chemical and biological signals Growth factors In addition to physical signals, chemical signals and biosignals are also crucial in the stem cell-assisted AESF scaffold for tissue reconstruction. The growth factors (GFs), such as vascular endothelial [ 230 ], epidermal [ 231 ], nerve [ 232 ], platelet-derived [ 233 ], and insulin-like growth factor [ 234 ], are often used to improve the regenerative effect of TE [ 235 ]. GDF-7 is a kind of GF that is involved during both tendon ontogeny and healing responses [ 236 ]. Compared with other GFs, GDF-7 has an effective differentiation potential, avoiding undesired non-tenogenic differentiation [ 111 ]. However, the application of GDF-7, like other GFs, is limited by its short half-life and is easily destroyed by enzymes in the physiological environment [ 237 ]. Therefore, mesoporous silica NPs (MSNs) were used as intermediate carriers of GDF-7 to the AESF scaffold due to their large specific surface area, simple surface chemical modification, uniform pore size distribution, adjustable pore network characteristics, pore size ranging from 2 to 50 nm, good biocompatibility, and no negative effects [ 111 , 238 ]. The bFGF is another commonly used GF in the stem cell-assisted AESF field. In a study by Lei et al., bFGF was loaded onto BSA NPs, and chitosan was then adsorbed on the shell of the NPs by electrostatic self-assembly to protect bFGF and sustain its release [ 24 ]. Chitosan-bFGF NPs fabricated through electrostatic spraying were also a strategy to separate bFGF from the environment [ 239 ]. N-cadherin (NCAD) is a cadherin present in the CNS and plays a critical role in normal CNS development, neurite outgrowth, and axon regeneration [ 240 , 241 ]. N-cadherin (NCAD) is a cadherin present in the CNS and plays a critical role in normal CNS development, neurite outgrowth, and axon regeneration [ 203 , 242 ]. The cytoplasmic domain of NCAD can form a cadherin-cadherin complex by binding to α-catenin and β-catenin. This complex anchors actin filaments (F-actin), enabling the cytoskeleton to sense and respond to mechanical coupling at the cell membrane, thereby regulating cell adhesion and migration [ 203 , 240 ]. Furthermore, NCAD promotes axonal growth and dendritic branching through fibroblast growth factor receptor activation. During CNS development, surface expression of NCAD by NSCs regulates NSC adhesion and differentiation. Maintained NCAD adhesion promotes NSC differentiation into neurons by regulating the Wnt/β-cadherin and AKT signaling pathways [ 203 , 243 ]. To utilize and present NCAD in TE, a recombinant NCAD human Fc chimeric protein (NCAD-Fc) was constructed, and then bound to the surface of AESFs via hydrophobic interactions, protein A (PA) affinity capture, or antigen-antibody interactions [ 203 ]. Bioactive inorganic elements Besides GFs, bioactive inorganic elements are also used to improve the repair effect of TE. Strontium (Sr) is one of the most widely used elements to modulate the osteogenic and osteoclastic activities of biomaterials and has beneficial effects on bone remodeling [ 244 ]. Sr-doped α-calcium sulfate hemihydrate (SrCSH) can be combined with polymer fibers and exhibits good biocompatibility, bioabsorbability, osteoconductivity, and osteoinductivity. Incorporation of Sr into AESF scaffolds facilitates osteogenic differentiation of UCMSCs [ 16 ]. Herbs Moreover, bioactive molecules derived from herbs are suitable for stem cell-assisted AESF scaffolds. Curcumin (Cur) is a widely available bioactive polyphenol with diverse biological and pharmacological properties [ 245 , 246 ]. Cur not only promotes neurite outgrowth and induces neural differentiation, but its phenolic hydroxyl groups can rapidly bind to free radicals in the SCI-induced microenvironment, demonstrating the ability to efficiently scavenge a variety of ROS [ 45 , 247 ]. The intracellular redox balance maintained by Cur is believed to reshape the hostile microenvironment, alleviate its pathological progression, and thus protect neurons from secondary injury [ 248 ]. Loading Cur into the AESF scaffolds prior to transplantation can avoid systemic elimination, bypass the blood–brain barrier, improve bioavailability, and enhance its neuroprotective effects [ 45 ]. Applications in TE The AESFs have a structure similar to collagen in the natural ECM, which can regulate cell morphology and provide physical indices for stem cells [ 6 , 199 ]. Furthermore, they have a high surface area to volume ratio and porosity, which facilitates cell adhesion and migration [ 24 , 95 ]. AESFs have a variety of fabrication strategies, with the ability to customize complex structures, high cost-effectiveness, ease of fabrication, and the ability to select different polymers to impart different functionalities to the fibers [ 249 , 250 ]. PHBV, poly(3-hydroxybutyrate-co-3-hydroxyvalerate) Bone TE Repairing and reconstructing large bone defects caused by a variety of clinical conditions, such as trauma, fractures, bone tumors, osteoporosis, skeletal deformities, and avascular necrosis, is a major challenge. Traditional therapies, including autologous transplantation, allogeneic transplantation, and xenotransplantation, are often limited by donor site complications, limited sources, and immune rejection [ 17 , 257 ]. Bone TE offers a promising approach to repairing and regenerating bone lesions. The ESF TE scaffolds can directly mimic bone tissue structure and combine with stem cells to reconstruct bone tissue. The Young’s modulus of cortical bone is approximately 3.1–34.3 GPa, and the strength is 65–238 MPa, which is mainly attributed to its main structure consisting of type I collagen fibers and hydroxyapatite NPs [ 12 ]. Bone healing is a complex multi-stage cascade process, which mainly includes three stages: inflammation, bone formation, and remodeling [ 6 ]. An ideal bone tissue engineering scaffold should possess the following advantages: sufficient mechanical properties as a temporary support platform for cell growth, degradation rate consistent with bone tissue repair, good biocompatibility, and physicochemical properties required for promoting bone regeneration at all stages [ 258 ]. Shi et al. produced a double-layer electrospun biomimetic periosteum, with aligned PCL/Gel/deferoxamine (DFO) ESFs as the outer fibrous layer and random PCL/Gel/aspirin (ASP) shell and PCL/silicon NPs (SiNPs) core coaxial ESFs as the inner cambial layer for bone regeneration. At the rat skull defect site, ASP is rapidly released in the early stages of bone healing to promote anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects, DFO exhibits slow release in the early stages to promote angiogenesis, and SiNPs are continuously released throughout the entire bone repair stage to enhance osteoblast proliferation, differentiation, and osteogenesis [ 6 ]. To achieve the structural and functional relationship of bone tissue in regenerated bone, it is crucial to manufacture biomimetic scaffolds that resemble the structure of the bone osteon. There are three main challenges in developing osteon-inspired scaffolds: First, scaffolds should mimic the structure and properties of the mineralized collagen fibers that make up osteon lamellae. Second, the scaffold should possess anisotropic properties to mimic the oriented collagen fibers in the lamina. Third, the scaffold should incorporate features of the different tissue interfaces observed in regenerating osteons, such as those between the bone matrix and blood vessels or nerve fibers in the Haversian canals [ 19 ]. From an engineering point of view, bone is a natural piezoelectrical material with a piezoelectric constant similar to that of quartz, primarily due to type I collagen, a major component of the organic ECM [ 51 , 125 ]. In normal physiological activities, original bone formation, bone repair, and bone remodeling are accompanied by piezoelectric effects. Cell behaviors, such as adhesion and migration, on piezoelectric materials can spontaneously generate potentials to cause noninvasive ES to regulate stem cell differentiation [ 125 ]. Furthermore, when bone is subjected to mechanical stress, electrical signals are generated, which in turn promote tissue growth and remodeling [ 51 ]. Polyhydroxybutyrate (PHB), a natural biopolyester, exhibits good mechanical strength and biocompatibility, and its inherent piezoelectric properties can simulate the piezoelectric effect of bones to promote bone growth [ 19 ]. PPy is a common piezoelectric and conductive polymer with antioxidant properties that is beneficial for stem cell differentiation [ 25 , 126 ]. Katti et al. fabricated PHB-based mineralized ESF tubular scaffolds to mimic the osteons for bone reconstruction. The inner layer was aligned 7%PHB/0.5% PPy ESFs to support nerve and blood vessel growth, and the outer layer was mineralized aligned 7%PHB/1%Gel ESFs with a low crystalline calcium phosphate surface to mimic mineralized collagen fibers in bone (Fig. 6 A) [ 19 ]. This study provided a promising AESF bone TE strategy to mimic both the structure of bone and its piezoelectric effect. Bone defects at special sites can also be repaired while reconstructing functions by designing a composite ESF membrane that combines fiber membranes with different properties. For example, in skull base repair, a composite radially AESF membrane composed of PCL, Gel, and core-shell structured PCPP (PDA as core and calcium polyphosphate as shell) NPs acts as the osteogenic layer, promoting directional cell migration and osteogenic differentiation. The PCL/cefazolin ESF membrane, as a leak-proof and antibacterial layer, prevents fluid leakage while simultaneously releasing antimicrobial agents to combat bacterial infection [ 259 ]. Cartilage TE Cartilage is a structural component found throughout the human body and can be divided into three types, distinguished by the composition of its ECM: elastic cartilage (ECM containing elastic fibers), fibrous cartilage (ECM rich in collagen fibers), and hyaline cartilage (ECM composed primarily of glycosaminoglycans (GAGs)) [ 260 ]. Articular cartilage (AC) is a type of hyaline cartilage, a tough connective tissue that covers the surface of bones in joints. Approximately 30% of its volume is composed of chondrocytes, most of which are embedded into the ECM composed of type II collagen and proteoglycans secreted by chondrocytes [ 12 ]. Typically operating in extremely harsh environments, the knee joint has been estimated to experience peak contact stresses as high as 12.6 MPa [ 261 ]. In cartilage explant models, cell death and ECM damage are induced by stresses of 15 MPa or greater [ 262 ]. Long-term high pressure can easily cause cartilage tears. AC has limited repair capacity due to impaired cellular function, a low number of progenitor cells, and low cell migration. Moreover, cartilage tissue lacks blood vessels, arteries, and lymphatics, and its nutrient supply relies on diffusion through synovial fluid and subchondral bone, further hindering repair [ 263 ]. The ECM of AC exhibits spatial variations in organization and composition from the superficial to the deep layers. In the superficial layer, the concentration of type II collagen decreases, while the content of proteoglycans increases [ 12 ]. This region is acellular, and collagen fibers are aligned parallel to the joint surface. The middle and deep layers are composed of densely packed collagen fibers that are oriented perpendicular to the joint surface. A calcified cartilage layer separates the non-calcified deep layer from the subchondral bone, which is rich in type X collagen [ 12 ]. The polar structure of cartilage limits the repair effectiveness of simple isotropic scaffolds, while anisotropic scaffolds constructed from AESF scaffolds are more conducive to cartilage reconstruction. The AESF scaffolds maintain the physiological function of the meniscus by stabilizing its fibers [ 264 ]. Since 1977, the first TE approach has been described for AC repair [ 263 ]. With the development of cell therapy and tissue materials, cartilage TE has been increasingly studied as an alternative treatment option to autologous cartilage transplantation. Wu et al. prepared dmECM from fresh porcine menisci and mixed it with PLLA-CL for wet electrospinning to produce AESF scaffolds (Fig. 6 B). Scanning electron microscopy images revealed that the microstructure of the AESF scaffold was similar to the porcine meniscus. As a result, the scaffold promoted chondrogenic differentiation and collagen secretion of BMSCs and ADSCs [ 96 ]. Tendon and ligament TE The tendon structure is composed of neatly arranged fiber bundles, with collagen fiber bundles measuring 20 nm in diameter and tendon fiber bundles measuring 2–3 mm in diameter [ 24 ]. Tendons possess a multiscale, hierarchical structure of type Ⅰ collagen that supports their tri-phasic mechanical behavior [ 20 , 100 ]. Collagen fibrils have a diameter of 100–500 nm, collagen fibers 1–20 μm, subbundles 15–400 μm, and bundles 150–1000 μm [ 93 ]. The collagen fibers of the ECM of ligaments and tendons are arranged in bundles, exhibiting a wavy and aligned configuration in the central region of the tendon, followed by a region of aligned and flattened fibers and randomly aligned fibers at the attachment sites [ 114 ]. During ligament motion, the wavy regions of the ligament unfold first, dissipating the load, which is then transferred to the rest of the tissues and ultimately to the attachment sites and bone through ligament stretching [ 265 ]. This mechanical process produces a tri-phasic mechanical behavior: the wavy collagen fibers begin to lose their wavy behavior and gradually align, resulting in tissue strains of 2–5% at the end of the “toe zone.” This is followed by a “linear zone,” where the collagen fibers are straightened and provide ideal elastic recovery. Finally, a “yield zone” occurs, with slippage between fibers and progressive failure until the tendon ruptures [ 93 , 265 ]. Therefore, wave AESF scaffolds are often used to mimic the ligament structure for ligament repair [ 114 ]. Due to factors such as insufficient blood supply, reduced elasticity, weakened mechanical properties, and high-intensity exercise, tendons accumulate fatigue damage and are susceptible to rupture under high-intensity loads [ 202 ]. Globally, over 30 million tendon injuries occur annually, costing over $156 billion in medical costs [ 24 ]. Surgical suturing of the torn ends to re-establish tendon alignment is necessary after a tendon rupture [ 3 ]. Following surgery, tendon regeneration undergoes a gradual and prolonged process involving overlapping phases of hematoma and inflammation, cell proliferation and matrix deposition, and matrix remodeling. The fibrin hematoma formed after tendon rupture serves as a temporary chemotactic scaffold that recruits inflammatory cells to the injury site, which is crucial for initiating regeneration [ 93 ]. Subsequently, avoiding the proliferative phase of the chronic inflammatory response is essential to prevent scar tissue formation. The topology of the ECM secreted by fibroblasts provides physical cues guiding cell alignment and dictates the arrangement of collagen during the remodeling phase. Furthermore, while blood supply during the early phase (2 weeks) is responsible for cell survival, nonfunctional blood vessels degenerate during the remodeling process (6 weeks after injury), and the tendon matures and regains its mechanical properties [ 93 ]. Poor clinical outcomes after tendon rupture are primarily attributed to the production of disorganized, highly cellular scar tissue, which causes chronic pain, fails to meet the mechanical demands of the tendon, and increases the risk of reinjury [ 195 , 266 ]. To achieve complete reconstruction of tendon structure and function, tendon TE has been studied by researchers. Existing biological tendon reconstruction strategies still face problems: (1) lack of topological cues to induce orderly cell arrangement and collagen fiber deposition; (2) poor tensile strength, prone to early fracture; (3) difficulty in controlling the duration of active ingredients, for example, continued vascularization in the later stage often leads to scar tissue formation and reconstruction failure [ 93 , 114 , 266 ]. The topography of aligned fibers guides stem cells toward the tendon lineage. In Duprez et al.’s study, the aligned PU ESF scaffold induced tenogenic differentiation of MSCs (Fig. 6 C) [ 120 ]. The TPCs and tendon-derived stem cells (TSCs) co-cultured with PLLA/PEO wave ESF scaffold also expressed tendonogenic differentiation (Fig. 6 D) [ 114 ]. TE scaffolds prepared by photocrosslinking hydrogels combined with AESFs also showed the ability to promote tendon-oriented differentiation of ADSCs [ 267 ]. Braided scaffolds also showed a tendency to promote tendinous differentiation of MSCs [ 268 ]. Peritendinous adhesions are one of the most common complications after tendon injury. Following tendon rupture, an acute inflammatory response is initiated, lasting 3 to 7 days [ 87 ]. During this period, fibroblasts from the peritendinous microenvironment are recruited to proliferate and secrete type III collagen (COL III). Overactivated fibroblasts secrete large amounts of COL III, rapidly covering the damaged tendon area. However, the randomly distributed excess protein leads to adhesions between the tendon and surrounding tissues [ 87 ]. The AESF scaffold loaded with IBU, a kind of nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug, promoted tendon regeneration while preventing surrounding adhesions [ 87 ]. Nerve TE SCI is a severe central nervous system (CNS) disorder that can lead to motor and sensory dysfunction, severely impacting the quality of life of patients. More than 500,000 patients worldwide suffer from severe neurological impairment or even permanent paralysis due to SCI [ 45 ]. SCI triggers multiple pathological processes, such as ion imbalance, free radical production, excitotoxicity, lipid peroxidation, and inflammation. These events lead to neuronal loss, axonal demyelination, Wallerian degeneration, and scar tissue formation [ 254 , 269 ]. Moreover, due to the harsh microenvironment of SCI, most activated NSCs differentiate into astrocytes [ 255 ]. These pathological developments disrupt the architecture of the neural parenchyma, alter axonal networks, and modify the composition of spinal cord tissue, ultimately leading to catastrophic consequences for spinal cord function [ 108 ]. The spinal cord ECM is composed of a variety of collagens, proteoglycans, glycoproteins, and matrix-related components, which contribute to the normal physiological function of the spinal cord [ 108 ]. The Young’s modulus of the ECM of neural cells is 0.1–5 kPa, and brain tissue generally has a stiffness of approximately 0.5 kPa [ 9 , 28 ]. Spinal regeneration biomaterials should have properties that mimic the soft ECM of neural tissue to promote the spontaneous neural differentiation of endogenous and/or exogenous stem cells [ 28 , 45 ]. In the rat SCI model, the multilayer-aligned PCL/ poly(pdioxanone) ESF scaffold combined with NSCs improved spinal cord regeneration (Fig. 6 E) [ 255 ]. The peripheral nervous system (PNS) has a stronger regenerative capacity than the CNS, with a rate of up to 1 mm per day [ 270 ]. Furthermore, neuronal functional recovery is primarily driven by the proliferation of Schwann cells (SCs) and macrophages [ 9 ]. When nerve damage occurs, there are two main types of repair: if the nerve axons are intact, repair mainly relies on compensatory sprouting and regeneration and recovery of damaged axons; if axonal damage exceeds 90%, axonal regeneration is the main method for repairing damaged nerves [ 42 ]. In the PNS, despite promising regenerative capabilities, functional recovery at sites with significant length (&gt; 10 mm) is often poor due to insufficient axonal bridging at distal targets [ 9 ]. Autologous transplantation is currently considered the “gold standard” for peripheral nerve repair (PNR) and is used to bridge large lesions (20–50 mm) [ 91 ]. However, it is limited by the difficulty in obtaining donors, damage to the donor site, and so on. The parameters of therapeutic implantable biomaterials must meet the following criteria: First, they should have mechanical properties matching those of soft neural tissue, which can support cell attachment and form a cell barrier. Second, they should contain bioactive molecules that promote axonal regeneration and injury bridging. Finally, they should enhance electrical conductivity to support the growth and differentiation of neural cells [ 9 ]. Besides, nerve guides must maintain their mechanical integrity for at least three months to allow axonal regeneration within the lumen [ 173 ]. The aligned Polyacrylonitrile (PAN)/polyaniline (PANI)/CNTs ESF scaffold promoted neural differentiation of ADSCs. The NSC differentiation rate of PLLA nanofibers is higher than that of microfibers, and it is independent of fiber orientation [ 271 ]. In addition, in cells cultured on scaffolds with better conductivity, the expression levels of MAP2 and β-tubulin III, markers of late neural differentiation, were higher than those in cells seeded on scaffolds with poor conductivity [ 83 ]. Cardiac TE Cardiovascular disease (CVD) is one of the leading causes of death worldwide, claiming 17.3 million lives annually. Based on these statistics, it is estimated that by 2030, this number will reach approximately 23.6 million deaths annually, representing approximately 31% of the total global annual deaths [ 85 , 122 ]. The heart is a complex organ composed of multiple cell types, including cardiomyocytes (CMs), fibroblasts, endothelial cells, valvular interstitial cells, and cardiac stem cells. CMs, the primary functional cells, are relatively unique due to their high degree of anisotropy and continuous spontaneous contraction and relaxation [ 105 ]. Additionally, CMs are metabolically active and require large amounts of adenosine triphosphate (ATP) to maintain their physiological functions [ 122 ]. However, the heart lacks endogenous repair or regenerative potential; thus, any morphological defects or insufficient numbers of CMs can lead to cardiovascular complications associated with myocardial infarction. Native cardiac ECM is a dense, regular, and defect-free structure. The primary components of the myocardial ECM are type I collagen (89%) and type III collagen (11%), with myofibrils measuring approximately 100 nanometers in diameter. The myocardial ECM possesses excellent mechanical properties and stimulus conduction capabilities, interacting with active cardiomyocytes to achieve coordinated and continuous contraction and relaxation of the myocardial tissue. This anisotropic tissue structure is essential for the efficient pumping ability of the heart [ 18 , 122 ]. These properties indicate that the AESF scaffolds are suitable for cardiac TE. CMs derived from iPSCs exhibit a relatively immature phenotype in two-dimensional in vitro culture, characterized by inefficient calcium ion (Ca 2+ ) handling, small cell size, lack of T tubules, disorganized sarcomere structure, low contractile force, and fetal metabolic characteristics [ 154 ]. As shown in Tang’s study (2023), AESF scaffolds proved to be a cardiac ECM-mimetic culture medium for iPSCs, as they improved the cardiac differentiation of iPSCs and iPSC-CMs maturation in vitro [ 105 ]. To simulate the contraction and relaxation function of the myocardium, elastic polymers are suitable for making cardiac TE scaffolds. Polyglycerol sebacate (PGS) is a polymer widely used in soft TE, including cardiac applications. PGS is hydrophilic and biodegradable, with a degradation time of approximately 60 days in vivo, and its byproducts are naturally metabolized within the body. With a Young’s modulus of 0.025–1.2 MPa and a tensile strength of 0.20–0.5 MPa, PGS possesses mechanical properties close to those of myocardial tissue [ 18 , 122 ]. PU, as an elastomer, exhibits excellent mechanical properties such as tensile behavior, as well as good blood compatibility and biodegradability, making it a suitable choice for cardiac TE [ 121 , 126 , 127 ]. In addition to mechanical properties, electrical conductivity is also an important aspect to consider for cardiac TE scaffold materials. PANI has the advantages of simple synthesis, tunable electrical properties, environmental stability, and low production cost, and exhibits good biocompatibility both in vivo and in vitro [ 18 ] Although PANI is brittle [ 83 ], the elastic modulus of the PGS/PANI scaffolds under wet conditions ranges from 0.65 to 2.18 MPa, similar to the properties of native myocardium [ 18 ]. Adding rGO to the poly(L-lactide-co-ε-caprolactone) (PLCL), which has high elasticity, can increase the conductivity of PLCL. The iPSC-CMs cultured on aligned rGO/PLCL ESF scaffolds exhibited an ordered structure, excellent mechanical properties, antioxidant properties, and effective guidance (Fig. 6 F) [ 15 ]. Interface TE Interface tissue is the transitional connective tissue between two different musculoskeletal tissue types and is found throughout the body. Common interface tissue types include cartilage–bone (also known as osteocartilage), tendon–bone (also known as enthesis), ligament–bone, muscle–tendon, and meniscus-bone interfaces [ 7 ]. Rotator cuff tears, primarily injuries to the suprascapularis tendon–bone attachment, are a common interface repair target and have a high prevalence (approximately 34%). Over 1.1 million rotator cuff tendon surgeries are performed worldwide each year [ 272 ]. Surgical reattachment of the tendon to bone results in poor reintegration and a high failure rate, ranging from 20% to 95% [ 7 , 103 ]. The enthesis is a transitional region that exhibits gradients in structure, composition, biofunction, and biomechanical properties [ 273 ]. However, the formation of highly disorganized fibrovascular scar tissue that exhibits poor biomechanical properties at the tendon-bone interface, rather than the regeneration of the gradient fibrocartilage transition zone after surgery, is the primary reason for the high re-treatment rate [ 272 ]. A modular approach to spatially controlling fiber alignment using magnetically assisted electrospinning has been reported. ESFs were highly aligned in the presence of a magnetic field and smoothly transitioned to randomly aligned fibers away from the magnetic field (Fig. 6 G) [ 7 ]. Magnetic-assisted electrospinning enables spatial control of fiber arrangement at submillimeter resolution, facilitating the construction of ESF scaffolds for interfacial TE with gradients similar to native structures. Muscle TE Skeletal muscle accounts for approximately 30%-45% of the total human body weight, and its primary function is to convert chemical energy into mechanical energy [ 274 ]. Muscles are able to repair and regenerate in response to any injury [ 275 ]. Physiological repair of skeletal muscle involves basal lamina and satellite cells, which can proliferate and differentiate to generate or repair muscle fibers [ 256 ]. However, when muscle loss exceeds 20%, also known as volumetric muscle loss (VML), the skeletal muscle loses its ability to regenerate [ 102 , 276 ]. This is because, in VML, extensive tissue and vascular loss hinders the regenerative capacity of skeletal muscle, resulting in chronic tissue loss, strength deficits, and disability [ 102 , 277 ]. Currently, the gold standard treatment strategy for VML is autologous muscle or fasciocutaneous flaps transplantation [ 276 ]. This treatment has various limitations, such as tissue availability, the inability of transplanted muscle flaps to restore lost function, postoperative infection, scar tissue formation, and donor site morbidity [ 275 ]. A possible solution to this challenge is to construct tissue-engineered muscle to fill the VML and promote muscle regeneration in situ. An ideal scaffold for treating VML should exhibit stiffness similar to that of skeletal muscle, with a Young’s modulus of approximately 5–15 kPa [ 278 ]. In addition, there are four main goals for an ideal TE scaffold for skeletal muscle regeneration: (1) promote cell alignment, (2) promote functional muscle regeneration, (3) stimulate vascularization and innervation, and (4) prevent scar tissue formation [ 102 ]. Mature muscle cells (skeletal muscle fibers), called myotubes, must be aligned so that intracellular contractions can propagate along the long axis of the muscle, resulting in directed contraction [ 279 ]. Subsequently, the forces generated by the muscle fibers are transmitted to the surrounding connective tissue via type I and type III collagen fibers, with the basal lamina serving as an intermediate carrier [ 280 ]. As a result, the AESF scaffold is an ideal choice for skeletal muscle TE. GDF11 promotes the myogenic differentiation of myoblasts (Mb) and ADSCs co-cultured on aligned PCL/collagen/PEO ESF scaffolds [ 104 ]. Aligned PCL/collagen/PEO ESFs are a suitable matrix for three-dimensional (3D) myogenic differentiation of Mb and ADSCs [ 104 ]. A study presented the engineering of a biomimetic muscle construct with aligned PCL ESF scaffolds and induced myogenic progenitor cells (iMPCs). The results showed that aligned PCL fibers are fabricated to facilitate iMPC differentiation into aligned myotubes, and the differentiation capacity of iMPC-derived skeletal muscle constructs (iSMCs) is augmented by Matrigel supplementation (Fig. 6 H) [ 256 ]. Skin TE The ECM of the skin, primarily located in the dermis, consists of collagen fibers arranged in a reticular, basket-like structure. The skin comprises two distinct layers: the epidermis and the dermis, with the epidermis having a lower regenerative capacity compared to the dermis [ 12 ]. The wound healing process typically involves four interrelated phases: hemostasis, inflammation, proliferation, and re-epithelialization or remodeling [ 109 , 281 ]. However, factors such as diabetes, vascular insufficiency, impaired nutrition and immune status, infection, and burns can disrupt the wound microenvironment, leading to persistent hyperinflammation, high oxidative stress, and decreased cellular function, hindering wound healing [ 282 , 283 ]. Because of the double-layer structure of skin and the complex wound healing process, simple AESF scaffolds are not enough to completely change the wound environment and promote wound healing. Incorporating stem cells can regulate the wound microenvironment and assist AESFs in promoting wound healing. For instance, ADSCs were seeded on SF AESF scaffolds, which were cocultured with normal human dermal fibroblasts (NHDFs), and improved the migration of NHDFs to improve wound healing [ 26 ]. Other TE The periodontium is a complex tissue characterized by its sandwich structure, consisting of the alveolar bone (AB), periodontal ligament (PDL), and cementum. PDL fibers tightly connect the AB and cementum, providing support and stability to the tooth [ 196 ]. Periodontal defects due to periodontitis and other causes are a major concern. Autologous tissue transplantation is a method of periodontal tissue reconstruction. The elastic modulus of native gingival tissue is approximately 37.4 MPa. The most common autologous transplant tissues are human buccal mucosa and hard palate tissue, which have elastic moduli of 8.3 MPa and 18.1 MPa, respectively [ 193 ]. The unsatisfactory therapeutic effect of autologous transplantation requires the development of other treatment strategies. Periodontal TE has attracted much attention due to its excellent ability to regenerate periodontal tissue. The effectiveness of PDLSCs in repairing periodontium tissue has been verified in animal and human trials and is an important source of seed cells for periodontal TE [ 113 ]. An aligned-random-aligned ESF scaffold seeded with BMSCs and PDLSCs had been developed to recover the three-layer structure of periodontal tissue. The aligned PCL/Gel ESF layers loaded with PDLSCs and BMSCs served as the PDL layer and bone layer, respectively, with an interporous layer of randomly distributed fibers inserted between them. Alcian red staining and immunofluorescence staining results demonstrated that BMSCs and PDLSCs had proliferative and differentiating behaviors and desired cell-specific ECMs (Fig. 6 I) [ 196 ]. Stem cell-assisted AESF scaffolds have also been used for corneal repair to address the challenge of insufficient donors for corneal transplantation. Aligned Gel nanofibers were produced by electrospinning, cross-linked using glutaraldehyde vapor, coated with hyaluronic acid, and finally cross-linked by 1-Ethyl-3-(3-dimethylaminopropyl) carbodiimide (EDC)/N-Hydroxysuccinimide (NHS) to fabricate human corneal MSCs (CMSCs) delivery scaffolds for treating deep corneal lesions. The scaffold promoted CMSC proliferation and migration while having a light transmittance of up to 93%, successfully promoting corneal wound healing in an in vitro model [ 4 ]. In short, stem cells-assisted AESF scaffolds and AESF-based composite TE scaffolds have been used for TE regeneration in various tissues and have shown promising potential in promoting in situ tissue regeneration. However, most studies are limited to in vitro experiments, and in vivo experiments primarily utilize small animal models, lacking data from large animal studies. Copyright 2023, Elsevier Summary and perspectives Summary and shortcomings AESF scaffolds have a structure similar to ECM collagen, especially the ECM of anisotropic tissues such as muscle, tendon, and cortical bone. Their biomimetic structure is conducive to guiding the elongation, migration, proliferation, and differentiation of stem cells, thereby making the regenerated tissue highly anisotropic. The AESF scaffolds can be prepared by the high-speed roll receiver, the wet spinning method, and SJES; however, these methods only consider the scaffold morphology, and their preparation efficiency can only meet the needs of laboratory preparation. For example, the productivity of a single-needle laboratory-scale electrospinning device is low (0.01–2 g/h) [ 284 ], which is not suitable for large-scale production of the AESF scaffolds. Stem cells such as NSCs, ADSCs, ESCs, and BMSCs have been loaded onto AESF scaffolds for various tissue repair and regeneration applications. CE technology allows for simultaneous loading of stem cells onto AESFs during fiber fabrication; however, the high biocompatibility requirements for spinning polymers and solvents limit its application [ 285 , 286 ]. Coculturing stem cells with prepared AESF scaffolds in vitro is the most commonly used stem cell seeding approach. However, this procedure has several limitations: it reduces the efficiency of stem cell-assisted AESF scaffold preparation, lacks standardization in manufacturing protocols, creates contamination risks, and only allows stem cells to be seeded on the scaffold surface [ 196 ]. While implanting the AESF scaffold at the site of a tissue defect, transplanting stem cells into the host is another approach to combining stem cells with the scaffold. However, this approach directly exposes the stem cells to the host’s damaged tissue microenvironment, reducing the protective effect of the scaffold on the stem cells. Furthermore, the application of stem cell-assisted AESF scaffolds in TE remains confined to the laboratory stage, with no substantive progress toward clinical translation. This gap is attributable to several critical limitations. First, preclinical models are inadequate: there is a notable absence of long-term safety and efficacy data derived from large animal studies. Second, stem cell-related ethical and biological concerns persist: critical questions regarding tumorigenicity, immunogenicity, and cell sourcing remain unresolved, compounded by the lack of harmonized ethical standards. Third, scaffold manufacturing lacks standardization: current fabrication methods are insufficiently standardized, and significant challenges remain in scaling up scaffold production and consistent integration with stem cells. Future directions and clinical translation The translation of stem cell-assisted AESF scaffolds into clinical in-situ tissue regeneration will necessitate scalable manufacturing processes, automated cell-seeding protocols, and well-defined ethical and regulatory frameworks. Large-scale electrospinning and autologous cell culture Scalable ESF production can be achieved by increasing jet output through modified single needles with a grooved tip [ 284 , 287 ], roll-to-roll electrospinning [ 288 , 289 ] (Fig. 7 A), multi-needle electrospinning [ 290 ], and nozzleless (free-surface) electrospinning [ 291 ]. Velásquez-García et al. designed a novel, low-cost, and modular miniaturized nanofiber electrospinning source, which has multiple needles on the same module. With this electrospinning source, PEO AESFs were collected by a rotating drum collector (Fig. 7 B) [ 292 ]. A high-throughput centrifugal electrospinning (HTP-CES) system was developed by Zhang et al. to produce high-yield AESFs. Notably, this design allowed the simultaneous production of AESFs at 102 collection sites. There was a thin-wire electrode configuration of the collector, hence nearly all the ejected polymer was deposited in the electrode gap with a production yield of nearly 75% (Fig. 7 C) [ 293 ]. A variety of needleless electrospinning heads have been developed. They can be categorized into two types based on spinning conditions: rotary spinnerets and stationary spinnerets. Rotary spinnerets have a spiral coil, a rotary cone, a sprocket wheel disk, a needle-disk, a rotating-disk, or a self-cleaning threaded rod. Stationary spinnerets include twisted wire, conical wire coil, metal dish, and tube [ 284 , 294 ]. A double-ring slit needleless spinneret (DRSNS), as a kind of stationary spinneret, was developed for large-scale facilitated PLLA AESF membranes as a drug delivery vehicle (Fig. 7 D) [ 294 ]. These applications of large-scale electrospinning demonstrate the potential for fabricating AESF scaffolds. However, the sterilization of AESF scaffolds, the assembly of complex scaffold structures, and stem cell seeding remain unresolved issues. Adding environmental monitoring and control modules to ensure a stable spinning environment, and adding solvent concentration detection modules to monitor residual solvent in the AESF scaffold, are also necessary. Finite element analysis can be used to predict mechanical properties, while molecular dynamics simulation can predict fiber degradation and drug release. Machine learning is expected to be used to predict the stretching frequency or orientation of AESF scaffolds in regulating stem cell activity, thereby modulating cell reactor parameters. From this angle, developing large-scale CE technology presents a promising research direction. Scaling up the CE process for the industrial production of stem cell-assisted AESFs requires further research on spinning solution materials and process parameters. Copyright 2019, American Chemical Society Clinical translation of stem cell-assisted aligned electrospun nanofiber scaffolds While stem cell-assisted AESF scaffolds have shown potential in various tissue regeneration applications, no related clinical trials have been registered or initiated to date. The clinical trials of stem cell-assisted AESF scaffolds can refer to the clinical trial conducted by Fesharaki’s team in 2021, published in International Immunopharmacology , on stem cell-assisted RESF scaffolds for the treatment of diabetic foot ulcers (DFUs) [ 298 ]. In this research, human placenta-derived mesenchymal stem cells (hPDMSCs) were loaded onto Gel RESF scaffolds with or without platelet-rich plasma (PRP) onto the DFUs to evaluate the effectiveness of the hPDMSC-loaded Gel RESF scaffold. The trial included 28 patients (10 women and 18 men) who were simply randomized into 3 groups: 11 patients in group A were treated with Gel RESFs seeded with hPDMSCs; 10 patients in group B were treated with Gel RESFs seeded with hPDMSCs after coating PRP onto DFUs; and 7 patients in group C received standard therapy [ 298 ]. After 12 weeks, the area of DFUs in groups A, B, and C reduced 66%, 71%, and 36%, which indicated hPDMSC-assisted RESFs significantly promote DFU healing [ 298 ]. However, the study included a small number of patients, and the specific mechanism by which it promotes DFU healing has not yet been verified. While research on stem cell-assisted AESF scaffolds is still hovering on the verge of clinical application, stem cell-assisted hydrogel scaffolds have already entered the field. For cartilage TE, Park et al. loaded allogeneic human UCMSCs on hyaluronic acid hydrogels to treat osteoarthritis and proved the safety and effectiveness of the stem cell-based medicinal products [ 299 ]. In the clinical trial NCT05967325 that began in 2023, stromal vascular fraction, a cell mixture primarily comprising stem cells, combined with functional self-assembling peptide nanofiber hydrogels, was used to treat chronic complete thoracic spinal cord injuries. The phase II clinical trial ChiCTR2400094739 showed that PDMSC-loaded hydrogels have significant potential for accelerating the repair of radiation-induced skin damage and reducing skin pain [ 300 ]. Most of the above clinical trials used perinatal stem cells, such as UCMSCs and PDMSCs, which have low immunogenicity and are readily available, reducing the risk of immune rejection after stem cell transplantation. Autologous stem cells avoid the problem of immune rejection. In this case, the abundance and ease of obtaining are the main factors influencing the choice of stem cell source. Besides immune rejection, tumorigenesis and oncogenicity are also among the main safety concerns to be considered in the field of stem cell-assisted AESF scaffolds promoting tissue regeneration. Shi et al. found that stem cells have the risk of malignant transformation into tumor cells under physiological conditions, which is attributed to the accumulation of chromosomal abnormalities [ 301 ]. Since then, numerous studies have shown that multipotent stem cells, such as ESCs and iPSCs, carry a risk of tumorigenesis in vivo [ 302 , 303 ]. However, the exact mechanism of stem cell tumorigenesis remains unclear. The risk of tumorigenic transformation should be minimized through measures such as pre-transplant genetic testing and morphological analysis of stem cells, limiting the number of in vitro stem cell expansion cycles, and using non-invasive imaging techniques to monitor tissue regeneration after transplantation. Autologous adult stem cells, such as ADSCs and BMSCs, have ethical advantages. On the one hand, they are obtained with the informed consent of the donor patients, and on the other hand, they are not genetically edited and are derived from non-embryonic sources. Conversely, the clinical application of ESCs and iPSCs faces significant ethical controversies. The acquisition process for ESCs involves the destruction of human embryos that are considered morally equivalent to humans. As early as 1991, Germany’s Embryo Protection Act reflected widespread concerns about the commercialization of embryos [ 304 ]. Since then, the tension between clinical efficacy and ethical restrictions has persisted in the clinical application of stem cells in various countries. At the same time, iPSCs face the societal implications of creating artificial human cells or even human clones [ 305 ]. With the development of gene-editing tools such as CRISPR-Cas9, stem cell therapy has ushered in new opportunities and challenges. The CRISPR (clustered regular-spaced short palindromic repeats) system was first discovered in bacteria and is a natural viral defense mechanism [ 306 ]. Jinek et al. demonstrated the potential of CRISPR-associated protein 9 (Cas9) as a programmable gene editing tool. Guide RNA is a component of the system that guides the Cas9 enzyme into a specific DNA region where a double-strand break is achieved [ 307 ]. Subsequently, the cellular repair process either uses homology-guided repair to achieve precise editing or introduces mutations using non-homologous end joining [ 307 ]. Complex gene modification can be achieved due to the ability of CRISPR-Cas9 to target multiple genes simultaneously, low cost, simple design, and high efficiency. While this technology has unique advantages, its ethical issues cannot be ignored. Given the safety and ethical concerns surrounding stem cell therapy, the use of stem cell-assisted AESF scaffolds in promoting tissue regeneration requires strict ethical standards and regulatory oversight. Establishing standards for the safety, efficacy, and consistency of stem cell-assisted AESF scaffolds for tissue regeneration depends on cooperation among researchers, physicians, and regulatory agencies.
