Introduction The specification of neural cell fates relies on coordinated interactions among chromatin accessibility, transcriptional activity, and cellular proliferation [ 1 – 5 ]. Neural progenitor cell (NPC) differentiation into neurons and glia is guided by lineage transcription factors (TFs) and histone modifiers that coordinate gene expression in a temporally regulated manner [ 6 – 14 ]. KMT2D is a catalytic component of the COMPASS complex that deposits H3K4me1/2 at enhancers and H3K4me3 at promoters, while also recruiting co-activators and chromatin readers to establish open chromatin states and support TF binding during development [ 15 – 20 ]. Heterozygous loss-of-function variants in KMT2D cause Kabuki Syndrome Type 1 (KS1), a congenital multisystem disorder characterised by intellectual disability and developmental delay [ 21 – 27 ]. In mice, deletion of the catalytic SET-domain disrupts KMT2D’s methyltransferase activity, leading to reduced H3K4me3 levels, impaired adult neurogenesis, and deficits in learning and memory, underscoring the importance of the catalytic domain during both development and adulthood [ 15 , 28 ]. At the molecular level, these neurodevelopmental defects have been linked to dysregulation of hypoxic response and neural maturation gene expression, resulting in precocious differentiation of hippocampal dentate gyrus progenitors. Further, additional studies demonstrate that SET-null variants can retain certain functions, such as maintaining stem cell identity, through its functional noncatalytic domains, highlighting a broader importance of KMT2D’s non-catalytic roles in development and disease [ 17 , 29 , 30 ]. Therefore, although KMT2D is clearly essential for brain development [ 15 , 25 , 28 , 31 ], its role in mediating the earliest stages of human neural fate decisions is not yet fully understood. Human iPSC-derived models provide a powerful system to capture the earliest stages of human neurodevelopment [ 32 – 35 ]. Here we interrogate the fundamental roles of KMT2D in early neural induction by modelling homozygous deficiency using hiPSC-derived cerebral organoids and directed differentiations of neural progenitors. To examine how KMT2D shapes the chromatin and transcriptional landscape during neural fate acquisition, we generated a CRISPR-engineered KMT2D knockout (i.e., KMT2D −/− ) hiPSC line. To evaluate KMT2D-dosage-sensitive molecular phenotypes, we also incorporated a KS1 patient-derived KMT2D +/− hiPSC line as an important point of clinical relevance to compare between a complete knockout and a heterozygous loss-of-function state. In both models, KMT2D deficiency led to rapid, but unstable, activation of lineage transcription factors and reduced efficiency of fate commitment, with milder yet consistent effects in the heterozygous patient-derived line. This work provides an integrated single-cell multiomic view of KMT2D-deficient neural progenitors and demonstrates how KMT2D loss disrupts the integration of key developmental programmes at the onset of fate specification, suggesting that the neurodevelopmental phenotypes of KMT2D loss may derive not solely from differences in cell identity, but also from impaired coordination of when and how effectively those identities are established. Materials and methods Stem cell culture The research described in this study complies with all relevant ethical regulations. De-identified patient-derived hiPSCs were provided by the Bodamer Lab at Boston Children’s Hospital. The KMT2D +/− patient hiPSC line harbours a heterozygous insertion mutation in exon 31 of KMT2D, resulting in a premature stop codon in a region previously identified as a mutation hotspot [ 24 , 110 – 113 ]. The KMT2D −/− line was generated at Gladstone Institutes by CRISPR/Cas9-mediated introduction of a premature stop codon in exon 2 of the KMT2D gene. To identify the most efficient editing strategy, human WTC iPSCs were transfected with 3–4 sgRNA-Cas9 RNP complexes, and pools showing the highest predicted knockout efficiency were selected for clonal expansion. Up to 24 single-cell colonies from two independent experiments were screened using PCR genotyping and Sanger sequencing (primers: Forward GCTGGCTGGTGAGGATAAAG, Reverse ACACGA GAGACATTGACGCC) and evaluated with an ICE KO analysis, prioritising clones with homozygous knockout scores &gt; 60%. Confirmed positives underwent a second round of subcloning to generate purer clonal populations, from which two independent homozygous knockout clones (i.e., KMT2D.5 and KMT2D.10) were expanded and validated. Final QC included ICE KO scores &gt; 80% with clean Sanger traces, absence of mycoplasma, expression of pluripotency markers, and normal karyotype. All hiPSC lines used in this work (WTC11 [Gladstone Institutes, UCSFi001-A], KMT2D −/− knockout, KMT2D-corrected (patient isogenic control), and KMT2D +/− (patient-derived line)) were maintained on Matrigel (Corning, #354277) coated 6 well plates (Corning, #3516) in mTeSR Plus medium (STEMCELL Technologies, #100–0276) supplemented with Primocin (InvivoGen, #ant-pm-05) and cultured in a humidified 37 °C incubator with 5% CO 2 with daily media changes. Once cultures reached 70–80% confluency, cells were passaged using ReLeSR (STEMCELL Technologies, #100–0483). Genomic stability of cells was evaluated using the KaryoStat + Assay service provided by Thermo Fisher Scientific. No aberrations were detected aside from the expected Y chromosome deletion that has been previously reported from the donor of WTC11 [ 36 ]. Cerebral organoid generation Cerebral organoids were generated using the protocol developed by the Shcheglovitov laboratory [ 37 , 38 ]. The WTC11 and KMT2D −/− hiPSCs were maintained in mTeSR Plus media (STEMCELL Technologies, #100–0276) supplemented with Primocin (InvivoGen, #ant-pm-05) and cultured in a humidified 37 °C incubator with 5% CO 2 with daily media changes until 100% confluent. To initiate neuroectodermal differentiation, the medium was switched to a 1:1 mixture of N2 medium (DMEM/F12 (Gibco, #11965–092), N2 supplement (Gibco, #17502048), MEM-NEAA (Gibco, #11140), heparin (STEMCELL Technologies, #09780, 2 μg/mL), and Penicillin/Streptomycin (Gibco, #15140122)) and B-27 medium (Neurobasal-A (Gibco, #10888022), B27 supplement (Gibco, #17504044), GlutaMAX (Gibco, 35050061), and Penicillin/Streptomycin (Gibco, #15140122)), further supplemented with the dual SMAD pathway inhibitors Dorsomorphin (Selleckchem, #S7306, 4 μM) and SB431542 (Selleckchem, #S1067, 10 μM) and incubated for 7–10 days. On Day 7–10, the confluent neuroepithelial cell sheets formed during neural induction (as referenced in Stage 3 Step 27 (38)) were detached and transferred onto Matrigel (Corning, #354277) coated 60mm dishes (Fisher, #FB0875713A) and cultured in neural induction medium containing EGF (STEMCELL Technologies, #78006, 10 ng/mL) and FGF (STEMCELL Technologies, #78003, 10 ng/mL) for another 7–11 days with daily media changes. By Day 14, neural rosette structures began to form and were manually isolated using a glass hook and seeded into ultra-low attachment 96-well U-bottom plates (Corning, #7007). These rosettes were cultured for an additional 14 days on an orbital shaker (Fisher, #88861021, 45 rpm) at 37 °C with 5% CO 2 for further maturation. By day 30, rosettes were then transferred to uncoated 10-cm Petri dishes (Fisher, #FB0875712) in neural induction medium lacking EGF/FGF (either embedded or supplemented with 1% Matrigel; Corning, #354234) and maintained under continuous shaking at 37 °C and 5% CO 2 for two months to promote cerebral organoid development. Dataset-specific protocol modifications are as follows: Organoids for all sequencing datasets were generated using the telencephalic protocol described by Ullah et al [ 38 ]. For the Multiome sequencing experiment, individual neural rosettes were isolated, grown to 1-month organoids in 96-well plates, and then embedded in Matrigel droplets, where they matured into ~2-month organoids before nuclei isolation ( Figure S7 ). For the Parse fixed-cell sequencing experiment, neural rosettes were isolated and grown to 1-month organoids in 96-well plates but were subsequently transferred to Petri dishes and maintained in medium containing 1% dissolved Matrigel rather than embedded droplets. All other media compositions, timing, and maturation conditions were identical to those described above. Multiome sequencing sample preparation For each genotype, cerebral organoids were pooled based on size and dissociated into single-cell suspensions using a papain-based enzymatic digestion protocol. The papain solution was prepared by adding 28 mg of papain (Millipore Sigma, #9001734) into 30 mL HBSS (Gibco, #14025092) with N -acetyl L-cysteine (Sigma-Aldrich, #A7250). The trypsin inhibitor solution was prepared by adding 180 mg trypsin inhibitor (Sigma Aldrich, #9035818) in 30 mL HBSS (Gibco, #14025092) with BSA (Sigma-Aldrich, #A7030). Trypsin inhibitor was used to neutralise papain activity, not trypsin, as phytocistatins are known to inhibit cysteine proteases such as papain [ 39 ], as described in previous work [ 40 ] and in the original organoid protocol (Downstream Assays Option A Step iv or Option B Step ix) [ 38 ]. Both were dissolved at 37 °C for 10 minutes, pH-adjusted to 7.4, sterile filtered through a 0.2 μM filter unit (Fisher, #5640020), and aliquoted for storage at −20 °C. Papain and trypsin inhibitor solutions were warmed to 37 °C, and DNase I (Thermo Scientific,#EN0521) was thawed on ice. DNase I was added to the trypsin inhibitor solution at a 1:20 dilution (e.g., 50 μL of DNase I in 1 mL of trypsin inhibitor solution). Organoids were transferred into 1.5 mL tubes containing approximately 1 mL of pre-warmed papain solution and were gently mixed and incubated at 37 °C for 20–30 minutes. The papain solution was then removed and replaced with ~500 μL of trypsin inhibitor solution containing DNase I. Samples were incubated at 37 °C for an additional 10 minutes. Following enzymatic treatment, organoids were gently triturated using sequential P1000, P200, and P20 pipette tips to promote dissociation while minimising cell damage. The resulting suspension was passed through a 40 μm pre-wetted cell strainer (Corning, #431750) to remove debris. Cells were pelleted by centrifugation at 1000 rpm for 7 minutes at 4 °C. Most of the supernatant was removed, and the pellet was resuspended in 100 μL of ice-cold 0.04% sterile-filtered BSA in DPBS (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #14190144). Suspensions were kept on ice and transported to the core facility for downstream applications. Nuclei isolation and library preparation Nuclei were isolated using a modified version of the 10X Genomics nuclei isolation protocol, adapted from embryonic mouse brain preparations (10X Genomics, CG000365 Rev C). First, dissociated organoid suspensions were filtered sequentially through a 70 μm cell strainer (Corning, #431751) and a 40 μm Flowmi strainer (Sigma, #BAH136800040) to remove debris and large aggregates. The filtered cell suspension was centrifuged at 500 rcf for 5 minutes at 4 °C, and the pellet was resuspended in chilled hypotonic lysis buffer, mixed by pipetting, and incubated on ice for 5 minutes to release nuclei. Following lysis, cells were washed three times in chilled wash buffer with centrifugation at 500 rcf for 5 minutes between washes. Nuclei were then resuspended in diluted nuclei buffer, passed through a 40 μm Flowmi strainer (Sigma, #BAH136800040) if debris remained, and quantified using a Countess II FL Automated Cell Counter. Approximately 5,000 nuclei per sample were targeted for capture, and nuclei were loaded into the Chromium Next GEM Single Cell Multiome platform (10X Genomics) for simultaneous ATAC and transcriptomic profiling. A sequencing depth of 200 million reads per sample was specified for both snATAC-seq and scRNA-seq libraries. FASTQ files were generated using Cell Ranger ARC v2.0.0 (10X Genomics), aligned to the GRCh38 human genome reference, and preprocessed using Seurat v4 and Signac v1.6. Cells with fewer than 800 detected genes or a high proportion of mitochondrial transcripts (&gt; 20%) were excluded. These thresholds remove low-quality or dying cells that could otherwise obscure biological signal. Following dimensionality reduction with PCA and WNN integration, data were visualised via UMAP and clustered using the Louvain algorithm. Cluster identities were annotated using lineage marker gene expression and ATAC accessibility, ensuring that the clusters correspond to biologically meaningful cell populations. Detailed methods on processing the snATAC and snRNA sequencing data are described below. Multiome snATAC analysis Single-nucleus ATAC-seq datasets from four samples each from two timepoints (Day 51 and Day 74) were merged, with unique cell identifiers assigned to each sample. Gene annotations were retrieved from EnsDb.Hsapiens.v86, and added to the Seurat object. Cell-level quality control metrics, including nucleosome signal, transcription start site (TSS) enrichment, fraction of reads in peaks, and blacklist ratio, were computed using Signac. Nuclei were retained if they had between 2,000 and 40,000 peak region fragments, &gt; 5% of reads in peaks, nucleosome signal &lt; 10, and TSS enrichment &gt; 2. These metrics ensure that retained cells exhibit genuine regulatory patterns and balance the removal of low-quality or damaged nuclei while retaining sufficient biological diversity for downstream analysis. These standard thresholds are indicators of intact chromatin structure (low nucleosome signal), active promoter accessibility (high TSS enrichment), and reliable peak detection (adequate fragment counts and fraction in peaks). Chromatin accessibility data were normalised using TF-IDF to account for differences in sequencing depth and feature frequency, ensuring comparability across cells. The top features were then reduced by singular value decomposition (LSI), which emphasises biologically relevant variation in accessibility patterns. UMAP was subsequently applied to LSI components 2–30 to visualise major cellular relationships while excluding the first component, which often captures technical noise rather than biological signal [ 41 , 42 ]. Clustering was performed using the shared nearest neighbour (SNN) graph and the Louvain algorithm with multiple resolutions. Sample identities were visualised on UMAPs for downstream comparison. Gene activity scores were computed to estimate promoter accessibility-driven gene expression, log-normalised, and incorporated as a new assay within the Seurat object for subsequent analyses. This step provides a functional bridge between open chromatin and gene expression potential, allowing us to link accessibility changes to downstream transcriptional changes [ 43 ]. Multiome snRNA analysis Gene expression matrices from single-nucleus RNA-sequencing were generated using Cell Ranger and imported with Seurat v4. Four samples each from two timepoints (Day 51 and Day 74) were merged into a single Seurat object, with metadata columns denoting sample identity, timepoint, and genotype. Initial quality control excluded barcodes with fewer than 200 detected genes to remove potential low-quality cells and empty droplets. The percentage of mitochondrial transcripts per cell was calculated and visualised prior to and after filtering to assess cell quality and remove likely doublets and degraded cells. Only genes expressed in at least two cells were retained for downstream analyses to reduce noise from spurious barcodes or sequencing errors while preserving genes with biologically meaningful expression. Data were normalised and log-transformed using SCTransform, regressing out the percentage of mitochondrial reads to control for cell degradation. Highly variable features were identified, and linear dimensionality reduction was performed using principal component analysis (PCA). Genes contributing most to the first nine principal components were examined, and elbow plots were used to inform selection of principal components for subsequent steps. Clustering was performed using the Louvain algorithm on the PCA graph, and clusters were visualised with UMAPs based on the top 20 principal components. Marker genes relevant to the experimental system and cell identities were pre-specified and their expression was examined to assist in annotation. Marker-guided annotation approach ensures that clusters correspond to biologically interpretable cell populations. Cell cycle phase for each cell was determined by scoring established S and G2/M gene signatures, classifying each cell as G1, S, or G2/M, and visualising distributions across clusters. Multiome data processing and integration Single-nucleus multiome (snRNA-seq + snATAC-seq) data were processed with Seurat and Signac. To capture the shared chromatin landscape necessary for integration across genotypes and timepoints, RNA counts and ATAC fragments were loaded for each sample and timepoint. Our dataset comprised of ~2B ATAC sequenced read pairs and ~3B GEX sequenced reads pairs, yielding 24,260 cells, with an average of 0.85 ATAC fraction of high-quality fragments in cells and 7159 GEX median UMI counts per cell. A union peak set was created by merging peaks from all samples, retaining standard chromosomal peaks between 20 and 10,000 bp. This step is necessary to compare accessibility profiles are compared across a consistent set of regulatory regions, avoiding artifacts from extremely short or long peaks, and captures the shared chromatin landscape necessary for integration across genotypes and timepoints. Chromatin accessibility matrices were constructed for each cell, and gene annotations were assigned using EnsDb.Hsapiens.v86 (hg38). After merging all samples, cells with fewer than 200 detected genes or greater than 25% mitochondrial transcripts were excluded. Quality control metrics, including gene and peak counts per cell and mitochondrial percentage, were visualised before and after filtering. To merge the RNA and ATAC data, we performed weighted nearest neighbour (WNN) analysis [ 43 ], which integrates both transcriptomic (PCA) and chromatin accessibility (LSI) dimensions to jointly define cell neighbourhoods, which are groups of cells that share similar expression and chromatin profiles. This approach provides more accurate cell state representation than single-modality or concatenation-based methods by balancing information from both omics layers at the single-cell level [ 43 ]. UMAP was performed on the WNN object to visualise the integrated cellular landscape. Clustering was conducted using the Louvain/Leiden algorithm on the WNN-based neighbourhood plot, and results were visualised by genotype and cluster across UMAP embeddings. FigR analysis FigR analysis was performed following the pipeline described by the Buenrostro lab ( https://buenrostrolab.github.io/FigR/ ), with adjustments for single-cell multiome data. For each genotype, we subset the multiome data to progenitor cell clusters based on established annotations. Chromatin peak-gene correlations were calculated using FigR’s runGenePeakcorr() function, which evaluates associations between chromatin accessibility peaks (± 10 kb from gene TSSs) and gene expression using a permutation-based background ( n = 100 permutations, p ≤ 0.05). ATAC data were converted to a SummarizedExperiment object and paired with normalised RNA expression matrices (SCT assay). Genes associated with at least 7 significant peaks were classified as DORCs using dorcJPlot(). To reduce technical noise from biologically similar neighbouring cells, cell-level DORC scores were smoothed via a k-nearest neighbours (k = 30) graph derived from PCA embeddings to account for local cell state structure. This reduces technical noise by incorporating information from biologically similar neighbouring cells. To define transcription factor (TF)-DORC regulatory networks, smoothed DORC scores and gene expression matrices were used as input for the runFigRGRN() function, and TF-DORC associations were filtered to include genes with ≥ 7 linked peaks and absolute regulatory scores &gt; 1. Key TF regulators were identified and visualised using rankDrivers() and plotfigRHeatmap(). Mutlivelo analysis Multivelo analysis was performed following the pipeline described by the Welch lab ( https://multivelo.readthedocs.io/en/latest/ ). The snRNAseq and snATACseq datasets were preprocessed separately using Scanpy (1.11) and scVelo (0.2.5) workflows. For snRNA-seq, cells with 800–30,000 total RNA counts were retained; for snATAC-seq, cells with 2,000–60,000 peak counts were retained. Barcodes and genes present in both modalities were intersected, yielding 5,602 cells and 12,283 shared features. RNA data were normalised using log1p transformation, followed by principal component analysis (PCA) on the top 30 components and construction of a nearest-neighbours graph (k = 50). For ATAC, gene-level peak aggregation and gene-peak linkage were performed using the aggregate_peaks_10x utility from MultiVelo, referencing peak annotation/linkage files. The peak matrix (from the ATAC data) was normalised with TF-IDF, which adjusts for differences in sequencing depth and emphasises peaks that are informative rather than universally open. To reduce noise and better capture biological patterns, aggregated peaks were further smoothed across cells using a k-nearest neighbours (k = 30) matrix derived from Seurat WNN integration, ensuring that accessibility signals align with matched transcriptomic states. MultiVelo’s recover_dynamics_chrom() function was run with the following parameters: max_iter = 5, parallel = True, fit = True, and n_anchors = 500, using inverted initialisation for improved model convergence to help the model reach a stable solution more reliably. Cells with only RNA information were excluded from downstream velocity modelling. Output files were stored in .h5ad format for visualisation. For each gene, Model 1 and Model 2 regulatory classifications were assigned based on the inferred chromatin-transcription dynamics. Gene state transitions (“primed,” “coupled-on,” “decoupled,” “coupled-off”) were visualised to assess coordination between chromatin accessibility and transcription over pseudotime. Latent time was calculated using MultiVelo’s latent_time() function and mapped onto UMAP embeddings to visualise developmental trajectories and genotype-specific differences in regulatory dynamics. Parse biosciences single-cell RNA sequencing of fixed cells Sample preparation, including organoid dissociation into single-cell suspensions, was performed prior to fixation according to the Parse Biosciences Evercode WT Mini kit (v2) protocol. Briefly, Day 90 organoids were dissociated into single-cell suspensions, filtered through a 40 μm strainer, and counted using a hemocytometer. Cell suspensions were adjusted to a concentration of ~1,000 cells/μL with viability &gt; 80% prior to fixation. Fixation and permeabilization were performed using the kit’s reagent, and ~5,000 cells per replicate ( n = 3 per genotype) were targeted for sequencing. Fixed cells underwent four successive split-pool barcoding steps: (1) in situ reverse transcription with barcoded primers, (2) two rounds of ligation-based barcoding, and (3) a final sublibrary indexing PCR. Barcoded cDNA was generated, amplified, and purified with KAPA Pure beads (Roche Diagnostics, Catalogue #07983271001). Following quality control, cDNA was fragmented, end-repaired, ligated to sequencing adaptors, and PCR-indexed. Final libraries were quantified using Qubit fluorometry and sized on a BioAnalyzer before sequencing. Sequencing was performed at the Boston University Microarray and Sequencing Core Facility on an Illumina NextSeq2000 platform using a P2 flow cell (≥ 400 million reads per run). Libraries were spiked with 5% PhiX, and the run was configured as follows: Read 1//74 cycles; i7 (index 1)//6 cycles; Read 2//86 cycles; i5 (index 2)//0 cycles. On average, ~100,000–150,000 reads per cell were obtained. FASTQ files were processed using Trailmaker ™ pipeline module ( https://app.trailmaker.parsebiosciences.com/ ; pipeline v1.5.0, Parse Biosciences). The scRNAseq dataset was processed, explored, and visualised using the same methods as described in the Multiome snRNAseq Analysis section above. Cultures were passaged if they reached confluency before day 7 as indicated by Assetta et al., 2020. Oligodendrocyte progenitor cells were generated according to Assetta et al. [ 44 ]. Day-7 NPCs were passaged using Dispase (StemCell Technologies, #07923) and replated at a density of 1–2 × 10 5 cells per well in a 24-well plate (Corning, #3524) with warmed OPC differentiation medium (DMEM/F12 (Gibco, #11965–092), 1% N2 supplement (Gibco, #17502048), 1% B27 supplement (Gibco, #17504044), bFGF (StemCell Technologies, #PHG0266, 20 ng/mL), SAG (Tocris Bioscience, #4366, 1 μM), and PDGF-AA (R&amp;D Systems, #221-AA-010, 10 ng/mL). Cells were maintained in this medium, with feeds every other day, and passaged as needed until Day 15. FUCCI cell line transduction and Nocodazole synchronisation To generate FUCCI-expressing hiPSC lines, cells were plated as aggregates in Matrigel-coated 24-well plates (Corning, #3524) and transduced with lentiviral FUCCI constructs encoding mCherry-CDT1 (G1 phase) and eGFP-Geminin (S/G2/M phase) (LipoExoGen, #LTV-0052). Cells were pre-treated with 6 μg/mL polybrene (Millipore Sigma, #TR-1003-G) before adding 10 μL of virus per well. After 18–20 hours, medium was replaced with additional polybrene (6 μg/mL) and a second viral dose (30 μL/well) was applied for an additional overnight incubation. Cells were maintained in mTeSR Plus media (STEMCELL Technologies, #100–0276) supplemented with Primocin (InvivoGen, #ant-pm-05) and passaged as needed using ReLeSR. Puromycin selection (1 μg/mL) was initiated 48 hours post-infection and maintained for 3 weeks to enrich for stably transduced cells. Successfully transduced colonies were monitored by fluorescence microscopy and expanded for downstream analyses. To synchronise cells at the G2/M phase, cells were dissociated and seeded at ~100,000 cells per well into 12-well plates containing mTeSR Plus media (STEMCELL Technologies, #100–0276) supplemented with Primocin (InvivoGen, #ant-pm-05). FUCCI-expressing WTC11 and KMT2D −/− lines were plated and allowed to adhere overnight before live-cell imaging. The hiPSC lines were then incubated for 40 hours in mTeSR Plus media (STEMCELL Technologies, #100–0276) supplemented with Primocin (InvivoGen, #ant-pm-05) supplemented with either Nocodazole (Noco, Millipore Sigma, #SML1665), or dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, Sigma-Aldrich, #67685) as controls. Nocodazole was freshly diluted into mTeSR Plus to yield final concentrations of 10 nM. DMSO control conditions were matched volumetrically, with 1% of DMSO added per well. Daily media changes continued through Day 2 of differentiation during timelapse imaging on a Keyence BZ-X700 fluorescence microscope (Osaka, Japan). Results KMT2D deficiency broadens chromatin regulation and destabilises lineage gene expression in cerebral organoid-derived neural progenitors The KMT2D −/− hiPSC knockout line (i.e., KMT2D.10) was generated in the WTC11 (WT) hiPSC background using CRISPR/Cas9 to introduce an early stop codon in exon 2 of the KMT2D gene ( Figure S1 ). Both this line and a second edited clone (KMT2D.5) were evaluated for neural rosette formation and organoid growth up to one month ( Figure S1B – D , I – K ; Figure S7 ). Because KMT2D.5 carried a compound heterozygous variant (insertion and deletion), we performed all downstream analyses with the KMT2D.10 line to minimise potential variability. To investigate how KMT2D deficiency alters chromatin accessibility and gene expression during neurodevelopment, we performed single-cell multiome sequencing (snATAC-seq and snRNA-seq) on cerebral organoids derived from both hiPSC lines ( Figure 1A ). Cerebral organoids were generated as described in Ullah et al [ 38 ]. In brief, hiPSCs were subjected to dual SMAD inhibition in N2/B27 medium, followed by neural rosette expansion in EGF/FGF media and later embedded-Matrigel droplets to mature. To ensure sufficient cell type representation of neural progenitors and early differentiating neurons, cerebral organoids were sequenced at two developmental timepoints (Day 51 and Day 74, n = 2 each), and the resulting datasets were combined to generate an integrated profile of early neural development (i.e., 2-month organoids). We generated a high-quality multiomic dataset with a total of 9,604 cells per genotype retained for downstream analysis following QC. UMAP-based dimensionality reduction and visualisation of snATAC-seq, snRNA-seq, and weighted nearest neighbour (WNN) integrations (43) showed minimal separation between genotypes, indicating that general cell type compositions are largely conserved at this developmental stage ( Figure 1B ; Figure S2A ). Cell populations were annotated by comparing curated lineage-specific gene signatures [ 46 ] along with an unbiased differential gene expression approach ( Figure S2B , FDR &lt; 0.05). The resulting clusters were identified as progenitor cells (PCs), neurons (excitatory), inhibitory neurons (IN), and mural cells ( Figure 1C – D , Figure S2B ). To evaluate how closely our in vitro organoid populations resembled stages of human cortical development, we compared the transcriptional profiles of WT and KMT2D −/− organoids to a publicly available foetal cortical RNA-seq dataset [ 47 ] ( Figure 1E , Excel File S1 ). Transcriptome-wide similarity was quantified using the Jaccard similarity index, a statistical method that measures the proportion of shared expressed genes relative to the total genes across samples [ 48 ]. WT organoids showed stronger Jaccard similarity to later post-conception weeks (PCW), consistent with greater maturation, whereas KMT2D −/− organoids showed weaker correlations across both early and late PCW, suggesting increased transcriptional heterogeneity marked by the coexistence of immature and partially maturing transcriptional programmes. To determine how KMT2D loss alters gene regulation at the level of chromatin accessibility and transcription, we applied Functional Inference of Gene Regulation (FigR) to identify regulatory relationships between accessible chromatin regions (cis-regulatory elements, or CREs) and gene expression within the same cell [ 49 ]. Given that fate decisions occur in progenitor populations [ 50 – 53 ], we focused the FigR analysis on the PC cluster to capture early regulatory disruptions before overt lineage differentiation occurs. We identified cis-regulatory interactions by correlating chromatin accessibility peaks within a 10 kilobase window of each gene’s transcription start site (TSS) with its corresponding gene expression. Visualising the total number of genes by their significant regulatory linkages shows a common trend applicable to both genotypes: most genes had few CRE linkages, while a smaller subset displayed higher numbers of linkages ( Figure 1F ). Notably, KMT2D −/− progenitors revealed a clear increase in genes with high linkage counts, indicating greater regulatory chromatin accessibility in general. Because more actively expressed genes are associated with larger CRE landscapes compared to non-expressed genes [ 54 , 55 ], we focused on genes supported by 7 or more significant gene-peak associations. We focused the analysis on genes with at least seven or more significant peak-gene associations, termed domains of regulatory chromatin (DORCs). From 5,000 genes, this threshold yielded 130 DORCs in WT and 163 in KMT2D −/− progenitors (p ≤ 0.05) ( Figure 1F – G ). While both genotypes retained DORCs associated with neural-associated transcription such as PAX6 [ 56 ], EMX1 [ 57 ], and LHX2 [ 58 ], KMT2D −/− progenitors also showed enrichment for the gliogenic genes OLIG1 [ 59 , 60 ] and PTCH1 [ 61 – 63 ], and neurogenic genes SLIT1 [ 64 ] and NEUROD4 [ 65 , 66 ] ( Figure 1G , Excel File S1 ). These peak-gene associations also ranked higher with more linked CREs than the WT condition, suggesting that KMT2D −/− progenitors engage broader regulatory landscapes at lineage-associated genes potentially influencing fate acquisition. Integrative genome browser views of the snATAC-seq data displaying chromatin accessibility and looping interactions for PAX6 , NEUROD4 , and OLIG1 confirm this difference in KMT2D −/− progenitors ( Figure S2C – E ). KMT2D regulates temporal chromatin and transcriptional changes in neural lineage progression Although our inferred regulatory chromatin analysis using FigR identified static correlations between chromatin accessibility and gene expression, it does not capture how these regulatory relationships change over time. To address this, we applied MultiVelo to investigate the temporal dynamics of chromatin accessibility and transcription in KMT2D −/− progenitors [ 67 ]. Multivelo extends traditional RNA velocity by incorporating chromatin accessibility alongside unspliced and spliced RNA, enabling more accurate modelling of regulatory coordination during lineage transitions [ 67 – 70 ]. Given that KMT2D mutations can disrupt enhancer function and transcriptional output, KMT2D may play a key role in aligning chromatin accessibility with gene expression during lineage acquisition [ 16 , 71 – 73 ]. Thus, we hypothesised that disrupted coordination between chromatin accessibility and transcription in KMT2D −/− progenitors contributes to the premature activation of lineage-specific TFs observed in the FigR analysis. To prepare the dataset for MultiVelo modelling, we identified suitable high-quality cells using RNA and ATAC quality control metrics consistent with the original publication [ 67 ]. The final integrated dataset comprised 5,602 cells and 12,283 genes, which were used for velocity modelling. The velocity vectors and latent time estimates recapitulated neurodevelopmental trajectories consistent with those seen in human foetal tissue and organoid models. ( Figure 2A , S3A ) [ 74 – 82 ]. Stream plots of RNA velocities overlaid on the UMAP embeddings confirm that trajectories move from progenitors to mature cell types, including neurons, inhibitory neurons, and mural cells. However, subclustering at higher-resolution ( Excel File S1 ) revealed genotype-specific differences. First, in WT organoids, progenitor and mature populations are clearly segregated, with a subset of cells positioned between the two, possibly representing intermediate transitional states though their exact identity remains unclear ( Figure 2B , S3B ). In contrast, KMT2D −/− progenitor populations are in closer proximity to mature cell clusters within the UMAP embedding and lacked these intermediate transitional populations, suggesting a more direct or unstable progression. Second, the KMT2D −/− late intermediate progenitor-immature excitatory neuron (Late IP Immature EN) cluster exhibits a split distribution across the UMAP embedding, appearing on either side of the mature populations, whereas this cluster forms a more cohesive grouping in WT organoids. Third, the KMT2D −/− neurogenic progenitor cell (PC) cluster shifts toward regions of the embedding associated with mature transcriptional states and its velocity vectors point back toward NPCs, suggesting possible instability in fate progression of those cells. Altogether, while these populations are classified as progenitors based on their transcriptomic annotations, their velocity vector placement in low-dimensional space suggests they may have begun acquiring molecular characteristics associated with more mature cell types. Consistent with these findings, latent time estimates show the expected developmental progression from early progenitor populations (dark purple) to mature states (yellow) in both genotypes ( Figure 2C ). However, the distribution of early latent time values in KMT2D −/− organoids are compressed within the progenitor area, suggesting accelerated maturation of progenitors relative to WT. Further, KMT2D −/− organoids have increased proportions of progenitor and neural populations relative to WT ( Figure S3C ), which is consistent with previous reports [ 15 , 83 ]. Next, we identified genes driving the velocity vectors for each cluster by ranking those contributing most significantly to dynamic transitions based on spliced-to-unspliced RNA kinetics ( Excel File S1 ). Among these, RBPJ , a TF involved in canonical Notch signalling [ 84 ], was uniquely enriched across all KMT2D −/− clusters, including neural progenitor cells (NPCs), neurogenic progenitor cells (neurogenic PCs), late intermediate progenitors-immature excitatory neurons (Late IP-immature EN), and immature neurons, but absent from the corresponding WT gene lists. We also observed continued expression of other Notch receptor genes such as NOTCH1 [ 85 ] and NOTCH2 [ 86 ] in the KMT2D −/− neuron cluster, suggesting an incomplete repression of the progenitor state. To visualise how genes are expressed through pseudotime, MultiVelo classifies genes based on regulatory dynamics: Model 1 genes undergo chromatin closing after the onset of transcriptional repression, while Model 2 genes undergo chromatin closing concurrently with, or prior to, transcriptional repression [ 67 ]. We generated heatmaps for each cluster to display Model 1 and 2 gene expression dynamics for each cluster across latent time, with each row representing a gene and colour intensity reflecting smoothed spliced RNA levels ( Figure 2D , Excel File S1 ). For both Model 1 and 2 genes, KMT2D −/− cells display more diffuse and elevated expression throughout pseudotime, with yellow signal emerging earlier and persisting longer than in WT, suggesting premature and prolonged activation. Additionally, early pseudotime events show marked expression differences between genotypes across both gene models ( Figure 2D , arrows), emphasising the potential importance of early chromatin-transcription decoupling events in driving downstream lineage dysregulation. We next assessed gene expression kinetics using MultiVelo’s four regulatory models. Induction-only and repression-only describe transcriptional activation or repression that occurs independently of changes in chromatin state, while Model 1 and Model 2 capture coordinated chromatin-transcription dynamics. In Model 1, chromatin closing lags the downregulation of transcription (i.e., chromatin remains open briefly after transcription ceases), whereas in Model 2, chromatin closing coincides with or precedes transcriptional repression [ 67 ]. MultiVelo further classifies these dynamics into four distinct phases: primed (red; chromatin opens while transcription is off), coupled-on (orange; chromatin is open and transcription is active), decoupling (green; chromatin opening or closing occurs independently of transcription), and coupled-off (blue; chromatin is closed and transcription is off). We analysed these regulatory phases using phase portraits of key developmental genes to visualise shifts in chromatin-transcription coordination across pseudotime. The core lineage TFs identified in the FigR analysis PAX6 , NEUROD4 , and OLIG1 displayed premature activation and temporal uncoupling in the KMT2D −/− condition, supporting a model of transient, unstable lineage gene expression that may compromise fate commitment ( Figure 2E – F ). Phase portraits of velocity-driving genes ( Excel File S1 ) including RBPJ , a Notch signalling effector that maintaines progenitors, and CDK6 , a G1-S phase regulator important for balancing neural progenitor proliferation and differentiation [ 87 ], show an extended duration in the coupled-on state in KMT2D −/− organoids ( Figure 2E – F ). This indicates prolonged chromatin accessibility and transcriptional activity at progenitor-associated loci. Interestingly, the early post-mitotic neuronal marker ELAVL3 [ 88 – 90 ] under-goes delayed activation in KMT2D −/− organoids, with phase switches occurring later in pseudotime ( Figure S4A – B ). Several markers of neuronal maturation, migration, and axon guidance such as RBFOX3 [ 91 , 92 ], MAP2 [ 93 ], SLIT2 [ 94 ], and DCX [ 95 ] exhibited either delayed or rapidly switching activation patterns ( Figure S4A – B ). These patterns contrast with the early induction NEUROD4 , indicating that KMT2D −/− cells may struggle to align neurogenic signals with maturation events over time. Finally, we examined phase portraits of chromatin modifiers identified from the velocity-driver gene lists (e.g., KMT2C, KAT6A, NCOR1, EZH2 ). While most showed negligible differences between WT and KMT2D −/− organoids, KMT2C shifted from a stable coupled-on profile in WT to a decoupled and ultimately coupled-off states in KMT2D −/− organoids ( Figure S4A – B ). This shift may reflect a transient compensatory response that fails to support prolonged regulatory activity during later stages of development. KMT2D −/− NPCs induce early lineage transcription factor expression and altered cell cycle phase occupancy To experimentally validate our bioinformatic findings and test whether the effects of KMT2D loss are intrinsic to progenitors rather than the organoid environment, we performed directed differentiations of neural and oligodendrocyte progenitors in KMT2D −/− hiPSCs using a 15-day protocol adapted from Assetta et al [ 44 ]. We differentiated the same WT and KMT2D −/− hiPSC lines into NPCs by Day 7 and the oligodendrocyte progenitor cells (OPCs) by Day 15, monitoring PAX6 [ 96 ] and OLIG2 [ 97 ] expression as respective lineage markers ( Figure 3A – C ). We included the oligodendrocyte lineage induction to assess whether KMT2D −/− affects both neural and oligodendrocyte specification, as our bioinformatic analyses showed dysregulation of TFs linked to oligodendrocyte precursors rather than astrocytes. Importantly, we confirmed reduced levels of KMT2D histone substrates in day 7 NPCs by western blot, demonstrating a global decrease in H3K4me1 and H3K4me3 consistent with loss of KMT2D activity ( Figure S1H ). To study the earliest stages of fate acquisition, we analysed Day 7 PAX6 + progenitors, or Passage 1 in the manufacturer’s protocol, which is a transient induction phase that precedes stabilisation at later passages [ 98 ]. While immunofluorescence analysis revealed that both genotypes successfully initiated lineage induction, flow cytometry quantification of the NPC markers PAX6 and SOX2 and the OPC markers OLIG2 and SOX10 [ 99 – 101 ] shows significantly reduced expression levels of these TFs in KMT2D −/− progenitors ( Figure 3C – D ). Further, we performed orthogonal flow cytometry-based validation of lineage marker expression in progenitors from Day 75 cerebral organoids generated in a second independent induction. This analysis showed two distinct PAX6 + populations differing in cell size (FSC-H), with smaller PAX6 + cells more abundant in KMT2D −/− progenitors than larger PAX6 + cells. SOX10 + OPCs were also less frequent in KMT2D −/− organoids, and, consistent with both PAX6 + populations, displayed reduced median fluorescence intensity compared with WT ( Figure S5 A – E ). Finally, we generated an additional scRNAseq dataset of a third independent induction on Day 90 cerebral organoids using an alternative sequencing method and found that the same genes identified as regulators in FigR and MultiVelo analyses (i.e., PAX6, NEUROD4, CDK6, RBPJ ) were significantly enriched in this dataset, confirming their dysregulation across independent methods and organoid inductions ( Figure S5E ). To assess the effects of KMT2D haploinsufficiency on progenitor fate commitment and extend our findings to a clinically relevant context, we differentiated KS1 patient-derived hiPSCs carrying a heterozygous loss-of-function mutation in exon 31 of KMT2D (i.e., KMT2D +/− ), along with their CRISPR-corrected isogenic controls (i.e., KMT2D-corrected). These patient-derived progenitors displayed similar, though less pronounced, reductions in PAX6, SOX2, OLIG2, and SOX10 lineage marker expression, suggesting that this particular heterozygous KMT2D mutation causes milder yet consistent disruptions in progenitor differentiation and aligns with the dose-dependent phenotypic effects observed in KS1 haploinsufficiency ( Figure S6 A – C ) [ 15 , 102 – 104 ]. To test whether KMT2D −/− NPCs reach PAX6 expression levels comparable to WT, we differentiated hiPSCs into NPCs and sampled over a 15-day period in NPC induction media ( Figure 3E ). To capture the disruptions observed in early-pseudotime progenitor populations from our KMT2D −/− organoid trajectory analyses ( Figure 2D ), we focused this experiment on the NPC differentiation protocol rather than the OPC differentiation protocol. Moreover, the competence shift toward gliogenesis typically occurs later in development, after neurogenesis is largely complete [ 10 , 105 , 106 ], likely making the early neurogenic window the most relevant for our analyses. Although PAX6 expression was detected in both genotypes throughout the time course, KMT2D −/− progenitors expressed significantly more PAX6 as early as Day 1, consistent with premature neural induction. Furthermore, glial-like progenitors expressing AQP4 [ 107 ] appeared by Day 3 in KMT2D −/− cultures and persisted, whereas in WT, they emerged around Day 11. KS1 patient-derived hiPSCs exhibited a similar, but less pronounced, temporal shift in lineage TF expression ( Figure S6D ). Together, these findings indicate that KMT2D −/− progenitors initiate neural progenitor lineage mark induction earlier than WT. Considering that chromatin remodelling is coordinated with proliferation during differentiation [ 108 – 111 ], and CDK6 showed prolonged coupled-on phases for KMT2D −/− organoids in our sequencing analysis, we next asked whether disrupted cell cycle dynamics contributes to the early TF induction observed in these cells. While previous studies have established G2/M accumulation in KMT2D-deficient cells [ 15 ], it remains unclear how initial cell cycle dynamics change during the onset of fate acquisition in the neural lineage. To test this, we utilised the Fluorescent Ubiquitination-based Cell Cycle Indicator (FUCCI) system, where mCherry-CDT1 marks G1-phase cells and eGFP-Geminin labels cells in S/G2/M phases [ 112 ]. WT and KMT2D −/− hiPSCs were transduced with FUCCI lentivirus (LipoExoGen) and enriched using puromycin selection, synchronised in G2/M using Nocodazole [ 113 ] or treated with DMSO as a control, and then induced toward NPC fate using the previously described NPC induction protocol ( Figure 3F ). Live-cell imaging and flow cytometry revealed that by 18 hours post-release, KMT2D −/− cells exhibited significantly higher G1-phase occupancy, suggesting accelerated cell cycle re-entry relative to WT, though the precise timing of cell cycle transitions was not directly measured ( Figure 3G , Figure S6F – H ). Consistent with these findings, we pulsed 18-hour non-FUCCI NPCs with EdU and quantified EdU signal intensity 3 hours later, which revealed accelerated EdU dilution in KMT2D −/− progenitors, indicating that these cells underwent more divisions within the same induction period ( Figure S6I ). Finally, KMT2D −/− progenitors undergo an earlier peak in cell division frequency, which plateaued over time, whereas WT cells showed a gradual increase in divisions throughout the first 48 hours of differentiation ( Figure 3H – I ). This early proliferative surge followed by stagnation in KMT2D −/− cells may reflect a burst of rapid proliferation that limits sustained expansion of the progenitor pool or increases susceptibility to cellular stress under these conditions. Together, these findings show that KMT2D −/− progenitors display altered cell cycle phase occupancy during the initial NPC induction period and express neural lineage TF marks sooner than WT, indicating that altered cell cycle timing may contribute to the premature induction of neural lineage genes observed in these cells. Discussion KMT2D is essential for normal cognitive development, with heterozygous variants causing learning, memory, and behavioural deficits in humans and mouse models [ 15 , 114 – 119 ]. Specifically, heterozygous loss of KMT2D has been shown to impair progenitor proliferation and disrupts neuronal subtype specification, suggesting that KMT2D safeguards both the expansion of progenitors and the proper pacing of neural maturation needed for healthy brain development [ 15 , 118 , 119 ]. Despite this progress, its role in neurodevelopment remains incompletely defined. In particular, it is unclear whether KMT2D directly governs progenitor fate commitment or primarily supports expansion, how it coordinates proliferative timing with differentiation, and whether it stabilises lineage trajectories once fate is initiated. Our work begins to address these questions by showing that early neural progenitors lacking KMT2D exhibit premature but unstable activation of lineage transcription factors, early-stage alterations in cell-cycle dynamics during neural induction, and disrupted coupling between chromatin accessibility and transcriptional activity. Together, these results highlight KMT2D as a regulator of timing and stability in early lineage acquisition during neurodevelopment. This work describes how chromatin landscapes integrate with transcriptional programmes in KMT2D-deficient human neural progenitors. Our multiome analysis shows that KMT2D −/− cerebral organoids retain expected neurodevelopmental cell populations but simultaneously display early chromatin and transcriptional shifts that likely impact cell-fate programmes. Notably, phase portraits of KMT2C in KMT2D −/− organoids showed a transient increase in chromatin accessibility and transcriptional activity that later declined, whereas WT organoids maintained steadily increasing dynamics across pseudotime, suggesting a compensatory activation in response to KMT2D loss that is not sustained. Consistent with this, prior work has shown that conditional KMT2D knockout in adipocytes delays lineage progression without altering cell identity, likely due to partial compensation by its paralog KMT2C [ 16 ]. Further, it is also possible that mature cell populations in older cerebral organoids remain vulnerable to the early regulatory disruptions we report here, highlighting the need to study organoids in extended cultures. Our FigR and MultiVelo analyses showed that progenitors in KMT2D −/− cerebral organoids undergo regulatory chromatin remodelling accompanied by premature transcriptional activation of neural lineage programmes. Supporting this, the Jaccard analysis showed that KMT2D −/− organoids retain transcriptional features of both early and later cortical stages. Importantly, the gene regulatory kinetics of core lineage markers including PAX6 , NEUROD4 , and OLIG1 show premature activation followed by decoupling or early inactivation, indicating unstable lineage commitment in the KMT2D −/− condition. Given that precise temporal expression of lineage transcription factors is required for proper timing of neural differentiation [ 120 , 121 ], these data suggest that KMT2D −/− cells prematurely engage lineage programmes but fail to maintain the transcriptional activity needed for maturation. Because our bioinformatic analyses showed dysregulation of OPC-associated transcription factors, we assessed OPC differentiation to test whether KMT2D loss affects both neuron and oligodendrocyte specification. Consistent with this, FigR analysis of KMT2D −/− organoids displayed enriched regulatory chromatin of both neural and oligodendrocyte precursor genes, which did not occur in WT organoids. This phenotype was validated in directed differentiation assays, where KMT2D −/− neural progenitors showed premature yet reduced PAX6 induction accompanied by the early emergence of cells with glial-like morphology and AQP4 expression by Day 3. Thus, early KMT2D −/− progenitors may also experience premature activation of glial lineage genes, deviating from the classical neurodevelopmental sequence in which early neural progenitors first give rise to neurons before transitioning to glia [ 105 ]. Alternatively, because AQP4 also mark radial glial neural stem cells [ 122 ], these KMT2D −/− AQP4 + cells may represent more heterogeneous progenitor states rather than committed glia, which is consistent with the absence of OLIG2 staining ( Figure S6E ). Although off-target glial induction has been reported in neural differentiation protocols [ 123 ], we did not anticipate it this early with our approach. An additional explanation is that variability in differentiation efficiencies across hiPSC backgrounds [ 124 – 129 ], particularly under epigenetic disruption, could contribute to the early phenotypes in KMT2D −/− cultures. Yet our multiomic analyses of unguided organoids also revealed early activation of OPC-associated genes in KMT2D −/− progenitors, indicating that loss of KMT2D can engage gliogenic networks even without directed cues and this phenotype is conserved across systems. Other progenitor-associated genes, including key Notch pathway regulators such as RBPJ , and the cycling gene CDK6 , remain persistently active across KMT2D −/− clusters. Upon interaction with the Notch receptor intracellular domain, RBPJ binds to DNA and activates transcription of target genes that maintain neural progenitor identity and suppress neural differentiation [ 130 , 131 ]. Its presence in KMT2D −/− cells may reflect a persistent progenitor-like transcriptional programme, even as cells initiate differentiation. This finding builds on our previous work demonstrating that Kmt2d-null zebrafish mutants display hyperactivation of Notch signalling, where elevated RBPJ expression in endothelial and endocardial cells led to cardiovascular defects [ 132 ], suggesting that persistent Notch activity could also interfere with neurodevelopmental lineage progression. Further, the persistence of progenitor-associated Notch signalling genes alongside activation of differentiation programmes in KMT2D −/− cells suggests a potential disconnect between fate initiation and repression of the progenitor state, though further functional validation is needed to confirm this relationship. While these findings are consistent with a disruption in the resolution of progenitor identity, it is also possible that Notch signalling does not directly drive developmental trajectories in WT progenitors, and that altered cell-cycle regulation represents the primary phenotype with Notch changes occurring secondarily. Alternatively, MultiVelo may not have identified Notch genes as velocity-driving regulators in WT because of technical limitations, such as low variability in unspliced-to-spliced RNA ratios or minimal changes in chromatin accessibility, both of which reduce the model’s sensitivity to detecting regulatory activity [ 67 ]. Interestingly, DLL3, a cis-inhibitor that promotes neural differentiation by reducing Notch activity within the same cell [ 133 ], was identified as a velocity driving gene in WT progenitors but not KMT2D −/− ( Excel File S1 ), suggesting that WT cells could more effectively suppress Notch signalling to support fate progression. Live-cell imaging of FUCCI-labelled KMT2D −/− cells shows accelerated G1-phase enrichment and earlier mitotic events compared to WT within the first 48-hours of NPC induction, suggesting that the cell cycle could be linked to the observed transcriptional dysregulation. Our EdU labelling experiment also revealed faster EdU dilution in KMT2D −/− progenitors, supporting an overall increase in division frequency during early neural commitment. Our findings may extend a previous report that KMT2D SET-null cells are enriched in G2/M phase (15) by suggesting earlier cell cycle abnormalities that precede this accumulation in KMT2D-deficient cells. The differences in which phase KMT2D-deficient cells accumulate in may reflect differences in the SET-domain knockout model versus our KMT2D knockout model. Complete loss of the protein may interfere with KMT2D’s non-enzymatic functions beyond its SET domain, which could heighten the sensitivity of differentiating hiPSCs to disruptions in linking cell cycle cues with transcription factor activation, a process required for stable lineage commitment [ 5 , 134 ]. These findings complement prior reports showing that KMT2D regulates replication timing during cell fate transitions and that its loss can disrupt Wnt signalling and induce G1-phase stalling via downregulation of CDK1 and Cyclin D1, together supporting a broader role for KMT2D in linking chromatin state to cell cycle control during differentiation [ 135 , 136 ]. Although we did not measure CDK inhibitor levels or G1-phase length in this study, prior work demonstrates that both sufficient CDK inhibitor accumulation and sustained G1-phase lengthening are essential for stabilising gene expression programmes during differentiation [ 137 , 138 ]. Consistent with findings from mutations in other epigenetic regulators, such as EZH2 and CHD7, which also exhibit cell cycle abnormalities and impaired differentiation [ 139 – 142 ], our data suggest that accelerated cell cycle re-entry may be associated with premature activation of neural lineage genes during early stages of fate commitment. Additionally, while KS1 is not classified as a cancer predisposition syndrome, KMT2D’s established role as a tumour suppressor highlights the clinical importance of understanding how it regulates progenitor proliferation in neurodevelopmental contexts [ 143 – 146 ]. Limitations of this study include the inherent variability of cerebral organoid development [ 147 , 148 ] and the absence of longitudinal sampling, which restricts our ability to capture changes in cell classes across extended developmental timeframes. Additionally, cerebral organoids lack key aspects of in vivo brain development, such as vascularisation, brain-specific extracellular matrices, and long-range signalling cues, which influences neural differentiation [ 149 – 153 ]. Although organoids from two developmental timepoints (Day 51 and Day 74) were pooled to yield a combined n = 4, the limited sample size at each individual timepoint ( n = 2) may reduce the resolution of timepoint-specific effects and introduce variability that impacts reproducibility and the robustness of certain bioinformatic analyses. While our full KMT2D knockout model offers mechanistic insights, patient-derived mutations may have subtler effects that differ from complete loss of function. Moreover, while computational frameworks like FigR and MultiVelo are powerful for inferring gene regulatory relationships and temporal coordination between chromatin and transcription, they do not establish direct causality and require further experimental validation. We did not directly measure histone post-translational modifications, which limits our ability to assess how specific chromatin states are altered in KMT2D-deficient cells. Lastly, the absence of astrocyte lineage markers identified in our multiome analyses may reflect limitations in bioinformatic detection QC thresholds, the developmental age of the organoids, or the specificity of our directed differentiation conditions, rather than providing definitive evidence that astrocyte development remains unaffected by KMT2D loss. Several important questions remain including which downstream targets mediate KMT2D’s effects on cell-cycle control, whether specific epigenetic pathways or compensatory mechanisms shape the observed instability, and how these early disruptions translate into the neuronal connectivity and circuit deficits seen in patients. Future efforts should focus on defining how KMT2D regulates histone mark dynamics across cell-cycle states and fate commitment, to distinguish phenotypes that depend on its methyltransferase activity from those driven by non-enzymatic functions. Approaches including recently developed spectral flow cytometry-based chromatin profiling platform [ 154 ] could help phenotypically characterise histone mark alterations during lineage instability. Assessing how KMT2D loss affects cell cycle regulatory networks during neural induction, including direct measurements of CDK activity, phase duration, and the expression dynamics of cell cycle inhibitors could reveal how disruptions in cell cycle timing interface with altered lineage progression. Our findings support a model in which KMT2D regulates the timing of chromatin accessibility to ensure proper engagement and maintenance of lineage programmes during neural induction. In its absence, progenitors show premature and unstable activation of lineage genes, persistent progenitor signatures, and disrupted coordination between chromatin state and transcription. Rather than a global loss of regulatory accessibility, the defect seems to reflect temporal misalignment, which may underlie impaired fate commitment. Indeed, recent work has shown that attenuating the function of either activating or repressive epigenetic regulators during early neural induction can both result in accelerated neural differentiation [ 155 ]. In the case of KMT2D −/− , however, cells present several scenarios: premature induction of lineage genes, persistent expression of progenitor-associated genes, and early loss of induced lineage gene expression. This surprising combination suggests a more nuanced scenario where the coordination between initiating fate commitment, repressing the progenitor programme, and sustaining lineage programmes are impaired, though this remains to be directly validated. The partial recapitulation of KMT2D −/− phenotype in KMT2D +/− hiPSC-derived progenitors from KS1 patients suggests a dose-dependent role for KMT2D in early neurodevelopment, with potential relevance to human disease, though further studies using in vivo systems are needed.
