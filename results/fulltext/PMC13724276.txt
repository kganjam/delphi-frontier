Main Understanding the developmental trajectory of individual neurons and the maturation of their roles within neural circuits is essential to elucidate brain development and plasticity during both embryonic and postembryonic growth, when progressively more refined functions emerge. The integration of neurons into developing circuits has been extensively investigated across vertebrate models 1 – 3 . Newly generated neurons are gradually incorporated into existing circuits by extending axons and dendrites and forming synaptic connections 4 . In several regions of the central nervous system, neurogenesis is spatially organized, with newborn neurons being progressively added in topologically distinct areas. Examples include the spinal cord 5 , hippocampus 6 , cortex and cerebellum 7 . In this context, the zebrafish larva offers a particularly advantageous model for studying neurogenesis and functional integration owing to its transparency and rapid development 3 , 8 . Importantly, a similar topographical organization is observed in the zebrafish nervous system, where some regions exhibit a highly ordered structure characterized by a developmental gradient 9 – 11 . A striking example is the optic tectum (OT), the main visual center in the fish brain: neurons generated early in development (early-born) occupy central regions, while newly generated neurons (late-born) are progressively added to peripheral regions throughout the animal’s life 12 , 13 . Previous studies were limited by low throughput and methodological constraints, underscoring the need for more advanced tools to examine neurogenesis and functional integration at the population level, ideally in vivo. Indeed, a comprehensive functional and causal study of neurogenesis and neuronal integration requires a method capable of identifying the maturation stage of individual neurons and distinguishing early-born from late-born neurons within the intact, developing brain. Furthermore, such an approach should also enable the monitoring of neuronal activity patterns, particularly in relation to specific behaviors or sensory inputs, and permit noninvasive manipulation of neuronal activity. This capability would facilitate precise correlations between neuronal activation, downstream circuit dynamics and behavioral outcomes. Ex vivo approaches to investigate neurogenesis typically involve the use of bromo-deoxyuridine (BrdU), ethynyl-deoxyuridine (EdU) or similar thymidine-analogs, which, when delivered to a tissue, are incorporated into the DNA of cells undergoing replication 14 , 15 . This allows the identification and analysis of the fate of cells born during the labeling period and their subsequent offspring. However, because these approaches require tissue fixation for post hoc staining, they are not applicable to functional studies in living animals. Multiple in vivo approaches have been developed so far, each with substantial caveats. One relies on sparse labeling of progenitors by DNA electroporation or heat shock, allowing researchers to monitor the growth, integration and migration of their progeny within developing circuits 16 , 17 ; this approach is inherently low throughput and labor intensive and often involves invasive procedures. More recently, CRISPR-based strategies have been developed to activate predetermined sequences of genetic reporters in dividing progenitors, enabling birthdating of multiple age cohorts 18 – 20 ; these require substantial optimization owing to their complexity, and even under optimal conditions, reporter switching is inefficient. A third approach, used in optically accessible models such as zebrafish, expresses a photoconvertible fluorescent protein in differentiating neurons, distinguishing neurons born after photoconversion 21 , 22 ; although applicable broadly across a genetically defined population, it cannot resolve neurons born within a defined period. A challenge common to such fluorescence-based methods is that combining them with functional tools such as optogenetics 23 or activity imaging 24 is hampered by overlapping excitation and emission spectra 25 , reducing the accuracy and reliability of both birthdating and functional readouts. To overcome these limitations, we developed CLOK (Chromatic halotag Labeling for cell Ontogeny tracKing), a chemigenetic method for reliable age-dependent neuronal labeling in vivo. CLOK is based on an original framework that exploits the self-labeling HaloTag protein 26 together with Janelia Fluor (JF) dyes, fluorescent ligands known for their excellent photostability and cell permeability 27 , 28 . HaloTag is expressed in differentiating neurons, and distinct JF dyes are applied sequentially over time, so that neurons born within a given interval are labeled by a distinct dye, enabling precise birthdate tagging. After validating this approach against thymidine-based birthdating, we introduce genetic strategies that enable age-dependent labeling of neuronal processes, simultaneous birthdating of up to five neuronal cohorts, long-term tracking over 2 months and functional characterization of visual and motor responses in early- and late-born neurons. We further combined CLOK with chemigenetic optical tools—calcium and voltage indicators and opsins—to directly record and manipulate the activity of birthdate-defined cohorts. Overall, CLOK addresses key limitations in in vivo neuronal birthdating, expands the anatomical and functional readouts of emerging neuronal populations and enables their functional manipulation, providing a powerful tool to probe neural development, circuit integration and plasticity. Results CLOK provides a clear view of brain-wide topographical organization based on neuronal age On exposure to a cell-permeable, fast-binding JF dye ligand, neurons genetically expressing HaloTag become permanently fluorescently labeled, enabling precise visualization of targeted cells 27 , 29 or, as recently shown, identification of newly inserted receptors or synaptic proteins 30 , 31 . We first performed a series of control experiments to determine key features and parameters of the CLOK labeling. In a first set of experiments, we examined if all genetically targeted neurons could be labeled with a JF dye in larval zebrafish using a transgenic (Tg) line co-expressing the HaloTag protein and a membrane-localized GFP. After incubation with a JF dye (here, JF 525 ) at 5 days post fertilization (d.p.f.) ( Methods ), we evaluated the degree of colocalization of the dye and GFP fluorescence, finding 97.43 ± 1.8% of co-expressing neurons (Supplementary Fig. 1a ; mean ± s.d., N = 3 fish, 16 planes), confirming robust, near-complete JF dye labeling. Delaying imaging after dye incubation in the same Tg line (incubation at 3 d.p.f. followed by imaging from 3 to 6 d.p.f.) increased the number of JF dye-negative, GFP-positive cells located at the periphery of the tectum (Supplementary Fig. 1b ). This observation confirms that the HaloTag-JF dye complex remains stable, preventing JF dye from binding to newly expressed HaloTag protein and thereby enabling temporally targeted HaloTag labeling. We then characterized how well JF dye binds HaloTag across developmental stages and remains fluorescent thereafter, using larvae expressing nuclear-localized HaloTag under a pan-neuronal promoter ( Methods ). Dye incubations were performed at different time points ranging between 0 and 5 d.p.f. and the OT was imaged from 1 to 6 d.p.f. At all time points, JF dye was successfully incorporated in neurons across the brain, and the labeled neurons remained fluorescent throughout, confirming effective JF dye loading across the developmental stages (Supplementary Fig. 1c ). In addition, we assessed, following dye incubation, the clearance of unincorporated dye and the resulting reduction of nonspecific fluorescence, after periods ranging from 0 to 24 h. We observed, for several JF dyes, a gradual decrease in the background signal over time (below 20% after 30 min and below 10% after 2 h), resulting in an almost total washout after 4 h (Supplementary Fig. 2 ). Finally, we examined potential developmental effects of loading single or multiple JF dyes early in development and found no clear effect on the survival rate, the swim bladder development and the body length, indicating negligible developmental effects of JF dye loading (Supplementary Fig. 3 ; Methods ). Neurogenesis studies often rely on the use of thymidine analogs such as BrdU or EdU, which are incorporated into the DNA of dividing cells during the S phase 32 . These markers require tissue fixation for postlabeling staining. Here, we set out to compare CLOK against EdU staining by birthdating cells every 24 h and comparing their distribution at 5 d.p.f. To do so, we generated Tg lines that express the HaloTag protein using the elavl3 promoter, a well-established marker of early neuronal differentiation 33 , 34 . To enable direct comparison of the two approaches, we implemented the following strategy (Fig. 1a ). First, we added a nuclear localization sequence to the HaloTag to mimic EdU’s nuclear signal. Second, we targeted a single age cohort per animal using two distinct JF dyes (cohort neurons were defined as those showing a stronger signal from the second dye; Methods ). Third, using the same Tg background Tg(elavl3:H2B-GCaMP6f) , we registered both datasets to the Zebrafish Brain Browser (ZBB) atlas on the basis of live microscopy volumes and generated a three-dimensional (3D) volume for each treatment group by averaging registered volumes across larvae (Extended Data Fig. 1 ; N = 5 or 6 fish per target). To visualize targeted neuronal age groups in CLOK data, we normalized each dye signal per fish, subtracted the first dye signal from the second and averaged the resulting volumes across fish to generate a volume for each age group ( Methods ; Fig. 1a ). The resulting subtracted volume looks similar across a range of normalization parameters (Supplementary Fig. 4 ), supporting the robustness of this visualization procedure. Both birthdating procedures revealed spatially clustered age-defined neuronal populations, uncovering topographical organization based on birthdate in more brain regions than previously reported 10 , 13 (Fig. 1b ). The labeling patterns were largely consistent across the brain, although with a few notable exceptions. First, EdU staining also targeted non-neuronal cell types, including progenitor cells in the ventricular zone (Fig. 1b , plus signs). Second, the spatial distribution of the EdU-labeled cells appeared broader (Fig. 1b , green and red curly braces in the tectum and the hindbrain, respectively; see Extended Data Fig. 2 for a grayscale image of each channel). These differences are expected, as EdU gets incorporated into all dividing cells and their offspring, while CLOK targets selectively differentiating neurons. To quantitatively evaluate each birthdating approach’s resolution, we implemented a protocol using the overall distribution of the color ratio rather than simple subtraction. The color ratio is defined as the angle of a two-dimensional (2D) vector represented by normalized dye signals, and each voxel is classified into two populations on the basis of this ratio using a Gaussian Mixture Model (GMM; Supplementary Fig. 5a ; Methods ). We then evaluated the area occupied by each age group within the brain volume and within the volume excluding the ventricular zone (Supplementary Fig. 5b ). In both cases, birthdating method had a significant main effect on the volume labeled (Fig. 1c,d , binomial regression, P &lt; 2.2 × 10 −16 ). Post hoc tests also show that CLOK labels more specific populations across age groups (Fig. 1c,d , P &lt; 0.001). Cross-correlation analyses within the neuronal nuclei volume also confirmed this observation (Fig. 1e,f ). Although CLOK and EdU showed similar topographic organization (Fig. 1e , see higher correlation values in near diagonal elements), CLOK showed much reduced correlation across age groups compared with EdU, supporting the higher birthdating resolution of CLOK (Fig. 1f ). We further examined whether the broad EdU labeling stemmed from the warping required to register fixed EdU samples to the ZBB atlas, which is based on live microscopy volumes. We repeated the same quantifications after registering both datasets to the MapZebrain atlas 35 , which is based on fixed microscopy volumes (Supplementary Fig. 6 ). All statistical effects described above remained significant, confirming that warping contributes negligibly to the observed difference in birthdating resolution. Motivated by the superior birthdating resolution of CLOK, we further examined its resolution limit by loading the first and the second dyes in shorter intervals (Extended Data Fig. 3 ). Neurons captured by CLOK begin to appear with a 3-h pulse labeling and are clearly detectable with a 6-h pulse labeling, suggesting that, for the elavl3 promoter, CLOK has temporal resolution of 6 h or less. Having established that the CLOK strategy offers clearly superior birthdating resolution compared with the standard EdU method, we further examined its unique applications. Source data CLOK reveals brain-wide chronological organization at subcellular level and enables simultaneous labeling of multiple neuronal age groups One advantage of CLOK is its ability to visualize specific subcellular compartments of a target neuronal age group by fusing a localization sequence to the HaloTag protein. This is in contrast to the thymidine-based birthdating approach where the signal is restricted to the nucleus. Here, we targeted axon terminals using the synaptophysin tag 36 and somatodendritic processes using the Voltron1 construct containing the HaloTag protein fused with the Kv2.1 localization sequence 37 . We targeted the same neuronal age groups as in Fig. 1 and generated the population average for each age group ( N = 5 or 6 fish per group). The area occupied by each age group was visualized on the basis of the subtraction of normalized signals as in Fig. 1 . This procedure enables visualization of the newly innervated area. We first confirmed the distinct labeling patterns of the two subcellular localization tags (axon terminals and somatodendritic processes) by comparing the JF 585 signal in group 5, which captures the majority of neurons except those born between 4 and 5 d.p.f. (Fig. 2a ). Interestingly, this comparison revealed not only the expected differential labeling in the cell body areas but also distinct domains within the neuropil that are dominated by either axonal terminals or dendrites. Furthermore, CLOK-based labeling of these subcellular tags revealed distinct age-dependent organization within these domains (Fig. 2b,c ; see JF 585 and JF 635 signals before subtraction in Extended Data Fig. 4 ). We observed that axon terminals from the later-born populations occupy progressively the outer layer of the neuropil area across multiple brain regions such as the tectum, cerebellum and hindbrain (Fig. 2b , see the ordering of colored arrowheads). By contrast, the majority of dendritic domains were colored by the earliest-born group (&lt;24; Fig. 2c ) except the tectum. Closer inspection of JF 585 and JF 635 signals across age groups suggests that this pattern reflects the mostly overlapping or smaller dendritic processes of the late-born populations (Extended Data Fig. 4b ). Taken together, CLOK labeling of subcellular processes revealed a brain-wide chronological organization of axonal and dendritic structures, highlighting a unique advantage of CLOK to visualize the subcellular architecture of birthdated neurons. Another advantage of CLOK is the broad spectrum of JF dyes biocompatible in zebrafish. This characteristic provides an original opportunity to simultaneously visualize multiple neuronal age cohorts in individual fish. As a proof of principle, we applied five distinct JF dyes, one every 24 h, and imaged the larvae at 5 d.p.f. with a white laser confocal microscope to excite each dye at its optimal wavelength (Fig. 2d ; Methods ). We then visualized each neuronal age group by subtracting normalized signals as in Fig. 1 (Fig. 2e ; Methods ). Despite the differences in lactone–zwitterion equilibrium that influence biocompatibility of the dyes 38 , this approach successfully captured the overall chronological organization of nuclei positions observed in the population-level dataset (Fig. 2f ). In summary, we demonstrated a unique capability of CLOK to simultaneously visualize five age-defined neuronal populations in a live animal. Together, these results show that CLOK resolves the topographic organization of neurons by age—across subcellular compartments and across multiple cohorts—within the same developing animal. CLOK enables persistent, stable and bright labeling across juvenile stages To assess the stability and persistence of the HaloTag labeling, we extended our analysis to juvenile stages and tracked neurons with single or multiple dyes in Tg(Xla.Tubb2-hsp70-ubc:H2B-HaloTag) . HaloTag-based labeling remained clearly detectable for up to 8 weeks after fertilization, enabling long-term visualization of neurons well beyond their initial integration into circuits (Extended Data Fig. 5 ). Larvae incubated with JF 525 (green) or JF 552 (red) at 5 d.p.f. retained clear labeling when imaged at 14 or 21 d.p.f. (Fig. 3a,c ), and the dyes were also successfully incorporated at juvenile stages (incubation at 14 d.p.f.; Fig. 3b,d ). Importantly, triple incubations at larval stages (1, 3 and 5 d.p.f.) still produced distinct birthdate labeling when imaged at 14 or 21 d.p.f. (Fig. 3e ), confirming the temporal resolution and persistence of the method. This stable labeling also enabled identification of neurons in fixed and cryosectioned brains and retinas (Extended Data Fig. 5a,b ), demonstrating compatibility of CLOK with histological analysis. In addition, HaloTag stability over weeks was confirmed by comparing transient expression of HaloTag alone or fused with the stable histone protein H2B 39 ; both conditions retained detectable JF signal 2 weeks after dye loading (Supplementary Fig. 7 ). Finally, sequential labeling at 5, 10 and 14 d.p.f. (larvae imaged at 14 d.p.f.; Extended Data Fig. 5c,d ) revealed topographic neuronal organization in juvenile fish similar to that observed during larval stages (Fig. 1b ). Source data We first examined visual responses of periventricular neurons (PVNs), the main neuronal population of the larval OT, whose somata reside in its deepest layer. Using dual-color CLOK labeling, we assessed birthdate-dependent responses through two complementary strategies. In a first macro-level approach (Fig. 4a,b ), regions labeled exclusively with JF 669 but lacking the first dye JF 552 were classified as late-born (neurons generated between the first and second dye loading), whereas centrally located regions labeled with both dyes (neurons born before the first dye loading) were classified as early-born ( Methods ). This spatial distribution—peripheral for late-born neurons and central for early-born—matches the known progression of tectal neurogenesis 12 , 13 . Integrating GCaMP signals across these macro-regions revealed distinct differences in visually evoked activity (Fig. 4b and Extended Data Fig. 6b ). Early-born neurons (more mature) generally displayed stronger responses than late-born neurons. Specifically, bright flash elicited similar responses in both populations, suggesting comparable baseline visual sensitivity. By contrast, moving gratings and looming stimuli evoked significantly greater responses in the early-born population (* P &lt; 0.05, Welch’s t -test; Extended Data Fig. 6b ). This macro-region approach is simple, fast and convenient when birthdate-defined populations form spatially distinct clusters or when cellular-resolution imaging is unavailable. We next performed single-cell analysis using a broader stimulus set, including dark and bright flashes, moving gratings and looming stimuli ( Methods ; Fig. 4c,d ). Neurons were ordered according to the dual-dye intensity ratio JF 552 /(JF 552 + JF 669 ), which serves as a proxy for neuronal birthdate. As the promoter is constitutively active in postmitotic cells, this ratio reflects the relative time elapsed since neuronal differentiation. Neurons born between the two dye-loading sessions incorporate both dyes and display high ratio values, whereas neurons born after the second dye loading incorporate mainly the second dye and exhibit low ratio values. Mapping these ratios across the OT closely matches the topographic organization shown in Fig. 1b : with higher ratios concentrated centrally and lower ratios in peripheral regions (Fig. 4c , left, and Supplementary Fig. 8 ). Calcium traces were then ordered according to dye ratio (Fig. 4c , right; N = 5 fish), and the correlation between neuronal activity and birthdate proxy was quantified using Spearman correlation analysis (Fig. 4d ; Methods ). Consistent with the macro-level analysis, early-born neurons exhibited higher visually evoked activity (Fig. 4d , red sections). Overall, early-born neurons showed a higher likelihood of activation, as indicated by a greater fraction of responsive cells across all the stimuli (* P &lt; 0.05, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA); Extended Data Fig. 6c ). Restricting analyses to cells within fixed tectal subregions confirmed that these differences were independent of retinotopic location (Supplementary Fig. 9 ). We then examined the relationship between neuronal maturation and direction selectivity (DS), by analyzing PVNs’ responses to gratings moving in eight orientations ( Methods ). We found a correlation between the age of individual neurons, their topographic organization and their directional tuning properties ( P &lt; 0.001, R 2 = 0.05). Specifically, early-born neurons exhibited stronger and more consistent DS to gratings (Extended Data Fig. 6d,e ), in accordance with previous studies showing that DS emerges progressively during development 12 , 40 , 41 . We confirmed these observations by tracking the visual responses of neuronal subpopulations born at defined developmental stages (Fig. 4e ), using single-dye labeling to isolate neurons born before a time point, or single-pulse (two-dyes) labeling to isolate those born between two time points. Calcium imaging in the following days revealed the progressive maturation of visually evoked activity within these birthdate-defined populations (Fig. 4e ). While PVNs exhibit a clear relationship between birthdate and topographical organization, this is not generally the case throughout the nervous system. CLOK can establish this birthdate–function relationship even in spatially intermixed populations. To demonstrate this, we analyzed tectal neuropil interneurons, including superficial interneurons (SINs) and deep interneurons (DINs), whose positions do not reliably predict developmental age 42 , 43 . Using the same dual-dye ratio approach applied to PVNs, we were able to sort neuropil interneurons according to their birthdate and investigate the birthdate dependence of their visual responses (Fig. 4f,g ). Although no functional differences were observed—even when SIN and DIN subpopulations were analyzed separately (Fig. 4g and Supplementary Fig. 10 )—this experiment illustrates the ability of CLOK to separate and analyze neurons of different birthdates even when they are spatially intermingled. Together, these experiments establish CLOK as a powerful tool for investigating the functional integration of neurons in the developing OT, enabling birthdate-based analysis of neuronal activity and the tracking of the maturation of visual responses over time. Source data Consistent with previous reports 10 , 44 , we observed significant changes in the repertoire of locomotor behaviors measured by electromyogram (EMG). Specifically, zebrafish at 2 d.p.f. escaped in response to electrical stimuli but rarely swam spontaneously (Fig. 5c , 2 d.p.f., and Fig. 5f , 2 d.p.f., EMG traces). Muscle activity also showed a unimodal distribution (Fig. 5c , 2 d.p.f.), suggesting that zebrafish at this stage produce a limited range of muscle activity. By contrast, 4 d.p.f. larvae showed robust spontaneous swims in addition to escape responses evoked by electrical stimuli (Fig. 5c , 4 d.p.f., and Fig. 5f , 4 d.p.f., EMG traces). Muscle activity during spontaneous swims was also distinctively weaker (Fig. 5c , 4 d.p.f.), resulting in a broader range of muscle activity (Fig. 5c , 4 d.p.f.). To assess how the activity of each neuronal age group changes during this behavioral development, we performed regression analysis to identify correlated activity with distinct locomotor events. In 2-d.p.f. larvae, we looked for neurons whose activity correlated with escapes or long swims, on the basis of the variability of the length of escape episodes at this stage (Fig. 5f , 2 d.p.f., EMG traces), and found that both functional types are present in each neuronal age group (Fig. 5d , 2 d.p.f.). However, a much smaller proportion of the late-born group showed activity related to these locomotor events (Fig. 5e , 2 d.p.f., P &lt; 0.0001, corrected for multiple comparisons, using a generalized linear mixed model for binomial distribution with subjects and locomotor types as random effects), suggesting that the late-born group is still functionally immature. In 4-d.p.f. larvae, we looked for neurons whose activity correlated not only with escape but also with locomotor events not present at 2 d.p.f., including spontaneous swim and strong lateralized responses during spontaneous swim. As in 2-d.p.f. larvae, each functional class was present in both neuronal age groups. However, the proportion of active neurons in each age group showed a significant interaction between subject age and neuronal age ( P &lt; 2 × 10 −16 ) (Fig. 5e ), suggesting ongoing functional maturation of the late-born group. To examine whether this gradual maturation of the late-born group also applies to functional classes besides those involved in locomotion, we examined the spontaneous activity of both neuronal age groups at 2 and 4 d.p.f. and determined the proportion of active neurons within each group and the diversity of their activity (Fig. 5f–h and Extended Data Figs. 7 and 8 ; Methods ). At 2 d.p.f., the majority of the early-born group was active (Fig. 5f,g , 2 d.p.f., &lt;24-h.p.f. neurons) while only a minority of the late-born group was active (Fig. 5f,g , 2 d.p.f., 24–48-h.p.f. neurons). At 4 d.p.f., the proportion of active neurons within the late-born population increased (Fig. 5f,g ; P &lt; 0.001). Furthermore, the functional diversity of the late-born population increased from 2 d.p.f. (Fig. 5f,h ; P &lt; 0.001) and became greater than that of the early-born population (Fig. 5h ; P &lt; 0.001). Activity maps of sorted neuronal activity indicate that the neurons not directly related to locomotion, including those in the tectum, contribute to this diversity (Extended Data Fig. 8 ). These observations support the idea that the gradual maturation of the late-born group applies generally across the examined brain regions. Interestingly, the analysis of locomotor-related activity revealed that early-born neurons were recruited into behaviors absent at 2 d.p.f. (weak swim: 48.2% (median), left strong bend: 10.3% (median), right strong bend: 14.7% (median); Fig. 5d,e , 4 d.p.f.). This raises the possibility that coordination between neuronal age groups emerges during this period and may play a critical role in the development of motor behavior. Taken together, we showed that CLOK enables longitudinal activity tracking of multiple neuronal age cohorts in a developing vertebrate and opens the avenue to investigating how their coordination contributes to behavioral maturation. Integrating CLOK with sensors and actuators for birthdate-selective functional imaging and photoactivation We next designed proof-of-principle experiments to demonstrate that CLOK can be coupled with multiple chemigenetic optical tools for versatile age-related optical investigations (Fig. 6 ). We first validated this strategy using WHaloCaMP, a chemigenetic calcium indicator in which the calcium-sensitive domain is fused to HaloTag 45 . Two-dye CLOK labeling in WHaloCaMP-expressing larvae (JF 552 at 3 d.p.f. and JF 669 at 5 d.p.f.), followed by sequential functional imaging of each dye, enabled selective monitoring of spontaneous activity in neurons of distinct birthdates (Fig. 6a–c and Supplementary Fig. 11 ). Clustering early- and late-born neurons according to fluorescent labeling ( Methods ), we found a higher frequency of spontaneous events in the early-born population, consistent with previous studies 13 . A similar strategy was applied to the rhodopsin-based voltage indicator Voltron2, which combines a microbial rhodopsin voltage sensor with a HaloTag-JF dye fluorophore 46 . Given the absorption spectrum of its rhodopsin domain, we loaded JF 525 and JF 585 at 3 and 5 d.p.f. to record voltage signals from two distinct neuronal age groups. Fast fluorescent transients were recorded in the forebrain and the olfactory epithelium, a region with reliable spontaneous spiking 46 . These results demonstrate the detection of action potentials and extraction of firing rates in birth-resolved neuronal populations (Fig. 6d–f ). Source data To further demonstrate CLOK’s versatility, we extended it to all-optical experiments, where opsins and activity indicators are used together to enable simultaneous optical manipulation and recording of neuronal activity 47 . To this end, we fused HaloTag to our opsin of choice, CoChR 48 , 49 . All-optical approaches typically benefit from spectral separation between the opsin, the activity indicator and the opsin’s fluorescent tag. First, to demonstrate an increased flexibility provided by JF dye labeling for tagging opsin-expressing cells, we characterized imaging-induced opsin activation and evaluated the cross-talk between dye imaging and CoChR: in Chinese hamster ovary (CHO) cells expressing CoChR-HaloTag, we recorded photocurrents during 2P photostimulation and simultaneous imaging at different wavelengths (Supplementary Fig. 12 ). The orthogonality between CoChR excitation spectrum 50 and the distinctive 2P absorption profile of JF dyes (here, JF 585 ; peaks at 800 nm and &gt;1,000 nm) allowed bright imaging and identification of CoChR-expressing cells with minimal artifactual photocurrents from the imaging laser. By contrast, GFP-tagged CoChR, which requires imaging at ~950 nm, induced substantially larger artifactual photocurrents (&gt;200 pA), potentially causing unwanted depolarization during cell screening. We next tested this construct in vivo by transiently expressing CoChR in Tg(elavl3:H2B-GCaMP6s ) and performing simultaneous 2P imaging and 2P holographic photoactivation of user-defined cell groups. Opsin-expressing neurons were identified by two-dye CLOK labeling (Fig. 6g ; JF 552 at 3 d.p.f. and JF 669 at 5 d.p.f.), and because of the spectral selectivity in both excitation and emission, HaloTag labeling could be readily separated. Using 2P holographic stimulation, we selectively targeted either early- or late-born neurons (Fig. 6h , left) while recording GCaMP responses across the full field of view (FOV) at reduced laser power to limit imaging-induced activation ( Methods ). Photostimulation-evoked calcium responses were clearly detected in stimulated neurons (Fig. 6h , right), confirming successful activation. Mean fluorescence changes (Δ F / F ) were similar between populations (early-born: 0.20 ± 0.02; late-born: 0.22 ± 0.02, P = 0.94, Student’s t -test; Fig. 6i ), demonstrating the capability to stimulate either early- or late-born neurons at will and with equal efficiency. Altogether, this proof-of-principle set of experiments demonstrates the potential of CLOK for all-optical investigation of birthdate-resolved subpopulations, opening avenues for dissecting their incorporation into developing neural circuits. Discussion We developed CLOK, a neuronal birthdating strategy designed to overcome longstanding challenges in tracking age-defined populations and studying the maturation of their functional roles within neural circuits. The approach combines HaloTag technology with JF dyes to label, in a living animal, the entire population of neurons differentiating within a defined time window. Unlike traditional birthdating approaches, CLOK is compatible with a broad range of genetic and optical tools, including subcellular tagging, calcium and voltage imaging, and optogenetic manipulations, providing a powerful and versatile platform to investigate how newborn neurons wire into functional circuits and shape behavior. Current birthdating methods each carry some drawbacks. BrdU and EdU, the most widely used markers, incorporate into newly synthesized DNA and thus label all dividing cells indiscriminately; worse, visualization requires tissue fixation, ruling out in vivo use altogether. Electroporation 51 and heat-shock-based genetic approaches 52 avoid tissue fixation but still mark all sibling cells across multiple rounds of division, thus requiring sparse labeling for interpretability. More sophisticated strategies that synchronize reporter switching with the cell cycle exist 18 , 19 but typically yield limited numbers of labeled late-born cells. By contrast, CLOK labels entire neuronal populations at single-cell resolution in vivo, without the cell-to-cell labeling stochasticity that plagues other strategies. The simplicity of HaloTag engineering further expands the scope of possible applications. At the anatomical level, subcellular labeling revealed where axonal and dendritic processes of each age-defined population reside across the vertebrate brain—a picture that becomes even more informative when combined with cell-type-specific targeting. On the functional side, pairing CLOK with HaloTag-based calcium and voltage indicators allowed us to read out neural activity sorted by birthdate. We also tagged opsins directly with CLOK, enabling 2P photostimulation of neurons selected by birthdate rather than by cell type or anatomy alone. Combined with recently developed HaloTag-based photoablation, similar protocols may also enable ablation of age-specific cellular populations 53 . Using the spectral range of biocompatible JF dyes from 525 to 669 nm, we simultaneously labeled up to five cohorts in a single animal. These JF dyes are clearly separable from GFP-based indicators under both 1-photon and 2P excitation, which is especially convenient for pairing with the most commonly used GFP-based calcium and voltage indicators 54 , 55 . A related advantage is that HaloTag itself produces no fluorescence, so CLOK Tg lines are spectrally neutral—the experimenter is free to choose whichever dye best suits the fluorescent proteins or opsins already in the experiment, rather than being locked into the spectral identity of a genetically encoded reporter. Fully exploiting multiplexed labeling will require further refinement of dye combinations for spectral separation and tissue compatibility, but the approach already opens up questions about individual variation in neurogenesis that were previously difficult to address. Newborn neurons in the OT, irrespective of their birthdate, exhibited more robust visual responses as they matured and became functionally similar to the early-born, pre-existing neurons. In the hindbrain, newborn neurons matured over a comparable time frame but settled into activity patterns that seem distinct from those of early-born neurons. These pre-existing neurons also change their activity as the newborns mature, suggesting potential interactions between neurons across age groups. Therefore, while the pace of maturation appears similar across regions, the endpoint differs—a distinction that CLOK is well suited to investigate further at the circuit and molecular levels. How well CLOK resolves distinct temporal cohorts over an extended period depends on a few factors. Promoters that switch on during differentiation and then go quiet—such as elavl3 —give sharper temporal boundaries. Those that stay active into maturity, such as Xla.Tubb2 56 , trade some precision for longer tracking windows. Progenitor-targeted expression is also possible but behaves more like thymidine-based methods, labeling progenitors and progeny alike; it is better suited to lineage tracing than to birthdating per se. Dye selection adds another consideration: differences in lactone–zwitterion equilibrium affect bioavailability and can shift the apparent age-group boundaries between experiments, so the same dye set should be used consistently across conditions. Protein tags are also known to affect protein turnover, influencing how long birthdated neurons can be observed. These factors can also influence dye signal ratios, which we use here to determine the age of labeled neurons by splitting them into distinct age groups or revealing the age gradient within the population labeled during a longer pulse. Treating the dye ratio as a continuous variable needs careful examination and validation, that is, by comparing the result to the discrete labeling of newborn neurons with a shorter pulse or to the standard thymidine-based birthdating methods. However, once validated, the dye-ratio approach can reveal age information without requiring a separate dye for each age group. Although precautions and systematic characterization are needed to account for how these factors influence dye signal ratios and age-related clustering, our successful demonstrations nonetheless speak to the robustness of CLOK. We focused on zebrafish owing to the high bioavailability (that is, skin penetration) of the dye, but the core mechanism of CLOK can also be applied to other in vivo animal models such as Caenorhabditis elegans 57 , Drosophila 58 , mouse 46 or in organoid systems, where it could help map neurogenic trajectories through complex 3D tissues 59 . The versatility of CLOK in terms of promoter-driven expression makes it readily adaptable to other biological contexts, such as the study of glial development and function, or hematopoietic lineage formation. In conclusion, CLOK offers a practical, noninvasive way to combine precise birthdate labeling with the growing toolkit of HaloTag-based reagents. By bringing together single-cell resolution birthdating, functional imaging and all-optical circuit manipulation within a single framework, it provides a platform for studying neuronal integration—anatomically, physiologically and at the circuit level—that improves on existing methods in both reliability and flexibility. Data analysis Static fluorescent images For protocols involving incubation with multiple dyes in the same larvae, efforts were made to minimize cross-talk in both excitation and detection of different dyes. Using a confocal microscope, we fine-tuned excitation and detection settings for each dye, achieving negligible cross-talk among the typical JF 525 , JF 552 and JF 669 dye combination. The cell count in Supplementary Fig. 1c was performed manually. To maintain consistency, we analyzed the same imaging plane across larvae, using anatomical landmarks as references (one plane per larva, N = 14 larvae). In 2P imaging, the overlap in excitation spectra (with dyes excited at 800–850 nm) and the use of only two PMTs resulted in some fluorescence cross-talk. To address this, the fluorescence intensities of individual dyes were measured in each PMT (with different emission filters) using larvae expressing single dyes. This allowed us to estimate the detection efficiency of each fluorophore and assess potential cross-talk across detection channels. These intensity values were then used as coefficients in a linear wavelength-demixing algorithm, enabling the isolation of the signal contributions and intensities of each individual dye. To quantify the area labeled by each age group in the HaloTag dataset, we classified each voxel in the individual volume into the area dominated by the first dye, the area dominated by the second dye and the area not labeled by either of the dyes with the following procedure. First, we applied a median filter (scipy.ndimage.median_filter with the size parameter set to 3) to register and normalized individual volume to make the color ratio metric more robust against the noise. Then, we defined each voxel as a 2D vector represented by the first dye signal and the second dye signal. The length of the vector ( r ) was used to classify voxels between signal ( r &gt; 0.08) and background ( r ≤ 0.08) (Supplementary Fig. 5a , dotted red line). Then, the signal voxels were classified between those dominated by the first dye and those dominated by the second dye by applying a GMM (sklearn.mixture.GaussianMixture) to the vector angle ( θ ). Neuronal age group 1 was defined as those dominated by the first dye in treatment group 2 (Fig. 1a ) to use the same color ratio-based classification across neuronal age groups. Spurious pixels in the generated binary masks were removed by binary opening (skimage.morphology.binary_opening with a ball with radius set to 2 voxels as a structuring element). To quantify the area occupied by each age group in the EdU dataset, we used intensity-based voxel classification. To account for the staining variability present in the EdU dataset, we slightly modified the normalization procedure described above and set the value at the top one percentile to 1. Then, we heuristically set the threshold to classify voxel as the signal to 0.6 to exclude the background staining (Supplementary Fig. 5b , see EdU). The same median filter and binary opening procedures used in the HaloTag dataset were used for the EdU dataset. Calcium imaging analysis during visual stimulation Calcium transients from GCaMP fluorescence signals of Fig. 4 were analyzed either by integrating over macro-regions or by extracting signals from individual cell bodies. (1) Macro-regions were defined as areas containing mainly either early-born or late-born neurons, based on the global distribution of the two dyes, JF 552 and JF 669 . Specifically, the edges of the contralateral side of the OT (opposite to the visually stimulated eye) were delineated using the JF 669 staining (second dye, incubated at 5 d.p.f.). In this region, both dye images were smoothed with a Gaussian filter ( σ = 8 µm) and, within each half of the rostral and caudal portions of the OT, late-born domains were identified as areas lacking JF 552 signal (defined as pixels with intensities below the 20th–30th percentile of the JF 552 distribution), which corresponded to the most peripheral tectal zones. Equally sized regions containing both dye signals were then defined within the central tectal area and considered to be populated by early-born neurons (born before the first dye incubation). GCaMP transients within these macro-regions were integrated and converted to ∆ F / F 0 signals, where F 0 was defined as the 20th quantile of the full intensity distribution. (2) For single-cell analysis, GCaMP fluorescence was extracted from individual neurons of the contralateral OT identified using CellPose (cyto2 model applied to JF dyes fluorescence) with manual refinement of ROIs in ImageJ. After background subtraction and motion correction, GCaMP transients were converted to ∆ F / F 0 signals, with F 0 calculated as the time-averaged intensity of each trace. For each neuron, the unmixed ratio of the two incubated dyes was also computed from the same ROI. Peaks of ∆ F / F 0 signals were defined as the maximum values within a 3-s time window starting at the onset of each visual stimulus. To compare early- and late-born neuron activity without dividing the dataset into arbitrary clusters, we computed the correlation between the Δ F / F signal and the dual-dye fluorescence ratio for all neurons using SciPy’s spearmanr function. The resulting Spearman coefficients, which reflect the strength of the correlation between the two variables, were plotted as a function of time. The threshold for significance was determined using Student’s t -distribution. More explicitly, the resulting Spearman coefficient plots (Fig. 4d,g and Supplementary Figs. 16 and 17 ) show the correlation between the Δ F / F values and the dye ratio of each neuron at each time point (1 = perfectly correlated, 0 = not correlated, −1 = negatively correlated). Time points with significant correlations are shown in red (the gray line indicates the threshold for significance). Note that under the null hypothesis, one still expects 5% of time points to reach significance (±1%, binomial confidence interval, α = 0.05). In the main Fig. 4 , for instance, 42% of time points are significant, indicating correlations between activity and dye fluorescence. These significant time points are not distributed uniformly; rather, the correlation is stronger—and therefore more frequently significant—during visual stimulation, suggesting that early-born neurons are more likely to be activated by the stimuli than late-born neurons. Separation of the neuropil interneurons into the two subpopulations, SINs and DINs (Supplementary Fig. 10 ), was based on their anatomical location. For each cell soma, we extracted its distance from the skin (perpendicular distance from the line connecting the intersection of the skin with the periventricular zone), and we performed a dual-Gaussian fit of this distance distribution. A distant value corresponding to approximately 3 σ (~15 µm) of the first Gaussian fit (imposed to be centered at 0) was set as the threshold to define SINs (distance &lt;3 σ ), while the remaining, more distal neurons (distance &gt;3 σ ) were defined as DINs. For the direction selectivity (DSI) analysis, only neurons that responded to at least one of the gratings were selected. The response amplitudes were defined as the maximum ∆ F / F over the ten frames following the stimulus onset. Responsive neurons were defined when response amplitudes were larger than four times the s.d. (evaluated in the 20 frames before stimulus onset). For each of the eight moving-bar stimuli, a vector was defined by its angle corresponding to the bar direction and its response amplitude. The DSI was calculated as the magnitude of the vectorial sum of these eight vectors, normalized by the sum of their individual magnitudes (Extended Data Fig. 6d ). We separated neurons into two equal-sized groups (according to the dye ratio) and plotted the distribution of preferred directions (Extended Data Fig. 6e ). Spontaneous calcium imaging analysis WHaloCaMP fluorescence transients from spinning-disk recordings were extracted by manually defining ROIs over individual cell bodies, thereby identifying each tectal neuron (&gt;600) present in the recorded plane. After motion correction and registration of the two color images, fluorescence values of each individual neuron were combined to compute their dual-dye intensity ratio (JF 552 /(JF 552 + JF 669 )). This ratio was used to cluster neurons into early-born and late-born populations (using k -means clustering, k = 2 clusters; Supplementary Fig. 11b ). After ROI-based background subtraction, raw fluorescence time traces were extracted for each neuron by spatially integrating signals over the corresponding ROI. Following detrending, Δ F / F was computed using a baseline defined as the tenth percentile of each trace, with baseline values verified by manual inspection. Before quantifying activity, traces were denoised using second-order autoregressive deconvolution implemented through the OASIS package 74 , which provided more meaningful denoising than smoothing or low-pass filtering approaches. Activity quantification was then performed on the denoised traces using automatic peak detection (minimum peak prominence &gt;2 × signal-to-noise ratio (SNR); minimum peak distance: 25 frames). Neurons were initially classified as active if they exhibited at least one Δ F / F event exceeding 3%. Automatic classifications were subsequently refined through manual verification using a custom MATLAB GUI that enabled rapid browsing of individual traces and user-guided adjustment of detected peaks and activity status. The resulting metrics, mean peak frequency and mean peak amplitude (averaged across all events of each cell), are reported for active cells (Fig. 6c ) and were used to compare spontaneous activity between early-born and late-born neuronal populations. Importantly, previous characterization 45 demonstrated similar sensitivity of WHaloCaMP transients when using either JF 552 or JF 669 dyes, ensuring that observed differences in fluorescence reflect variations in neuronal activity between the two populations. In addition, the high selectivity in the excitation and detection of each dye minimizes cross-talk, avoiding contamination of the functional signal due to dual-color labeling. Spontaneous voltage imaging analysis Voltron2-JF 552 and Voltron2-JF 585 fluorescence transients obtained from light-sheet imaging were recorded from labeled neurons in the zebrafish olfactory epithelium. The sparse labeling in the tissue enabled manual identification of neurons predominantly expressing either JF 552 or JF 585 , corresponding to early-born and late-born cellular populations, respectively. Fluorescence traces were extracted by integrating signal intensities over ROIs placed on individual cell bodies, followed by background subtraction and detrending. Δ F / F was then computed for each neuron. Cells exhibiting narrow (&lt;3 ms) Δ F / F transients above the noise level, corresponding to putative spiking activity, were classified as active. Spiking frequency was quantified by manually counting spikes in these active neurons. Analysis of longitudinal functional imaging of the hindbrain Image processing The GCaMP channel of the functional imaging data was used as a source to determine the mismatch between the odd and even scan lines and the lateral shift of the sample in each plane by phase cross-correlation (the phase_cross_correlation function from scikit-image library). Then, these corrections were applied to all the channels, and a high SNR volume was generated for each channel by averaging across all the time points. Bleed-through of the GCaMP signal to the JF 585 and JF 646 channels was estimated on the basis of the voxels in the top 10% of GCaMP signals with a linear regression. The JF 585 and JF 646 channels were subtracted with the bleed-through estimated voxel by voxel, and then the subtracted volume was normalized between the median and 99.99-percentile voxel values as described above. The summed image of the two JF dye signals was then upsampled at two times higher resolution and fed to the nuclei detection algorithm on the basis of local contrast 75 . Subsequently, remaining local peaks from neuronal nuclei were divided into two neuronal age groups using sigma clipping. This procedure is motivated by our observation that the second age group corresponds to the long tail on the higher angle. Thus, the second age group was defined as the group with the angle higher than three s.d. away from the Gaussian distribution detected by sigma clipping. The ∆ F / F 0 of GCaMP signal from individual neurons was calculated by dividing the raw fluorescence signal from each identified nucleus with the baseline estimated as a 20-percentile value within a 60-s rolling window and used in the following regression and clustering analyses. Analysis of locomotor activity EMG traces were processed to detect stimulus-induced escapes and spontaneous swims as follows. First, large transient deflections caused by the electrical stimuli were identified by the find_peaks function from scipy library and the time windows dominated by the artifacts were blanked (typically −1.5 to +5 ms around the peak of the artifact). A slow-decay component of the artifact after large transients was modeled by the interp1d function from SciPy and subtracted from the trace. Then, the EMG traces from the left and the right sides of the axial muscles were processed as follows to generate a smoothed EMG amplitude estimate robust against noise. They were first z -score normalized, then their power was calculated in a Gaussian-kernel window (8 ms at full-width at half maximum), and its square root product was used as a robust EMG amplitude estimate. Then, the peaks corresponding to each burst of muscle activity were detected by the find_peaks function for the left and right EMG traces separately. To reject the peaks corresponding to the strong EMG bursts from the other side, the following procedure was taken. After merging the peaks from both sides, each pair of neighboring bursts was compared, and if the interpeak interval was shorter than 6 ms, the smaller peak was excluded from further analysis. Then, swim episodes were defined as a series of bursts with inter-burst intervals below 70 ms. Instances of burst groupings with fewer than four bursts (corresponding to two left–right alternations) were not classified as swim episodes. Regression analysis of locomotor activity To map the neurons recruited during distinct types of locomotor activity, regression analysis was performed on ∆ F / F 0 of the GCaMP signal extracted from individual nuclei using regressors representing distinct swim events. In both developmental stages, escape events were defined on the basis of the electrical shock that reliably induced escape behavior. In 48-h.p.f. embryos, another regressor was constructed to capture the variability in the length of shock-induced swim episodes by treating each detected burst from EMG traces from both sides. In 96-h.p.f. embryos, we constructed three regressors besides the escape regressor based on the expanded range of EMG burst amplitudes (Fig. 5c ), similarly to ref. 10 . Bursts from each EMG trace were classified as ‘weak’ or ‘strong’ using a GMM with two components. Then, the ‘strong’ bursts from left and right EMG traces were treated as separate events on the basis of our previous observation that distinct sets of neurons are recruited on the basis of the side of strong locomotor activity. The ‘weak’ bursts from left and right EMG traces were combined together as in ref. 10 given the slow kinetics of jGCaMP7s relative to left–right alternating bursting activity. Regressors were constructed by convolving each of these locomotor event types with the GCaMP6s impulse response function given its similar kinetics to jGCaMP7s 76 and then z -score standardized for each session. Neuronal ∆ F / F 0 response matrix ( Y ) was fitted with these standardized regressors ( X ) using the following linear model, Y = X β + ϵ . The standardized coefficient ( β ) was estimated by ordinary least squares, and then the T value for each neuron was calculated on the basis of the standardized coefficient ( β ) and residual ( ϵ ). Correction for multiple comparisons was performed with the false discovery rate (FDR). The threshold for T maps was set at P FDR &lt; 0.05. The neurons that met these criteria for a given locomotor event were classified as active neurons. Analysis of all-optical experiment For the all-optical experiments in Fig. 6g–i , fluorescence signals were integrated over 8-µm circular ROIs centered on individual somata selected for holographic stimulation. Photostimulation artifacts were semi-automatically detected and removed using a GUI-based MATLAB script. The percentage change in fluorescence was calculated as ∆ F / F = ( F − F 0 )/ F 0 , where F 0 is the baseline fluorescence level. F 0 was defined within the MATLAB GUI as the average fluorescence over a ~3-s time window preceding holographic stimulation and manually adjusted when necessary, to exclude distortions from spontaneous activity. Photoinduced ∆ F / F peaks were automatically identified, manually verified and averaged across stimulation events. Statistics and reproducibility No statistical method was used to predetermine sample sizes, but our sample sizes are similar to those generally used in the field. For each experiment, fish were chosen at random from batches of healthy siblings that had developed normally, and, unless otherwise stated, all randomly sampled fish were included in the analyses; any exclusion criteria are specified in the corresponding sections (for example, image volumes that could not be properly registered to the reference atlas were excluded). Data collection and analysis were not performed blind to the conditions of the experiments. For readouts that were not normally distributed (for example, the proportion of labeled volume or of active neurons), we did not rely on statistical tests that assume normality and equal variance; instead, we used generalized linear and generalized linear mixed models with link functions appropriate to the data distribution (for example, a logit link for binomially distributed proportions), as well as Kruskal–Wallis ANOVA, Kolmogorov–Smirnov and Spearman tests. The assumptions of these models—approximate normality and equal variance of the residuals on the link-function scale—were examined rather than formally tested, and the data met these assumptions. Student’s t -tests and parametric ANOVAs were performed after normality, and homogeneity of variance were formally tested using Shapiro–Wilk and Levene tests, respectively. The Welch’s t -test was used for normally distributed data with unequal variances.
