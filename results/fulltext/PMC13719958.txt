Introduction Skeletal muscle constitutes approximately 45% of total body weight in vertebrates and represents one of the most metabolically dynamic tissues in mammalian systems. These striated muscle tissues facilitate locomotion through coordinated muscle contraction and play a central role in developmental growth and metabolic adaptation [ 1 , 2 ]. As the fundamental contractile units, individual muscle fibers demonstrate remarkable phenotypic plasticity. Based on the subtypes of myosin heavy chain (MyHC), the skeletal muscle types are systematically categorized into type I (MyHC I), type IIa (MyHC IIa), type IIx (MyHC IIx), and type IIb (MyHC IIb) [ 2 ]. The conversion of muscle fiber types follows a general pattern of sequential and reversible transitions, occurring bidirectionally between fast and slow fibers. This pattern is represented by the sequential transitions: type I ↔ type IIa ↔ type IIx ↔ type IIb [ 3 – 5 ]. Disruptions in this balance—such as a pathological shift toward glycolytic fibers, reduced oxidative capacity, or impaired fiber type adaptation—are hallmarks of numerous muscle-related disorders, including muscular dystrophies, sarcopenia, and insulin resistance [ 6 – 8 ]. For instance, patients with Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD) exhibit progressive loss of oxidative fibers and accumulation of degenerative glycolytic fibers, which exacerbates muscle weakness and metabolic dysfunction [ 9 , 10 ]. Thus, deciphering the molecular mechanisms governing muscle fiber type conversion is not only fundamental to understanding muscle biology but also holds direct translational relevance for developing targeted therapies for muscle degenerative and metabolic diseases. MyoD family inhibitor (Mdfi, also known as I-mfa) is an inhibitor of the MyoD myogenic regulatory family, with its genomic loci identified on human chromosome 6 and mouse chromosome 17 [ 11 ]. Existing data indicate that Mdfi regulates placental development, skeletal and spinal development, human diseases, and carcinogenesis [ 12 – 19 ]. Notably, studies on myogenic differentiation have revealed cell type-specific regulatory effects: Mdfi suppresses differentiation in non-myogenic cells such as NIH-3T3 fibroblasts and P19 embryonal carcinoma cells [ 20 , 21 ], whereas its structural domain MDFIC enhances differentiation in avian models, specifically chicken embryonic primary myoblasts (CPMs) and quail myoblast cell lines (QM-7) [ 22 ]. Intriguingly, we have observed paradoxical regulatory patterns in mammalian systems – Mdfi overexpression potentiated myogenic differentiation in C2C12 murine myoblasts [ 23 ] but inhibited this process in porcine satellite cells (PSCs) [ 24 ]. Moreover, the injection of lentiviral vectors that interfere with Mdfi significantly promoted muscle fiber hypertrophy and increased myofiber diameter and area in the leg muscles of mice [ 24 ]. These results suggest that the regulation of myogenic differentiation by Mdfi in cells from different species and cell models is inconsistent. Additionally, we have demonstrated that Mdfi overexpression in C2C12 and PSCs promoted the conversion of cellular myofiber types from glycolytic to oxidative [ 23 – 25 ], positioning Mdfi as a potential master regulator of muscle metabolic phenotype. Given the critical role of a slow, oxidative fiber profile in mitigating disease severity in models like DMD [ 10 ], understanding how Mdfi orchestrates this shift has significant implications for identifying novel therapeutic targets for neuromuscular diseases. The CRISPR/Cas9 system has emerged as a groundbreaking genome-editing technology, distinguished by its operational simplicity, high targeting efficiency, and capacity for multiplex genome modification across diverse species [ 26 – 31 ]. Notably, temporal control systems, such as the inducible Cre-loxP mechanisms, have been developed to complement CRISPR-Cas9 technology by addressing spatial and temporal resolution in gene function analysis, enabling the precise investigation of gene function during specific developmental stages or through lineage tracing via conditional gene targeting. The Cre-loxP system enables spatiotemporal gene editing in murine models by combining Cre driver strains (expressing tissue-specific recombinase) with floxed mice (containing loxP-flanked target genes) [ 32 ]. The optimized Cre ERT2 system achieves precise temporal control through TMX induction, with successful applications including neural lineage tracing in brain development studies and targeted oncogene activation in pancreatic cancer models, demonstrating superior sensitivity and reduced baseline activity compared to earlier versions [ 33 – 36 ]. The synergistic combination of CRISPR/Cas9’s editing precision with the temporal control offered by optimized Cre-lox systems, such as Cre ERT2, provides a robust methodological framework for dissecting gene function in vivo, which is essential for modeling complex processes like postnatal muscle adaptation and disease progression. To systematically elucidate the role of Mdfi in muscle plasticity, we employed CRISPR/Cas9 coupled with the Cre ERT2 system to develop a TMX-inducible, skeletal muscle-specific Mdfi overexpression murine model. This approach enables age-specific interrogation of Mdfi’s regulatory roles in postnatal myogenesis. Concurrently, we assessed the morphology, behavior, and genetic stability of the induced skeletal muscle-specific Mdfi overexpression in mice. Through integrated in vivo and in vitro analyses, we further delineated the molecular mechanism underlying Mdfi-mediated muscle fiber type conversion. This study advances our understanding of Mdfi’s functional orchestration of skeletal muscle plasticity. The results offer insights into the regulation of muscle metabolism and contractile function, with potential implications for developing strategies to modulate fiber type in therapeutic contexts. Results Development of a skeletal muscle-specific inducible Mdfi overexpression mouse model To further investigate the function of Mdfi overexpression in vivo, we constructed a conditional Mdfi-overexpressing mouse model (Fig. S1 ). These conditional Mdfi-overexpressing mice were subsequently crossed with Cre mice containing skeletal muscle-specific promoters to establish TMX-inducible, skeletal muscle-specific Mdfi-overexpressing mice (Fig. 1 A). Genotyping confirmation of the resultant mice is shown in Fig. 1 B. Following intraperitoneal TMX administration, qPCR results reveal that only Rosa26 LSL/+ ;Cre mice injected with TMX exhibited a high increase in Mdfi mRNA ( P &lt; 0.001), with no detectable leakage expression in other three groups ( P &gt; 0.05; Fig. 1 C). The WB results were consistent with the qPCR results (Fig. 1 D). We further evaluated Mdfi overexpression efficiency across eleven skeletal muscle tissues and fifteen non-muscle tissues. The results show that Mdfi mRNA only increased in skeletal muscle tissues ( P &lt; 0.05) and non-skeletal muscle tissues maintained baseline expression levels ( P &gt; 0.05) (Fig. 1 E). Among skeletal muscles, the highest Mdfi expression was in the calf muscles ( P &lt; 0.05) (Fig. 1 F). Given its predominant mass within the hind limb calf muscles, the gastrocnemius muscle (GAS) was selected for subsequent functional analyses. Therefore, we have successfully established an inducible skeletal muscle-specific Mdfi overexpression model with strict tissue specificity. TMX-induced Mdfi overexpression was initiated at two critical developmental stages: the weaning period (3 weeks, representing early muscle development) and somatic maturity (8 weeks, representing mature muscle). Following Mdfi overexpression during early muscle development (Fig. 2 A), the time-course body-weight growth curve shows no differences between Rosa26 LSL/+ ;Cre + TMX groups and Rosa26 LSL/+ + TMX groups across all weeks ( P &gt; 0.05, observed in both sexes). But the growth trait analysis demonstrates that Rosa26 LSL/+ ;Cre + TMX groups exhibited reduced body weight gain compared to Rosa26 LSL/+ + TMX controls ( P &lt; 0.01, observed in both sexes). Feed intake displays no alterations in either sex ( P &gt; 0.05). Female Rosa26 LSL/+ ;Cre + TMX mice show an elevated feed-to-weight gain ratio (F/G) than female controls ( P &lt; 0.01), but no differences in male mice ( P &gt; 0.05). These findings indicate that skeletal muscle-specific Mdfi overexpression during early postnatal development impairs growth performance in mice. Next, we evaluated glucose metabolism, growth, carcass, and body size traits following Mdfi overexpression induction during skeletal muscle maturation. These results reveal no differences in growth traits (Fig. 3 A), glucose metabolism (Fig. 3 B), carcass and body size traits (Fig. 3 C-D) between Rosa26 LSL/+ ;Cre + TMX mice and Rosa26 LSL/+ + TMX controls ( P &gt; 0.05, both sexes). Given this null phenotype in heterozygous adults, we expanded our investigation to homozygous mice. No alterations in glucose metabolism, carcass, or body size traits were observed in homozygous adults ( P &gt; 0.05, both sexes). These results demonstrate that Mdfi overexpression during mature skeletal muscle development does not affect glucose metabolism, growth, carcass, or body size traits. Consequently, subsequent Mdfi-mediated fiber type transformation investigations were restricted to the maturation phase to eliminate confounding effects from early-age myogenesis. The results of grip strength and aerobic running capacity show that Mdfi overexpression reduced four-limb grip strength ( P &lt; 0.001, Fig. 5 A) but increased endurance running distance ( P &lt; 0.05, Fig. 5 B). We then analyzed the skeletal muscle morphology and the composition of muscle fiber subtypes. Network interaction analysis coupled with KEGG pathway enrichment identified seven enriched signaling pathways (Fig. 6 F-G, Table S3 ). Subsequent validation in both murine skeletal muscle tissue (Fig. S3 ) and MuSC (Fig. 6 H) demonstrate that Mdfi modulates myofiber type conversion through coordinated regulation of multiple pathways and genes: the calcium signaling pathway (Camk2b, Camkk2, Calml4, Ip3r, and Ryr3), cAMP signaling pathway (Akap12, Plcb1, Adcy2, and Rapgef3), Wnt signaling pathway (Wnt5a), PI3K-Akt signaling pathway (Pik3cb, Pik3ca, Akt1, Akt2, Gsk3b, and Pten), mTOR signaling pathway (Mtor, Mlst8, and Rictor), insulin signaling pathway (Irs1), and AMPK signaling pathway (Ampk and Pgc1-α). To further elucidate the molecular mechanism underlying Mdfi-mediated skeletal muscle fiber type transformation, we empirically focused on the calcium signaling pathway, which has the highest P -value (Table S3 ). No differences were observed between homozygous and heterozygous mice ( P &gt; 0.05) (Fig. S4 ). But treatment with ryanodine alone, U-73,122 alone, or a combination of both did not result in significant differences in the expression levels of MYH7 and MYH2 ( P &gt; 0.05) (Fig. 7 E and G). Therefore, these findings suggest that Mdfi overexpression counteracts the suppressive effects of calcium channel inhibitors on calcium signaling and downstream regulators (CAMKK2, AMPK, and PGC1α), thereby facilitating the shift of muscle fiber subtypes from glycolytic to oxidative. Discussion Age-dependent effects of skeletal muscle-specific Mdfi overexpression on skeletal muscle development To further investigate how Mdfi regulates muscle fiber type conversion, we created a TMX-inducible, skeletal muscle-specific Mdfi-overexpressing mouse model using CRISPR/Cas9 technology combined with the Cre ERT2 system. We validated the integrity of the model through multiple approaches: correct gene targeting and the absence of ectopic integration were confirmed by genotyping and Southern blot analysis, while Sanger sequencing verified precise homologous recombination without LSL cassette leakage. Furthermore, protein-level analysis demonstrated that MDFI expression was robustly and specifically upregulated exclusively upon tamoxifen-induced Cre recombination. This tightly controlled expression system provides a reliable tool for subsequent investigations into the role of MDFI in skeletal muscle fiber type determination and metabolic regulation. Using this tightly controlled inducible model, we observed that skeletal muscle-specific Mdfi overexpression exerts age-dependent effects on muscle development. The rationale for this experimental design stems from conflicting reports in existing literature regarding Mdfi’s function in myogenic differentiation. The role of Mdfi in muscle biology has been controversial, with prior studies reporting seemingly contradictory effects on myogenic differentiation. Our findings provide a unifying framework that reconciles these discrepancies. Current understanding unveils three distinct patterns: (1) inhibition of differentiation by overexpressing Mdfi in non-myogenic stem cells [ 20 , 21 ], (2) promotion of differentiation by overexpressing Mdfi in CPMs and QM-7 myoblasts [ 22 ], (3) high expression of Mdfi mRNA level in CTX induced muscle injury tissue and Duchenne muscular dystrophy mouse muscle tissue [ 38 , 39 ], and 4) stage-dependent effects of elevated Mdfi observed in our prior studies. Specifically, Mdfi overexpression inhibits myogenic differentiation in a transient model of PSCs, while interference with Mdfi promotes muscle hypertrophy in 3-week-old mice [ 24 ]. Conversely, elevated Mdfi promotes myogenic differentiation in a steady-state C2C12 cell model [ 23 ]. These paradoxical findings suggest that Mdfi’s regulatory function exhibits species-specificity and experimental model dependency, particularly varying between transient vs. stable expression systems and developmental stages. In this study, we established a skeletal muscle-specific mouse model exhibiting sustained, stable Mdfi overexpression. Inducing skeletal muscle-specific overexpression of Mdfi during early postnatal development attenuates body weight gain and adversely affects key slaughter performance, including body weight and skeletal muscle weight. However, when overexpressed in mature skeletal muscle tissue at postnatal week eight, Mdfi exhibits no detectable influence on muscle growth. Three principal factors may account for the observed divergence from previous in vitro findings: (1) interspecies variation in myogenic regulation, (2) inherent physiological differences between intact organismal systems and ex vivo models, particularly regarding endocrine and paracrine signaling networks, and (3) the continuous high expression of Mdfi in our mouse model, as opposed to transient expression, allowing Mdfi to exert sustained effects. Our study thus emphasizes the necessity of in vivo validation for interpreting in vitro findings and establishes a context-dependent paradigm for understanding Mdfi function in muscle biology. Notably, Mdfi exerts a dual-phase regulatory role in skeletal muscle development, with distinct effects on early postnatal myogenesis and mature muscle fiber-type conversion. Preliminary analysis suggests that this stage-specific regulatory pattern may be attributed to the comprehensive effects of multiple factors. First, Mdfi exhibits dynamic expression characteristics during skeletal muscle development, with its expression level varying significantly between the early postnatal period (when myoblasts are in active proliferation and differentiation) and the mature stage (when muscle fibers maintain a stable phenotype), which may directly affect its regulatory potency and target specificity. Second, the cellular microenvironment differs remarkably between early postnatal myoblasts and mature muscle fibers: early myogenesis is dominated by proliferation and differentiation signals, while mature muscle fibers are in a relatively quiescent state and mainly respond to metabolic and functional regulation signals; this difference in microenvironment may alter the functional status of Mdfi and its interaction with downstream molecules. Third, Mdfi may interact with different downstream partners in a stage-specific manner—binding to molecules involved in myoblast proliferation/differentiation during early postnatal development, while targeting factors related to muscle fiber-type transformation (such as calcium signaling and metabolic pathway molecules) in mature muscle, thereby mediating distinct regulatory effects. Given that Mdfi does not affect the development of mature skeletal muscle in mice, we conducted more in-depth studies at the stage of skeletal muscle maturation. Mdfi overexpression does not affect morphology, behavior, metabolism, carcass traits, and reproductive traits As genetically modified animal technologies advance and gain broader application, growing attention has been directed toward assessing their biological safety. Key health evaluation metrics for transgenic models should systematically address multiple dimensions including: (1) external morphology and behavioral patterns, (2) hematological and biochemical parameters, (3) organ homeostasis, (4) metabolic regulation, (5) reproductive competence, and (6) genetic integrity [ 40 – 42 ]. In this study, we observed that the overexpression of mature skeletal muscle-specific Mdfi in mice does not exhibit any overall abnormalities in morphology or behavioral characteristics. Serum biochemical analysis shows that hepatic function markers, renal function parameters, and lipid metabolism indicators remain within normal reference ranges. Organ-to-body weight ratios of major visceral organs (heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys) show no alterations. Normal glucose metabolism profiles are confirmed through IPGTT. Results from the six-generation breeding experiments indicate comparable reproductive capacity. Overall, these multilayered assessments demonstrate that the mature skeletal muscle-specific Mdfi-overexpressing mice model, constructed using CRISPR/Cas9 technology and the Cre ERT2 system, maintains physiological homeostasis across major biological systems, establishing its suitability for subsequent mechanistic studies. Effects of Mdfi overexpression on muscle fiber-type transition, muscle enzyme activity, exercise capacity, and serum parameters in mature skeletal muscle In this study, we investigated the role of Mdfi and its molecular mechanisms during muscle fiber type transformation in murine mature skeletal muscle. Our findings demonstrate that Mdfi overexpression during mature skeletal muscle development neither alters myofiber quantity nor cross-sectional area, yet enhances the transition from glycolytic to oxidative fiber types. These observations align with our previous findings in C2C12 and PSCs [ 23 , 24 ]. Although it is widely accepted that glycolytic myofibers tend to have a larger cross-sectional area than oxidative myofibers, this relationship is not absolute [ 43 ]. Multiple confounding factors may influence this relationship, including: (1) substantial variation in myofiber cross-sectional area, (2) differences in myosin distribution between muscles, (3) interspecies variability, (4) sexual dimorphism, and (5) changes throughout the lifespan. The composition of muscle fiber types exhibits strong correlations with metabolic characteristics, exercise capacity, and serum markers [ 44 , 45 ]. In this study, our investigation reveals that mature skeletal muscle-specific Mdfi overexpression elicits three coordinated phenotypic alterations: (1) enhances endurance capacity coupled with diminished explosive force generation; (2) metabolic reprogramming favoring mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation over glycolysis; (3) systemic elevation of calcium, phosphate, and IGF-1 serum levels. The skeletal muscle fiber divergence originates from distinct metabolic profiles: slow-twitch fibers predominantly employ oxidative phosphorylation, whereas fast-twitch fibers rely on glycolytic pathways for rapid energy production [ 46 – 48 ]. Elevating the proportion of slow-twitch fibers in murine models enhances endurance and improves performance in endurance-related activities [ 49 , 50 ]. Notably, nutritional interventions have a substantial influence on muscle physiology. For example, vitamin D deficiency induces muscle atrophy, as reflected in reduced cross-sectional area and compromised contractile strength—pathological manifestations that are reversible via calcium supplementation [ 51 ]. Hypophosphatemia may progress from muscular weakness to severe cardiovascular impairment, highlighting phosphate homeostasis as a critical regulatory axis [ 52 ]. Overexpression of the IGF-1 gene has been shown to reduce the proportion of type IIB muscle fibers [ 53 ]. These literatures further corroborate our findings: Mdfi overexpression is positively correlated with oxidative muscle fibers, muscle fatigue resistance, serum calcium, phosphorus, and IGF1 levels, and negatively correlated with glycolytic muscle fibers, muscle explosive power, and lactate dehydrogenase activity. In addition, our comprehensive multi-dimensional assessment—integrating muscle enzyme activity assays, fiber-type distribution analysis, physiological performance tests, and serum metabolic parameters—provides robust evidence that Mdfi overexpression promotes oxidative fiber-type transition. While histochemical staining and immunofluorescence analyses would offer more direct visualization at the single-fiber level, the convergence of evidence from functional, metabolic, and systemic levels consistently supports our conclusions. We acknowledge a limitation of the current study: histochemical staining or immunofluorescence analyses for LDH and SDH at the single-fiber level were not performed, because the original tissue samples were exhausted and the mouse line is currently cryopreserved. While our functional, metabolic, and systemic data provide convergent evidence, direct visualization of enzyme activity in individual fibers would further strengthen the conclusions. Future studies using re-derived animals from the cryopreserved line will incorporate these complementary approaches. Furthermore, these Mdfi overexpression-induced phenotypic alterations display stable transgenerational inheritance patterns, which is an unexpected observation in this study. Although epigenetic modifications such as DNA methylation, histone modification, or chromatin remodeling may contribute to this heritable phenotype, the detailed molecular mechanism remains to be further explored. Notably, the observed inheritance characteristics show no additive effects in homozygous specimens compared to heterozygous counterparts. This dosage-independent phenomenon suggests that heterozygous Mdfi-overexpressing models may be particularly valuable for functional genetic studies. Subsequent investigations using appropriate model systems will help determine whether skeletal muscle-specific Mdfi manipulation similarly influences metabolic and contractile properties in other biological contexts. Molecular mechanisms underlying Mdfi overexpression in promoting skeletal muscle fiber type conversion in mature skeletal muscle We further elucidated the molecular mechanisms by which the overexpression of mature skeletal muscle-specific Mdfi promotes the transformation of skeletal muscle fiber types from glycolytic to oxidative in murine models. Through bioinformatics analysis and screening, we identified and preliminarily validated seven candidate signaling pathways that may be involved in this phenotypic conversion, including the calcium signaling pathway, PI3K-Akt signaling pathway, cAMP signaling pathway, mTOR signaling pathway, Wnt signaling pathway, insulin signaling pathway, and AMPK signaling pathway. Of particular mechanistic significance, we found that MDFI overexpression counteracts the pharmacological inhibition by ryanodine (a RYR channel blocker) and U-73,122 (an IP3R inhibitor). These results suggest a functional interaction between Mdfi and calcium channel activity, although the direct molecular mechanism by which Mdfi regulates RYR3 or IP3R gating or expression remains to be elucidated. Our findings further indicate that calcium-channel inhibition potently modulates the expression of fast-twitch myosin heavy chain isoforms, with Mdfi overexpression further enhancing the downregulation of Myh1 and Myh4, while reversing the Ryanodine-induced reduction of Myh2. This highlights a coordinated role of Mdfi and calcium signaling in fine-tuning fast-fiber subtype specification. The consistent lack of change in MYH7 expression across all treatments aligns with its biological characteristics: as a stably expressed slow-twitch marker with low transcriptional plasticity, MYH7 is far less responsive to acute pharmacological interventions compared to fast-fiber genes [ 54 – 58 ]. Collectively, these data are consistent with a model in which Mdfi-mediated fiber-type switching is predominantly achieved through the calcium-dependent regulation of fast-twitch, rather than slow-twitch, myosin isoform expression. However, the precise molecular mechanism by which Mdfi directly interacts with or regulates calcium channels (e.g., RyR3 and IP3R) remains to be elucidated. Future studies employing co-immunoprecipitation, electrophysiological recordings, or channel activity assays are needed to determine whether Mdfi physically associates with these channels or modulates their expression/activity indirectly. In addition, elevated Mdfi is associated with enhanced calcium flux through both receptor types (RYR and IP3R), and with activation of pCaMKK2 and pAMPK. This calcium surge coincided with activation of the CaMKK2-AMPK axis and upregulation of PGC1α expression. As a master regulator of mitochondrial biogenesis, PGC1α ultimately drives the specification of oxidative fibers. Notably, this calcium-dependent regulatory cascade aligns with our prior findings in C2C12 myotubes [ 25 ], reinforcing the translational relevance of both cellular and animal models. Nevertheless, it is important to note that while our data strongly support the involvement of the CaMKK2-AMPK-PGC1α pathway, loss-of-function experiments (e.g., pharmacological inhibition or genetic knockout of CaMKK2 or AMPK) are required to establish its necessity. Therefore, we interpret this pathway as a candidate mediator rather than a definitively established causal mechanism. Although other intracellular calcium channels are present, RyRs and IP3R represent the two largest ion channels in the sarcoplasmic/endoplasmic reticulum [ 59 , 60 ], with molecular weights of approximately 2.2 and 1.2 MDa, respectively. These channels supply the calcium required for excitation-contraction coupling in skeletal muscle [ 61 , 62 ]. Specifically, ryanodine, calcium, and ATP bind to distinct sites on the RYRs to regulate their gating activity, thereby governing calcium release [ 63 ]. Meanwhile, external signals trigger G protein-coupled receptors on the cell membrane, initiating a cascade that activates phospholipase C, which in turn generates IP3. This second messenger subsequently binds to IP3Rs, inducing calcium efflux [ 60 ]. Previous studies have demonstrated that elevated Mdfi upregulates Ryr3 expression in C2C12 cells without affecting Ryr1 [ 25 ], which is consistent with our results. Building on this evidence, our study establishes that Mdfi selectively modulates the release of calcium ions through IP3R and RYR3 channels, excluding the contribution of extracellular calcium influx. We observed that CAMKK2, pAMPK, AMPK, and PGC1α proteins expression were increased after ryanodine and U‑73,122 treatment, and decreased following MDFI overexpression. This trend appears somewhat different from the expected response to calcium channel inhibition. We speculate that this unexpected trend may reflect a compensatory feedback mechanism triggered by long‑term or mild calcium handling disturbance, rather than an immediate acute response. This regulatory pattern may be related to the specific cellular context and the duration of drug treatment in our model. Beyond the well-characterized calcium signaling pathway and its downstream CaMKK2-AMPK-PGC1α axis, we have identified several additional signaling pathways, including PI3K-AKT, mTOR, Wnt, cAMP, and insulin signaling pathways. These signaling pathways have been implicated in the conversion of muscle fiber types [ 64 – 67 ]. However, the precise mechanistic links between these pathways and Mdfi-induced muscle fiber type conversion remain poorly defined, highlighting a critical gap in current knowledge that warrants systematic exploration. However, the direct regulatory mechanism linking Mdfi to the calcium signaling pathway, as well as the specific downstream molecules mediating mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation regulation, require further investigation to fully delineate the regulatory network through which Mdfi governs muscle fiber-type conversion. Additionally, although our multi-omics and molecular data are consistent with the involvement of the CAMKK2/AMPK/PGC1α pathway in Mdfi‑mediated muscle fiber‑type conversion, the present study does not provide direct evidence for its absolute necessity or exclusivity. Further genetic or pharmacological loss‑of‑function studies will be required to confirm whether blocking this pathway abolishes or attenuates the effects of Mdfi on skeletal muscle fiber type switching. Accordingly, our conclusions regarding this pathway remain preliminary and warrant cautious interpretation. In this study, we establish the first TMX-inducible, skeletal muscle-specific Mdfi overexpression mouse model using CRISPR/Cas9 and Cre ERT2 integration, offering a precision tool for temporally controlled genetic studies in myogenesis. Mdfi as a critical regulator with dual-phase roles: it suppresses early myogenesis while steering glycolytic-to-oxidative fiber type switching in mature muscle. Mechanistically, elevated Mdfi activates the calcium signaling pathway to enhance mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation, directly linking its function to improved muscle endurance—a discovery with implications for sports science and rehabilitation strategies aimed at optimizing metabolic efficiency. These findings provide crucial insights into muscle plasticity and highlight Mdfi’s potential relevance for treating muscular dystrophies by modulating metabolic function and regeneration. Notably, previous studies have reported altered expression of Mdfi in muscle tissues from Duchenne muscular dystrophy mouse models and CTX-induced acute muscle injury models, further supporting its potential involvement in pathological muscle adaptation. Our model offers a valuable tool for future research into therapeutic strategies for neuromuscular disorders. Materials and methods Animals To generate conditional Mdfi-overexpressing heterozygous mice, we used CRISPR/Cas9-mediated homologous recombination to integrate the CAG-LSL-Kozak-Mdfi-P2A-EGFP-polyA expression cassette into the Rosa26 locus. The experimental workflow comprised four key steps: (i) In vitro transcription-produced Cas9 mRNA and guide RNA (gRNA, 5’-3’: GGGGACACACTAAGGGAGCTTGG), (ii) Construction of a donor vector via In-Fusion cloning containing 3.3 kb 5’ and 3’ homology arms flanking the CAG-LSL-Kozak-Mdfi-P2A-EGFP-polyA cassette, (iii) Microinjection of Cas9 mRNA, sgRNA, and donor vector into C57BL/6J fertilized oocytes for F0 generation production, (iv) PCR/sequencing-confirmed F0 mice were outcrossed with wild-type C57BL/6J mice to obtain positive founder mice verified by Southern blot analysis. All founder mice were generated and genetically validated by Shanghai Model Organisms Center, Inc. For skeletal muscle-specific induction, the conditional heterozygous mice (designated Rosa26 LSL/+ ) were crossed with Tg(ACTA1-cre/Esr1*)2Kesr/J mice (JAX Strain #025750), followed by TMX administration to activate recombination (heterozygous: Rosa26 LSL/+ ;Cre, heterozygous control: Rosa26 LSL/+ , homozygous: Rosa26 LSL/LSL ;Cre, homozygous control: Rosa26 LSL/LSL ). Mice were housed under standard 12:12-hour light-dark cycles with ad libitum access to food. After genotype confirmation, male and female cohorts were randomly allocated to experimental groups. Tamoxifen induction Tamoxifen (TMX) was prepared as a 20 mg/mL stock solution by dissolution in corn oil with continuous shaking at 37 °C for 12–16 h. The homogeneous solution was aliquoted and stored at 4 °C under light-protected conditions before administration. Intraperitoneal injections were administered daily (75 mg/kg body weight per dose) for five consecutive days, in accordance with established pharmacokinetic protocols [ 68 , 69 ]. Strict aseptic techniques were implemented, including disinfection of the injection site with 75% ethanol before each procedure. Two distinct developmental phases were targeted for TMX administration: 1) Early postnatal stage: Initiated at three weeks postnatally (post-weaning, skeletal muscle development phase). 2) Adult stage: Initiated at eight weeks of age (skeletal muscle maturity phase). A seven-day washout period was systematically observed between the final injection and subsequent phenotypic analyses to ensure complete drug clearance. Assessment of murine morphology, behavior, reproductive performance, and genetic stability In this investigation, we systematically evaluated the developmental health of Rosa26 LSL/+ ;Cre + TMX offspring through quantitative assessment of multiple morphological parameters. These included limb morphology, neurological status, behavioral patterns, integumentary features (hair coat quality), craniofacial development (ocular structure, oral cavity, and dental arrangement), mammary gland distribution (nipple count), and urogenital system morphology. Subsequent reproductive performance analysis encompassed six filial generations (F1-F6), with detailed recording of parturition metrics: litter size, live births, birth weight, weaning survival, and weaning weight. Neonatal sex identification was performed through standardized genital tubercle examination at postnatal day five. In murine multigenerational inheritance studies, the F2 and F3 generations represent critical stages for phenotypic segregation and stabilization [ 70 ]. To optimize research cost-efficiency and establish transgenerational genetic stability, we conducted multiparametric analyses on F2 and F3 generations, including glucose homeostasis assessment, grip and locomotor capacity evaluation, carcass composition analysis, metabolic enzyme kinetics in skeletal muscle, and expression levels of muscle fiber subtypes. Growth phenotype characterization and intraperitoneal glucose tolerance test (IPGTT) Following the final TMX injection, we monitored the mice’s body weight gain, food intake, and feed efficiency weekly for five consecutive weeks. Subsequently, mice underwent an IPGTT. After 24 h of fasting, we administered a glucose solution (2 g/kg body weight) via intraperitoneal injection. Blood glucose concentrations were measured from the tail vein at 0, 15, 30, 60, 90, and 120 min using a glucometer [ 71 , 72 ]. The glucose concentration-time curves were constructed, and the area under the curve (AUC) values were calculated using the trapezoidal method. Quantitative evaluation of four-limb grip strength and aerobic running capacity One week after the IPGTT, the body weight of mice was recorded before conducting the four-limb grip strength and treadmill exhaustion tests. Four-limb grip strength was assessed using a grip strength meter (YLS-13 A, Yiyan Scientific Instruments, Jinan, China). During testing, mice were allowed to grasp a horizontal metal rod with all four paws, after which their tails were gently pulled backward until grip release occurred. Ten consecutive trials were performed with two-minute intervals, with the highest and lowest values excluded. The mean value from eight valid measurements was used for statistical analysis. Subsequently, exercise endurance was evaluated using a motorized rodent treadmill (Model 47303, Ugo Basile, Comerio, Italy) according to established methods [ 73 ]. Following a three-day acclimatization (10 m/min, 10° incline, 5 min/day), a graded exercise protocol was initiated on day four. The treadmill maintained a constant 10° incline with an initial speed of 10 m/min, increasing by 2 m/min every two minutes until exhaustion. Exercise performance parameters (total running duration and distance covered) were digitally recorded. The exhaustion criterion was defined as failure to resume running within five seconds of contact with the electric shock grid (0.3 mA, 50 Hz). Euthanasia and sample collection Following completion of the IPGTT, limb grip strength assessment, and endurance running evaluation, mice were allowed a seven-day recovery period before a twelve-hour fasting protocol. Subsequently, the mice were euthanized, and samples were collected. Live weight and body size traits before euthanasia were measured. Blood serum was collected for the biochemical analysis of ALT, AST, TP, BUN, CREA, TG, Ca, P, and IGF-1 concentrations. Next, the following tissues were dissected and weighed using calibrated analytical balances: 1) Skeletal muscles: soleus muscle (SOL), GAS, extensor digitorum longus (EDL), tibialis anterior (TA), quadriceps, biceps femoris, triceps brachii, brachial biceps, lingualis, masseter, and diaphragm. 2) Non-skeletal muscle tissues: heart, liver, spleen, lungs, kidneys, brain, cerebellum, stomach, large intestine, small intestine, subcutaneous adipose tissue (SAT), brown adipose tissue (BAT), visceral adipose tissue (VAT), testis, and ovary. Finally, the muscle, fat, bones, and viscera were separated, and the percentages of muscle, fat, and bone, as well as the organ indices, were calculated. Analysis of skeletal muscle enzyme activity and hematoxylin-eosin (HE) staining We assessed the activities of LDH (a glycolytic marker enzyme) and SDH (a marker enzyme for mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation) in GAS muscle using commercial assay kits (SDH: BC0955; LDH: BC0685; Beijing Solarbio Science &amp; Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) according to the manufacturers’ protocols. Histological evaluation was performed using hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining of GAS muscle sections following an established methodology [ 24 ]. Isolation and identification of mouse skeletal muscle satellite cells To investigate the molecular mechanisms underlying Mdfi-mediated regulation of muscle fiber type conversion, primary mouse skeletal muscle satellite cells (MuSCs) were isolated from GAS of one-week-old skeletal muscle-specific Mdfi-overexpressing mice using established protocols [ 74 – 76 ]. The MuSCs were maintained in a complete medium containing 79.5% DMEM/F12 (high glucose; Hyclone, PA, USA), 20% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (Gibco, NY, USA), and 0.5% penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco, MD, USA) under standard culture conditions (37 °C, 5% CO 2 ). At 90% confluence, the medium was replaced with a differentiation medium (2% heat-inactivated horse serum; Hyclone, UT, USA), with medium changes performed every two days. Cell passaging was conducted using 0.25% (w/v) trypsin-EDTA (Gibco, CA, USA). To induce Mdfi overexpression, 0.2 µM tamoxifen (TMX; Sigma-Aldrich, MO, USA) was administered to the differentiation medium on day five post-differentiation. Cells were harvested on day seven for subsequent molecular analyses. Treatment of endoplasmic reticulum (ER) calcium channel inhibitors and Fluo-4 AM calcium flux assay The methods for pharmacological blockade of endoplasmic reticulum calcium channels (RyR/IP3R) and Fluo-4 AM-based calcium flux measurements were performed as previously described [ 25 ]. Specifically, the skeletal muscle satellite cells were treated with ryanodine (1µM, HY-103306, MedChemExpress, USA), U-73,122 (1µM, HY-13419, MedChemExpress, USA), and a combination of ryanodine and U-73,122 on the sixth day (D6) after cell differentiation in a culture medium without calcium ion sources. Then, the intracellular calcium level and the expression of related genes were measured twenty-four hours later. Three visual fields were selected from each well, and the fluorescence intensity was analyzed using NIS-Elements BR 4.60.00 (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan). Immunofluorescence Methods used for the immunofluorescence of cells have been described previously [ 25 ]. Three biological replicates (three wells) were performed for each experimental treatment, and three visual fields were captured from each well. The Desmin-positive and MyHCs-positive cells were calculated with Image Pro Plus (Media Cybernetics, MD, USA). The antibodies used in this study are listed in Table S1 . RNA extraction and qRT-PCR RNA extraction and PCR analysis methods have been described previously [ 25 , 77 ]. The 2 −ΔΔCt is defined as the ratio of the relative mRNA level of the target gene between the experimental group and the control group. Primers were designed using Primer Premier 5 according to the mouse gene sequence obtained from NCBI. Primers used for PCR are shown in Table S2 . Protein extraction and western blot The methods used for protein extraction from tissues and cells, as well as the western blot assay, have been described previously [ 24 , 25 ]. The antibodies used in this study are listed in Table S1 . The band intensities were quantified using Image J (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, United States). Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism and expressed as a change in fold relative to the control. Statistical analysis Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (IBM SPSS Statistic 18, Chicago, IL, USA). All data were expressed as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM), and at least three replicates were used per group. The significant differences between the two groups were determined by the Student’s t-test. One-way ANOVA and Fisher’s test were used for the remaining data. We considered P -value &lt; 0.05 statistically significant: * is P -value &lt; 0.05, ** is P -value &lt; 0.01, and *** is P -value &lt; 0.001.
