Introduction Fish show remarkable diversity in the form and function of their fins and bodies, supporting a wide range of locomotory modes. In recent years, considerable effort has gone toward understanding the contributions of body and fin material and structural properties to swimming capabilities. These studies have shown that morphology, kinematics, and swimming performance are tightly integrated, as recently reviewed by Jimenez et al. (2023) . Much of this work has relied on flexible foil models with largely homogenous structure, often based on the body and caudal fin [reviewed in ( Jimenez et al. 2023 )]. Unlike these models, fish fins are not homogenous. In both bony and cartilaginous fishes, fins are made of relatively stiff fin rays embedded in more flexible tissue ( Kemp 1977 ; Motta 1977 ; Alben et al. 2007 ; Lauder and Madden 2007 ; Fish and Lauder 2017 ). There is evidence that artificial fins with supporting fin rays behave differently than those without an internal structure ( Nguyen et al. 2017 ). Here, we take a first principles approach to study how internal fin structure influences pectoral fin kinematics. Through this investigation, we seek to establish a foundation for future research exploring other factors that might impact the kinematics of propulsive surfaces with embedded internal structures. The significance of internal structure for fin function is best understood in bony fishes, which actively deform the surface shape and tune the bending stiffness of their fins. For most bony fishes, this is accomplished not by altering the fin’s intrinsic material properties, but through muscular bending of bilaminar fin rays called lepidotrichia at the base of the fin ( Alben et al. 2007 ; Lauder and Madden 2007 ). When this bending occurs, the collagenous membrane between fin rays is stretched until it resists further bending and effectively stiffens the fin ( Nguyen et al. 2017 ). Materials with supporting fin rays behave differently than a single material, as evidenced by fish increasing their swimming performance by actively tuning fin stiffness through fin ray curvature ( Tangorra et al. 2011 ; Esposito et al. 2012 ). Although cartilaginous fishes share this fundamental structure of pectoral fins (rays supporting tissue), the anatomy and control of pectoral fins differs greatly between bony and cartilaginous fishes ( Motta 1977 ; Alben et al. 2007 ; Fish and Lauder 2017 ; Hoffmann et al. 2020 ). Unlike their bony counterparts, cartilaginous fishes are not known to actively tune the stiffness of their fins. The gradient of control over fin surface shape between the three major lineages of cartilaginous fishes—skates and rays (collectively, batoids; Batomorphi), sharks (Selachii), and chimaeras (Holocephali)—results from anatomical differences. The pectoral fins of batoids are supported by a series of cartilaginous radials, of which each fin ray is comprised ( Schaefer and Summers 2005 ; Hall et al. 2018 ). These skeletal elements are attached via thin muscles originating from the base of the pectoral fin ( Schaefer and Summers 2005 ). When these muscles are actuated, the pectoral fins deform into sizeable undulatory waves ( Rosenberger and Westneat 1999 ; Rosenberger 2001 ; Blevins and Lauder 2012 ; Di Santo et al. 2017 ). Incidentally, the convention in fish biomechanics that oscillation (“flapping”) is the presence of less than half a wave on the body or fin and that undulation is the presence of more than one wave is problematic, as all wave motion, regardless of the number of waves, is inherently undulatory ( Sfakiotakis et al. 1999 ; Rosenberger 2001 ). Like batoids, sharks and chimaeras have radials, but they do not possess intricate pectoral fin musculature or radials extending to the tip of the fin and are only capable of actuating their pectoral fins at the base ( Tomita et al. 2014 ; Hoffmann et al. 2019 ; Hoffmann and Porter 2019 ). Distal to the radials of both sharks and chimaeras (lacking in batoids) are thin filaments of elastic proteins called ceratotrichia, which act as fin rays and cannot be actively bent because they lack direct muscular attachments ( Goodrich 1904 ; Kemp 1977 ; Motta 1977 ; Durán et al. 2011 ; Fish and Lauder 2017 ). Chimaeras propel themselves using a mix of pectoral fin oscillation and undulation, making them an intriguing group of pectoral swimmers. Their unique mode of locomotion, aptly named flapping flight due to the oscillation of enlarged pectoral fins, generates an undulatory wave that travels from the leading edge to the trailing edge ( Combes and Daniel 2001 ). Because chimaeras do not have the anatomy necessary to actively control the curvature of their fin rays and hence the stiffness and surface shape of their fins, their pectoral fins likely deform passively during swimming. However, passive flexibility still shapes swimming biomechanics. It has been shown repeatedly, in a variety of physical and computational models of fish bodies and fins, that increasing passive flexibility improves several metrics of swimming performance up to a point, after which further increase in flexibility prevents fish-like models from producing coherent kinematics and generating appreciable propulsive forces [reviewed in ( Jimenez et al. 2023 ); see also fin-like examples in ( Tangorra et al. 2010 ; Esposito et al. 2012 ; Nguyen et al. 2017 )]. For example, Esposito et al. identified an optimal passive flexibility of fin rays in a physical model of a teleostean fin to maximize swimming performance, measured as mean thrust and lift forces ( Esposito et al. 2012 ). Given the tight links between internal structure, kinematics, and performance, the underlying structure of chimaera pectoral fins likely has strong implications for their life history and ecology. Particularly, a continuum of manoeuvrability and cruising efficiency exists for paired fin swimmers between undulatory and oscillatory motion ( Rosenberger 2001 ). Undulatory movements aid in manoeuvring around obstacles and hunting prey in cluttered, benthic environments, whereas oscillatory movements contribute to efficient migration for those species travelling inshore for spawning ( Walker and Westneat 2000 ; Rosenberger 2001 ; Wainwright et al. 2002 ; Didier et al. 2012 ). If fin ray structure influences the pectoral fin’s bending kinematics, then where a chimaera falls on the undulatory-oscillatory continuum is likely partly determined by internal fin structure. However, the connections between fin ray structure and overall fin kinematics are complex and requires further exploration ( Jimenez et al. 2023 ); it is unclear what specific aspects of fin ray structure are important for determining swimming capabilities. Here, we focus on fin ray diameter and packing density. Therefore, the bending stiffness of a ray is also proportional to the diameter of the ray. Additionally, stiffness increases with the number of supporting rays within a fin. While there are clear connections between stiffness and swimming performance ( Tangorra et al. 2010 ; Esposito et al. 2012 ; Jimenez et al. 2023 ), the exact nature of these relationships is difficult to predict and are often non-linear ( Shelton et al. 2014 ; Lucas et al. 2015 ; Jimenez et al. 2023 ). We seek to clarify how fin ray diameter and packing density alter swimming kinematics using flexible artificial fins that mimic the pectoral fins of Hydrolagus colliei —a northeastern Pacific chimaera whose range extends from Alaska to Costa Rica ( Foster and Higham 2010 ; Didier et al. 2012 ; Hall 2023 ). We chose this species because it is the most readily accessible in museum collections and its swimming is the most well-studied among extant chimaeras ( Combes and Daniel 2001 ; Foster and Higham 2010 ; Higham et al. 2018 ; Hall 2023 ). We predicted that fins with thinner and fewer rays would experience more bending, exhibited by larger fin tip amplitudes, curvature, and induced undulatory waves. The three primary findings of this study include 1) fin ray diameter and packing density both influenced pectoral fin kinematics; 2) diameter had a much greater effect on fin tip amplitude than packing density over the tested values of each; and 3) the relationship between undulatory wave speed and fin stiffness is more nuanced than suggested in previous literature. If genetic underpinnings exist for fin ray diameter and packing density in chimaeras, as they likely do in other fishes ( Tave 1984 ; Freitas et al. 2007 ; Iida et al. 2014 ; Navon et al. 2017 ; Barry and Crow 2017 ; Corcoran et al. 2026 ), then our findings suggest that chimaera fin ray structure may have been shaped by the swimming performance demands of chimaera’s environments and behaviours. While we focus on chimaeras, our first principles approach to understanding the impacts of these parameters on kinematics allows for broader translation of our findings to a widespread form of fin locomotion and points to relevant design principles for fin-actuated underwater vehicles. Artificial fin design To study the effects of fin ray anatomy on chimaera pectoral fin kinematics, we constructed nine artificial fins of varying fin ray diameters and packing densities. The fin shape was based on the average shape of H. colliei fins. We generated this average by applying standard geometric morphometrics techniques on museum specimens, as detailed in the following paragraphs. Pectoral fin photographs were taken of five H. colliei specimens from the Field Museum of Natural History’s (FMNH) Division of Fishes ( Table 1 ) with pectoral fins in good condition. In obtaining a representative fin outline, it was assumed that pectoral fins maintain constant shape across sizes for fully developed individuals—those with a body length greater than 40 cm ( Didier et al. 2012 ). We note that some chondrichthyans have sexual dimorphism in pectoral fin shape. Using the representative fin outline, a fin mold was designed in Fusion 360 (Autodesk, Inc., San Rafael, CA, USA) and printed on a Form 3 + resin printer (Formlabs, Somerville, MA, USA). Monofilament fishing line of varying diameters (0.3, 0.5, and 0.7 mm) and packing densities (14, 30, and 44 strands of fishing line) were threaded through holes in the mold to mimic fin rays (Figure 1B ). A fin-robot coupler based on the fin muscle outline, designed in Fusion 360, and printed on the Form 3 + resin printer, was also embedded in the mold. We then filled the mold with Ecoflex 00–30 (Young’s modulus = 0.125 MPa), a flexible silicone rubber with material properties similar to those of soft biological materials ( Wang et al. 2021 ; Jimenez et al. 2023 ). This process produced an artificial fin with a single layer of fin rays that were approximately centered within the fin’s thickness. We made every effort to produce fins of uniform, 5 mm thickness, though slight tapering (&lt;1 mm) occurred between the fin’s center and the edge on the dorsal surface due to natural cohesive and adhesive properties of fluids (i.e., uncured Ecoflex). Because of the different filament embedding schemes, artificial fins follow the naming convention diameter-packing density. For instance, 30 strands of fishing line 0.7 mm in diameter are embedded within the 0.7–30 fin (Figure 1B ). The artificial fins of this study are a compromise between ease of construction and biological realism. Chimaera pectoral fins have thinner and far more fin rays than the artificial fins (Figure 1C ). The chimaera’s rays are also arranged in a bilaminar configuration, and the fin tapers from the muscle block to the edge, though note that the artificial fins’ thickness was of the same order of magnitude as the thickest regions of a chimaera fin (Figure 1A, C ). It is impractical to embed so many rays of such small diameter in artificial fins of such thinness without resorting to advanced manufacturing methods. Further, this design choice enabled control over fin ray diameter and packing density. Though our models are therefore somewhat distant from those of real chimaera, we chose to focus on simplicity and to isolate the effects of fin ray diameter and packing density to the extent that was feasible. This design philosophy has previously been successful for simple physical models exploring the underlying mechanical principles of complex fish structures ( Lauder et al. 2011 ; Lucas et al. 2015 ; McKee et al. 2016 ) and enables exploration of the central question of this study: to what extent do fin ray diameter and packing density of tensile fin elements influence kinematics. To allow us to more completely discuss the limitations of our model design, we also prepared a scanning electron micrograph (SEM) of a pectoral fin cross-section (Figure 1C ). The individual was a juvenile wild-caught via trawling in San Juan Channel, Friday Harbor, Washington, in 2022. Immediately post-mortem, the fin was dissected from the individual, the entire, intact fin was pinned in place, sun-dried, and then cut in half with a scalpel in preparation for microscopy. We coated the sample in gold palladium and imaged it on a Jeol NeoScope JCM-5000 (Jeol Ltd., Tokyo, Japan). To track artificial fins during kinematic trials, 54 black dots were painted on the fins approximately 1 cm apart. All fins had a frontal area of 5000 mm² and a leading-edge angle of 26.1⁰. This angle is the average angle between the left-right axis of the fish and the leading edge of the pectoral fin for the five H. colliei specimens examined in this study (denoted as α in Figure 1 ). Angles were measured manually from the photos collected at the FMNH in ImageJ (Rasband, W.S., U. S. National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA). Robot design and verification We constructed a fin-flapping robot ( Figure 2 ) to “swim” the artificial fins using two stepper motors. The first motor (NEMA 17) flapped the fins, and the second motor (a 5V stepper) controlled the pitch of the fins. Both motors repeatedly rotated the fins clockwise then counterclockwise, in phase, producing motion like that of real chimaeras. The robot was housed in a 75.2 cm by 31.7 cm by 43.2 cm tank filled with freshwater. Fig. 2 Fin-flapping robot. The NEMA 17 motor flaps the fin around the y-axis, equivalent to the dorsoventral oscillation of a live chimaera’s pectoral fin. Thus, FTA is measured in the xz-plane. The 5V stepper motor changes the pitch of the fin around the z-axis. Photograph of the fin model and flapping robot, with labels indicating the functions of the motors. To ensure that the system accurately represents chimaera flapping flight, we programmed the robot with kinematics comparable to those displayed by a live H. colliei individual swimming in a flume with no flow ( Figure 3 ; ( Hall 2023 )). Fin beat period (s), fin tip amplitude (FTA, cm), and fin pitch (°) were calculated by tracking and averaging nine pectoral fin strokes of the live individual using ImageJ. To standardize the position and orientation of the individual, the base of the dorsal spine was set to the origin and body pitch—the angle between the vector from snout to cloaca and the x-axis (horizontal axis depicted in Figure 3 )—was set to 0⁰. Kinematic parameters were measured with respect to the body. A stroke begins when the fin tip reaches its maximum amplitude (fin tip maximum) at the top of the upstroke, continues while the tip traverses to its minimum amplitude (fin tip minimum) on the downstroke, and ends when the tip regains its maximum amplitude on the upstroke ( Figure 4 ). Fin beat period is the time it takes to complete a stroke. FTA is the distance between fin tip minimum and maximum. Fin pitch is the angle between the fin base and the x-axis. Based on these measurements from the live individual, the robot was programmed to beat the fins with a period of 1.16 s, a NEMA 17 rotation angle of 12.3⁰ (which is equivalent to a linear amplitude between 3.6 and 4.8 cm at the artificial fin’s base), and a pitch of 15.1⁰. The fin beat period and pitch settings were identical to those displayed by the live individual. The NEMA 17 motor’s rotation angle produced a fin-base amplitude, which we treated as our input variable and was consistent across all fins. FTA was then measured as a response variable. Note that the geometry of our model differs from a real fin in that we had to mount the artificial fin at the end of a rod to protect the motors from water, whereas a real fin attaches directly to the chimaera’s trunk. In a real chimaera, the ratio of fin chord length at the fin base to FTA is ∼1–1.5. Once the fin-base amplitude was accounted for (see below), the ratio in our models was ∼0.85–1.44, indicating comparability among motion parameters. Kinematic data collection We filmed each of the nine artificial fins at 60 fps with two GoPro Hero10s (GoPro, Inc., San Mateo, CA, USA) and an Apple iPhone XR (Apple, Inc., Cupertino, CA, USA). One Hero10 filmed the fins from the top left corner of the tank, the other Hero10 filmed from the bottom right corner, and the iPhone XR filmed from the centre; camera angles were similar to those recommended by and shown in Figure 3B of Theriault et al. ( Theriault et al. 2014 ). Filming order of the fins was randomized. To reconstruct the 3D movement of the fins and calibrate the cameras, we waved a calibration wand through all three camera’s shared field of view. Both ends of the wand were tracked through 86 shared frames using the point-tracking software DLTdv8 ( Hedrick 2008 ). The calibration software easyWand5 computed relative camera positions and orientations using the wand points and six stationary background points ( Theriault et al. 2014 ). Our measures of calibration inaccuracy were 0.003807 for the standard deviation of the estimated wand length divided by its mean and 0.50, 0.51, and 0.47 pixels for the root mean squared distances between the original and reprojected points, indicating high accuracy in the calibration of our cameras ( Theriault et al. 2014 ). Filming began after the robot ran for a minimum of one minute to ensure that the system reached steady-state—when the same flow is established around the fin for every stroke. A flashlight was turned on and off in the cameras’ shared field of view for synchronization purposes. Ten strokes were then recorded for each fin. Videos were imported into DLTdv8, synchronized with the flashlight strobe, and the 54 black dots on each of the nine artificial fins were tracked through all ten strokes. Kinematic analysis Because chimaeras cannot translate the base of their pectoral fins while swimming, we mathematically removed translation at the base of each artificial fin using MATLAB (MathWorks, Inc., Natick, MA, USA). Point 1 on each artificial fin, which corresponds to the base of the fin in live chimaeras, was set at the origin by subtracting the amplitude of point 1 from the amplitudes of all 54 points. Fin orientation was standardized by aligning the vector from fin tip minimum to fin tip maximum with the z-axis and by aligning the leading edge of the fin at midstroke with the x-axis using Rodrigues’ rotation formula ( Figure 4 ). FTA was measured over ten strokes and analyzed using ANOVA. A post-hoc orthogonal polynomial contrast was performed to disentangle linear and non-linear effects of fin ray diameter and fin ray packing density on, and their relative contributions to, FTA ( Steel and Torrie 1980 ). To calculate leading-edge curvature, we first fit a second-degree polynomial to the leading edge of each artificial fin using MATLAB’s “polyfit” function. A second-degree polynomial was selected as it is the lowest order polynomial to accurately outline the leading edge. Overfitting becomes more likely as polynomial order increases ( Lever et al. 2016 ). The presence of a wave is indicated by an inflection point—the point where curvature changes sign ( Videler 1993 ; Tytell et al. 2010 ; Lucas et al. 2020 ). Undulatory wave curvature is calculated in the same way as leading-edge curvature ( Equation 2 ), but in this case with a fourth-order polynomial instead of a second-order polynomial. We calculated wave interval—when a wave begins and ends—by recording when an inflection point appears and subsequently disappears ( Videler 1993 ; Tytell et al. 2010 ; Lucas et al. 2020 ). Intervals have been normalized to the wave period and are reported as percents. Average wave speed (m/s) was calculated by tracking and averaging the position of an inflection point through time ( Videler 1993 ; Tytell et al. 2010 ; Lucas et al. 2020 ). Results Artificial fins shared various kinematic patterns as they were flapped by the robot. Amplitude increased from base to tip, undulatory waves appeared on the trailing edge of several fins and travelled distally, and the fin tip lagged slightly behind the fin base until stroke reversal when the tip would rapidly accelerate past the base. Many of these general characteristics were visible in the kinematic envelopes representing the motion of the models’ leading edge ( Figure 6 ), and more detailed discussion can be found throughout the subsections below. Trailing edge undulatory wave There was no discernable pattern, based on diameter and packing density, for the presence of an undulatory wave travelling distally on the fin’s trailing edge ( Figure 9 ). The fins that did exhibit a wave (0.3–14, 0.3–44, 0.5–14, 0.5–30) did so near the end of the upstroke. The wave on the 0.3–44 and 0.5–14 fins continued through stroke reversal and ended on the downstroke. Additionally, the most flexible fin (0.3–14) was the only fin to produce another undulatory wave on its trailing edge during the downstroke. Of the fins that generated a wave, more flexible fins with slimmer and less densely packed rays—such as the 0.3–14 and 0.3–44 fins—tended to have greater average wave speeds. Discussion We constructed artificial fins of nine different fin ray diameter and packing density combinations and actuated these fins with a custom-built fin-flapping robot designed to mimic chimaera flapping flight. Our simplified models mimic the basic structure of chimaera fins: tensile elements (ceratotrichia, monofilament) embedded in a flexible material (the rest of the fin, Ecoflex). This design allowed us to change ray diameter and packing density separately and determine how tensile elements affect fin kinematics without confounding factors. An increase in both fin ray diameter and packing density led to changes in fin kinematics. We hypothesized that fins with thinner and fewer rays would display larger amplitudes at their tips, greater curvature along their leading edges, and undulatory waves because of their flexibility. FTA and leading-edge curvature followed this convention, with flexible fins displaying a higher FTA and leading-edge curvature. Undulatory waves did not: the presence and size of waves did not increase with a reduction in diameter or density. Research on flapping flight kinematics in chimaeras has been focused on one species, H. colliei , and is limited to a few studies ( Combes and Daniel 2001 ; Foster and Higham 2010 ; Higham et al. 2018 ; Hall 2023 ). Measures of material properties of ceratotrichia from any chondrichthyan are only beginning to be investigated [e.g., ( Rajan et al. 2025 )]. This study builds on previous research, is the first to explore how fin ray diameter and packing density alter chimaera pectoral fin kinematics, and identifies parameters for further exploration in future work on flapping flight. Fin kinematics depend on stiffness An increase in fin stiffness—due to an increase in fin ray diameter and/or packing density—led to a decrease in FTA. Individual rays act as cantilever beams with a load applied at their free end (along the distal border of the fin) due to hydrodynamic and inertial forces during flapping flight ( Schaefer and Summers 2005 ). The deflection of a cantilever beam’s free end is inversely proportional to the second moment of area, and thus diameter ( Equation 1 ). As diameter increases, the deflection of a ray decreases, and FTA decreases. Tangorra et al. ( Tangorra et al. 2010 ) designed a biorobotic pectoral fin modelled after the pectoral fins of bluegill sunfish ( Lepomis macrochirus ) and investigated how fin rays of varying cross sections and stiffnesses affected swimming performance. Rays of uniform rectangular cross section bend considerably at the base but deflect very little along their remaining length, whereas rays that taper in a fashion more comparable to sunfish fin rays deflect along their entire length. Thick fin rays, either those with large diameters (this study) or uniform cross sections, have relatively small amplitudes at their tips. Thin fin rays, those with small diameters (this study) or tapered cross sections, have relatively large amplitudes ( Tangorra et al. 2010 ). Additionally, fin rays do not act independently of one another; the stiffness of each ray contributes to the overall stiffness of the fin ( Nguyen et al. 2017 ). A fin with a greater fin packing density (i.e., more rays for a given fin surface area), including the artificial fins of this study, has more support and a smaller FTA. Nguyen et al. ( Nguyen et al. 2017 ) developed a mathematical model of a rayed fin to determine how fishes modulate fin stiffness and discovered that fin ray bending and fin membrane stretching are coupled. When rays bend, the membrane between them is stretched and elongated. Further ray bending results in additional resistance to stretching by the membrane and a stiffening of the fin. The properties of this coupled system change as fin ray packing density increases: a greater number of rays leads to an increase in fine-scale control over the membrane’s surface and in the overall bending stiffness of the fin. However, Puri et al. ( Puri et al. 2018 ) found that the bending stiffness of zebrafish caudal fins was unaffected when the fin membrane was cut, suggesting that fin stiffness primarily relies on fin rays and not stretching of the fin membrane. Although this appears to be in direct disagreement with Nguyen et al. ( Nguyen et al. 2017 ), Puri et al. ( Puri et al. 2018 ) agree that the material properties of the interray membrane must be considered when examining fin locomotion. Considering these findings, we find it convincing that both the rays and membrane of the artificial fins in this study contributed to the overall stiffness of the fins. The membrane may also stretch and stiffen as the fin and internal rays passively deform through the water, contributing to the non-linearity seen between fin ray packing density and FTA. Future work on artificial and dissected chimaera pectoral fins could examine whether these fishes employ a bending-stretching mechanism during flapping flight with an experimental setup similar to the setup described in Puri et al. ( Puri et al. 2018 ). Leading-edge curvature is brought about by a bending of the fin as it travels through fluid; therefore, as fin ray diameter and packing density increase, fins are more resistant to bending, and curvature decreases. Additionally, curvature decreases non-linearly from fin base to fin tip ( Figures 6 , 8 ). Inherent structural properties, such as changes in stiffness, can lead to variation in curvature ( McHenry et al. 1995 ; Blevins and Lauder 2012 ). The base is supported by a relatively stiff fin-robot coupler (3D printed resin) which abruptly ends as the main, flexible body (silicone rubber) of the artificial fin begins. This sudden change in material could give rise to the high initial curvature seen between the stiff fin-robot coupler and the flexible fin body; maximum curvature looks to occur at the point of material transition between the stiff foil body and flexible trailing edge in Cleaver et al. ( Cleaver et al. 2014 ). The continuous silicone rubber body of the artificial fins then dampens curvature gradually from base to tip. Though kinematics is not necessarily a predictor of swimming performance ( Rosic et al. 2017 ; Jimenez et al. 2023 ), our data suggest some theoretical future avenues to explore. For example, boundary layer separation might occur if curvature is too high, leading to an increase in drag and a decrease in performance ( Vogel 2013 ). Conversely, an increase in curvature could strengthen spanwise flow from fin base to fin tip, stabilizing vortices and increasing efficiency ( Wong and Rival 2017 ). Fin ray diameter played a much larger role in dictating FTA than fin ray packing density. Although this finding might apply to other kinematic measurements, we note that our current findings regarding FTA should not be extrapolated to leading-edge curvature or undulatory wave speed. Dynamic oscillatory tests, following the recommendations of Jimenez et al. ( Jimenez et al. 2023 ), could be used to measure the stiffness of artificial fins and disentangle the effects of diameter and packing density on stiffness. If diameter has a larger effect on stiffness than packing density, as it did for FTA, then it would be reasonable to posit that diameter also affects other kinematic parameters to a greater extent than packing density. We also suggest that changes in diameter may matter more for kinematics specifically in fins with small fin ray diameters. We observed a much larger decrease in FTA between the 0.3 and 0.5 mm models (∼1.5 to 2.5 cm reduction) than the 0.5 and 0.7 mm models (∼0.25 to 0.5 cm reduction). We estimate that the largest H. colliei ceratotrichia are ∼0.1 mm in diameter based on measurements from our SEM (Figure 1C ). This is within the diameter range we estimate from other published photographs of chimaera pectoral fins ( Foster and Higham 2010 ; Kemper et al. 2010 ) and the range of 0.06 to 0.2 mm reported for elasmobranchs ( Kemp 1977 ). That said, our smallest fin ray models do appear to reflect or exceed the larger end of the biological range, and the range of diameters among our fishing lines (0.4 mm) is larger than the range in diameters among shark ceratotrichia (0.14 mm) ( Kemp 1977 ). Real chimaeras have a smaller fin ray diameter than we were able to utilize in our models and, presumably, would experience larger FTA and larger changes in FTA with decreasing diameter. So, while a wider range of models would be required to fully characterize this relationship, our findings suggest that small changes to ray diameter among chimaera species may be important for the waveform generated by their pectoral fins. This would impact species’ placement on the undulatory-oscillatory continuum separating those species specialized for manoeuvrability from those specialized for cruising efficiency ( Rosenberger 2001 ). Flexible fins defy simple kinematic paradigms Unlike FTA and leading-edge curvature, which decreased with an increase in fin ray diameter and packing density, the presence of an undulatory wave on a fin’s trailing edge could not be predicted based on diameter and packing density. The most flexible fin (0.3–14) produced two waves: one on the upstroke and one on the downstroke. Stiffer fins only produced a wave on the upstroke. This finding aligns with our hypothesis that flexible fins would display undulatory waves more readily than stiff fins, as they are less resistant to deforming and bending as they move through the water. The two stiffest artificial fins that induced undulatory waves on their trailing edges (0.5–14 and 0.5–30) exhibited identical wave speeds. This suggests that as fins become stiffer their kinematics become more similar. A comparable trend appears in FTA, which decreased but began to level off as fin ray diameter increased (Figure 7A ). A certain amount of flexibility might be necessary for fins to respond to hydrodynamic forces they encounter and create in the water; fin surface deformations will not occur if a fin is above a certain level of rigidity. Future work involving artificial fins that cover a greater and more finely incremented range of ray diameters and packing densities might indicate that kinematic differences between fins begin to disappear as stiffness increases. The faster average wave speed of our more flexible fin models contradicts expectations from literature. Kinematic wave speed in fish-like bodies and fins typically increases with the propulsive structure’s stiffness ( Blight 1977 ; McHenry et al. 1995 ; Long 1998 ; Combes and Daniel 2001 ). However, Combes and Daniel ( Combes and Daniel 2001 ) found that when flexible fins are flapped at higher frequencies, the expected relationships between stiffness, wave speed, and mechanical efficiency break down. They used a mathematical fluid flow model to determine the relative performance of flexible fins with different shapes and found that at high flapping frequencies, fins with a broad shape can outperform slender fins in terms of both thrust generation and efficiency. That said, these same shapes perform poorly when flapped at low frequencies or when made with stiffer material. Similarly, our findings suggest that there is non-linearity in the relationships between stiffness and wave speed, which may not be captured by simple kinematic paradigms which anticipate wave speed to uniformly increase with stiffness. Instead, such paradigms might fall apart when flexible fins are flapped at specific frequencies. Possibilities for fin ray evolution Genes have been shown to regulate fin structure, and indeed, vertebrates as a whole display deep homology of genetic pathways determining the body plan, including paired fins/limbs ( Freitas et al. 2007 , Nakamura et al. 2016 ; Barry and Crow 2017 ; Corcoran et al. 2026 ). Packing density of fin rays is a function of ray count and fin size/shape, both of which are genetically regulated. Ray count is heritable in the guppy, carp, and two African cichlids ( Tave 1984 ; Navon et al. 2017 ). Ray count is also well-documented as being consistent within and across species of batoids, where the total number of fin rays and distribution of fin rays is set early in development before hatching or birth ( Barry and Crow 2017 ; Hall et al. 2018 ). More broadly, a conserved suite of Hox genes determines the anteroposterior patterning of fin shape, fin ray count, and distribution of rays in teleosts and cartilaginous fishes ( Freitas et al. 2007 ; Barry and Crow 2017 ; Corcoran et al. 2026 ). Collectively, this suggests the potential for a genetic system of control over packing density. Likewise, consistent variation in fin ray diameter has been demonstrated among species of batoids, where radial thickness (mineralization) has been associated with Omd ( Schaefer and Summers 2005 ; Hall et al. 2018 ; Swenson et al. 2018 ). Additionally, the rays of mutant medaka, or Japanese rice fish, do not normally elongate or thicken ( Iida et al. 2014 ), suggesting a level of genetic influence over ray diameter in teleost fishes. If similar genetic foundations for diameter and packing density occur in chimaeras, selection pressures might act on these traits to evolve well-adapted pectoral fins with stiffnesses specialized to the environmental niches chimaeras occupy and behaviours they display ( Schaefer and Summers 2005 ; Nguyen et al. 2017 ; Hall et al. 2018 ). Notably, the stiffness of skeletons in batoids affects swimming kinematics, swimming performance, and location in the water column ( Rosenberger 2001 ; Schaefer and Summers 2005 ; Hall et al. 2018 ). Batoid oscillators typically have stiff, highly calcified skeletons; are more efficient during cruising; and occupy the pelagic zone. Conversely, batoid undulators have relatively flexible skeletons, specialize in manoeuvrability, and inhabit more benthic environments. Variation in the pectoral fin stiffness of chimaeras could also lead to differences in kinematics, performance, and habitat preferences like those stated above for skates and rays. Although chimaeras likely cannot actively control the stiffness of their pectoral fins, the range of stiffnesses they do express may have been shaped by their benthic lifestyle and unique ecology. For instance, if an increase in fin ray diameter improves swimming performance, then thick rays may be selected for. However, based on the findings from our artificial fins, we would expect ray diameter to increase only until it no longer has a strong influence on kinematics or swimming performance. In our artificial models, the decrease in FTA with increased ray diameter appears to be leveling off. An increase in diameter beyond the range we studied might have little or no effect on kinematics and swimming performance and thus would not be selected for. Additionally, if developing and maintaining fin rays is more energetically expensive than developing and maintaining the surrounding pectoral fin membrane, then stabilizing selection might act to limit the thickness of fin rays. Given that our artificial fins generally exceed real fin ray diameter, we suggest that such diminishing returns and energetic costs for increasing fin ray diameter may be serving as an upper constraint on ceratotrich size. In summary, both fin ray diameter and packing density influence fin kinematics ( Schaefer and Summers 2005 ; Tangorra et al. 2010 ; Nguyen et al. 2017 ). By examining the kinematics of artificial chimaera pectoral fins of varying stiffness, we found that both an increase in diameter and packing density led to a decrease in FTA and leading-edge curvature, diameter played a larger role in dictating FTA than packing density, and trailing edge undulatory waves unexpectedly travelled faster on flexible fins. Genes likely regulate fin ray diameter and packing density in other fishes, and kinematic waveform has been associated with underlying fin ray morphology in other fishes ( Tave 1984 ; Schaefer and Summers 2005 ; Freitas et al. 2007 ; Tangorra et al. 2011 ; Esposito et al. 2012 ; Iida et al. 2014 ; Navon et al. 2017 ; Barry and Crow 2017 ; Hall et al. 2018 ; Corcoran et al. 2026 ). So, chimaera fin rays likely have similarly been shaped by selection pressures related to swimming performance. This first principles study lays the groundwork for future research on chimaera pectoral fin kinematics in relation to stiffness; reveals biologically relevant parameters of interest for further investigation; and could lead to the design of novel underwater vehicle propulsors with optimally tuned embedded fin rays.
