1. INTRODUCTION Healthy human pregnancy outcomes require precise regulation of physiological processes, including adaptations to ensure appropriate delivery of oxygen (O 2 ) and nutrients to the developing fetus ( 1 ). In the mother, extensive respiratory and cardiovascular adjustments occur throughout pregnancy to accommodate the demands of the growing fetus. However, when remodeling of the maternal uterine vasculature is impaired, limiting nutrient and O 2 transport from the mother to the fetus, the risk of fetal growth restriction (FGR) and harm to fetal development increases ( 2 ). In addition, healthy placental function is necessary for a successful pregnancy ( 3 ), and abnormal placental development and function can result in several pregnancy complications, including FGR and preeclampsia ( 4 , 5 ). The reduction of O 2 availability is generally defined as hypoxia and can be measured at molecular, cellular, tissue, organism or environmental levels. In the context of pregnancy, the exposure of reproductive tissues, such as the uterus or the placenta, to hypoxia is considered as a reduced availability of O 2 to reach those tissues. Residents at high altitudes are exposed to chronic hypobaric hypoxia due to reduced barometric pressure, which lowers the partial pressure of O 2 (PO 2 ) in inspired air ( 6 ). High-altitude physiological responses can be observed starting at 1,500 m – home to over 500 million people worldwide (6.58% of the total population) ( 7 ). By this altitude, the partial pressure of inspired O 2 at 20 °C has fallen to roughly 83% of that at sea level. However, most high-altitude studies consider marked and long-lasting responses at elevations of 2,500 m or higher, where nearly 80 million people reside and the partial pressure of inspired O 2 is reduced to around 72% compared to sea level ( 7 ). The concomitant reduction in O 2 availability has been shown to affect pregnancy outcomes independently of other stressors ( 8 ), potentially due to lower PO 2 available in the uterine and placental tissues. Against this backdrop, high-altitude populations provide a natural experiment to study both maladaptive and adaptive maternal, placental, and fetal responses to chronic hypobaric hypoxia, and emerging omics studies are beginning to identify candidate molecular pathways ( 9 , 10 ). This review explores how chronic hypoxia and/or high altitude impacts pregnancy and summarizes the underlying molecular, cellular, and physiological mechanisms. Additionally, it highlights adaptations observed in high-altitude human populations and animal models from maternal, placental, and fetal perspectives, and how chronic hypoxia affects long-term offspring health. Finally, we discuss emerging therapeutic targets for preventing or mitigating altitude-related pregnancy complications. 2. MODELS TO STUDY PREGNANCY UNDER CHRONIC HYPOXIA To study the effects of low O 2 levels on pregnancy, researchers have investigated human populations living at high altitudes and employed several experimental animal and cellular models of chronic hypoxic exposures during pregnancy, as displayed in Figure 1 . 2.1. HUMAN POPULATIONS AT HIGH ALTITUDE Humans live in nearly every region of the world ( 11 ), including those at high altitudes, defined as elevations of 1,500 m or greater above sea level. While numerous environmental stressors are present at high altitude, including colder temperatures, marked diurnal temperature variation, aridity, and increased ultraviolet exposure, the predominant stressor is decreased O 2 availability (hypoxia), due to the altitudinal decline in the atmospheric pO 2 . Currently, about 80 million people live at high altitudes above 2,500 m ( 7 ) in North and South America, central Asia, the Arabian Peninsula, and the East African highlands, with these populations projected to grow ( 12 ). Human populations have lived in these regions for varying lengths of time, ranging from 11,000–13,000 years in the Andes ( 13 , 14 ), 30,000–40,000 years on the Tibetan Plateau ( 15 ), and 31,000 to 47,000 years in the East African Highlands ( 16 ). Ascent to high altitude triggers a range of acclimatization responses, including a rise in ventilation (which helps normalize arterial pO 2 and hemoglobin O 2 saturation), increased heart rate, and redistribution of blood flow to favor key organs. Hemoglobin concentration also increases, initially due to plasma volume loss and later due to an increase in red cell mass ( 17 , 18 ). Additional changes occur with lifelong residence at high altitude, such as increased total lung volume and diffusing capacity ( 19 , 20 ). With multigenerational high-altitude residence, genetic adaptations promote physiological characteristics that enhance reproductive success and survival under the chronically hypoxic conditions ( 21 – 29 ). For example, Andean and Tibetan populations have improved maternal vascular and other adaptations during pregnancy that protect fetal growth and birthweight ( 30 – 33 ) as well as other characteristics that enhance maximal exercise capacity. Tibetan highlanders also demonstrate reduced hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstrictor responses, lower pulmonary artery pressures and hemoglobin levels compared to other high-altitude populations ( 17 , 18 , 34 ). While some populations show protective adaptations, pregnancy complications such as fetal growth restriction, pregnancy-induced hypertension, and preeclampsia are several-fold more common at high than low altitudes, with rates varying by condition and by the population studied ( 8 , 35 ). This variability makes short- and long-term residents of high altitude particularly valuable for identifying attributes that promote healthy pregnancy outcomes as well as those contributing to complications. Both Andean and Tibetan high-altitude residents are protected from altitude-associated reductions in fetal growth compared to European or Han highland residents with less than one generation of high-altitude residence (defined henceforth as “newcomers”) ( 8 , 36 – 40 ). Tibetans, compared to Han, are also protected from hypertensive disorders of pregnancy ( 30 , 41 ). Little is known about the physiological effects of high altitude during pregnancy in East African highlanders ( 42 , 43 ). Furthermore, some studies have explored pregnancy adaptations in other moderate-to-high-altitude regions (1,500–2,800 m); for example, in southwestern Saudi Arabia ( 44 ), Kyrgyzstan ( 45 , 46 ), eastern Turkey ( 47 ), and northwest Argentina ( 48 ). Although human studies at high altitudes pose logistical and socio-cultural challenges, numerous epidemiological, physiological, molecular, and genomic studies have been conducted. As reviewed within this article, these include retrospective human health records analyses ( 37 , 49 , 50 ); longitudinal collections of blood samples and hemodynamic measurements ( 51 – 53 ); ex vivo studies of uterine resistance artery vascular function ( 54 – 56 ); and genomic, transcriptomic, and epigenomic analyses ( 9 , 57 – 61 ). 2.2. RODENTS AND LAGOMORPHS Rodent models have contributed to our understanding of the effects of high altitude on pregnancy as they offer the advantages of a short gestational period (~18–23 days in mice and rats, and ~70 days in guinea pigs) ( 62 ); the opportunity for longitudinal studies throughout gestation; conduct of genetic manipulations using CRISPR technology, viral-mediated genetic editing, or nanoparticle-delivery of plasmids; and the opportunity to perform pharmacological and nutritional interventions. Such models create hypoxic conditions either by lowering the inspired percent O 2 in isobaric chambers via nitrogen dilution ( 63 – 68 ) ( Figure 1B ) to achieve mild (13–14% O 2 ), moderate (10–11% O 2 ) or severe hypoxia (less than 10% O 2 ). Pregnant rodents have also been placed in hypobaric chambers ( 69 – 72 ) ( Figure 1B ) to simulate high-altitude conditions by lowering the atmospheric pressure from ambient conditions (e.g., 760 mmHg at sea level) to 460 or 385 mmHg, corresponding to altitudes of 4,000 or 5,400 m, respectively ( 69 , 72 , 73 ). Rabbits are also used in studies of fetal development and physiological responses to pregnancy during hypoxia ( 74 ). Similar to rodents, rabbits have a comparatively short gestational period (approximately 29–34 days) ( 75 ). Because they are larger than mice or rats, rabbits enable longitudinal blood sampling for measurements of circulating factors. Rabbits have also been studied under hypoxic conditions ( 76 – 78 ), examining for example the effects of intermittent hypoxia on endothelial nitric oxide (NO) synthase (eNOS) expression in fetal carotid and femoral arteries ( 79 ). Although hypoxic exposure reduces fetal growth in rabbits as well as rodents, the effect of hypoxia on the uterine circulation differs between these experimental animal models and human beings, and therefore caution should be exercised in interpreting study results. The differences stem from variation in uterine shape with the human uterus being pyriform or bulb-shaped vs bicornuate or two-horned in rodents ( 80 ). Litter size also varies, with humans typically supporting one or two offspring per pregnancy, whereas rodents normally range from about 6 to 12 pups per birth and lagomorphs average 7.4 pups per litter ( 81 , 82 ). Placental structure also differs ( 83 ) with the human and guinea pig placentas being hemomonochorial, rodents being hemotrichorial and rabbits hemodichorial ( 84 ), and these differences have been extensively reviewed elsewhere ( 85 , 86 ). 2.3. SHEEP Sheep have been extensively used to study the effect of chronic hypoxic exposure during pregnancy. Like humans, sheep have a relatively long gestation, typically singleton deliveries, and comparable birth weights ( 87 ). While the ovine placenta is anatomically different, it shares uterine to fetal O 2 gradients, fetal rates of O 2 consumption, similar glucose transfer mechanisms and nutrient transporter expression ( 88 , 89 ). Moreover, both the maternal and fetal circulations can be chronically catheterized, permitting serial blood sampling and longitudinal measurement of in vivo cardiometabolic function ( 90 – 93 ). The effect of chronic gestational hypoxia has been studied in sheep by using prolonged isobaric hypoxic exposures ( 91 , 92 , 94 , 95 ), under conditions of chronic hypobaric hypoxia following transport to high-altitude research laboratories or for shorter periods of time via tracheal catheter and adding nitrogen to the inspired air ( 96 – 98 ) ( Figure 1C ). Other methods for exposing the fetus to chronic hypoxia have also been employed such as uterine carunclectomy ( 99 ), hyperthermia ( 100 ), placental embolization ( 101 ) and single umbilical artery ligation ( 102 ). These models reduce uteroplacental perfusion and thereby invoke fetal hypoxemia and hypoglycemia as have been extensively reviewed elsewhere ( 103 , 104 ). 2.4. HIGH-ALTITUDE ADAPTED MAMMALS Using animals adapted to high altitudes offers another opportunity to identify adaptations to high-altitude pregnancy. For example, the Altiplano llama ( Lama glama ), a camelid living at elevations of 3,500 m and above as well as another camelid, the alpaca ( Vicugna pacos ), have been widely studied in the context of pregnancy at both low and high altitudes ( 105 – 108 ) ( Figure 1D ). Native altitude-adapted highland species such as the North American deer mouse ( Peromyscus maniculatus) ( 109 , 110 ), the Tibetan pig ( Sus scrofa domesticus ) ( 111 ), and the yak ( Bos grunniens ) ( 112 , 113 ) have also been studied with respect to their maternal vascular, fetal growth, placental gene expression and placental structural features under chronic hypoxia. 2.5. AVIAN AND REPTILIAN EMBRYO MODELS When mammals experience chronic hypoxia during pregnancy at high altitude or in iso- or hypobaric chambers, the mother, placenta, and fetus are exposed to reductions in oxygenation. Therefore, it is difficult to disentangle whether the effects of chronic hypoxia on fetal development are secondary due to maternal and/or placental effects or to direct effects on the fetus. In this regard, avian and reptilian models have provided a useful tool for isolating the direct effects of chronic hypoxia on fetal development, independent of additional effects on maternal and/or placental physiology ( 114 – 116 ). 2.6. CELLULAR MODELS Cell culture models have proven useful for examining the effects of chronic hypoxia on a cellular, biochemical and molecular levels ( 117 ). Most often used have been human umbilical vein or arterial endothelial cells ( 118 – 121 ), cultured primary trophoblast implants ( 122 – 125 ), cell lines (e.g., BeWo, JEG-3, and HTR-8/Svneo cells) ( 126 , 127 ), and stem cell cultures ( 128 , 129 ) maintained in hypoxic incubators ( 130 ) ( Figure 1D ), although the limitations in determining the precise pericellular pO 2 must be considered ( 117 ). Primary cultures could also be isolated from high-altitude human tissues and compared to their low-altitude counterparts ( 131 ). Another tool that could be applied to the study of hypoxic pregnancy-related issues is the use of microfluidic devices (a.k.a. organ-on-a-chip) ( Figure 1D ). These devices combine engineering technology and biology to generate models of cellular interaction. Placental models and vascular models have been developed and expanded to include maternal-fetal tissue interactions ( 132 ). Future studies could utilize these organ-on-a-chip tools to expose cells to hypoxic conditions or could be built from cells from women exposed to chronic hypoxia. Almost a decade ago, two research groups independently established placental organoid models ( 133 , 134 ). These organoids naturally organize into the tissue structure of the placenta, thereby better recapitulating placental function, and can be cultured and proliferate continuously. Although organoids from these initial studies had the syncytiotrophoblast layer facing inwards, subsequent studies have produced placental organoids with the apical membrane of the syncytiotrophoblasts facing outwards ( 135 ), providing an additional tool for recapitulating placental structure. As mentioned above, stem cells have been used to study placental function in vitro and to generate organoids in 3D cultures ( 136 , 137 ). Overall, these organoids have tremendous potential for use in studies of high-altitude pregnancy, whether through chronic exposure to hypoxic conditions in vitro or obtained from placentas from high-altitude residents. 2.7. PHARMACOLOGICAL OR GENETIC INDUCTION OF HYPOXIA Hypoxia influences numerous cellular processes and pathways. Among these pathways are the hypoxia-inducible factors (HIF-1 and 2), which are transcription factors crucial to the cellular response to hypoxia ( 138 , 139 ). Under normoxic conditions, HIF complexes are targeted for proteosomal degradation through the hydroxylation of specific proline residues via HIF-prolyl hydroxylases (PHDs). In contrast, under hypoxic conditions, PHDs are inhibited, HIF is stabilized and migrates to the nucleus to promote the transcription of target genes ( 140 , 141 ). Thus, compounds that stabilize HIF proteins by inhibiting PHD can chemically induce hypoxic states under normoxic conditions ( 142 ). Among these are cobalt chloride (CoCl 2 ) ( 143 ), nickel chloride (NiCl 2 ) ( 144 ), deferoxamine ( 145 ), dimethyloxalylglycine ( 146 ), the less cytotoxic compound hydralazine ( 147 ), and hypoxia-mimetic agents, as has been extensively reviewed ( 142 , 148 ). Recently, another compound, hypoxystat, has been described to induce hypoxia in vivo via increasing hemoglobin O 2 affinity, left-shifting the hemoglobin-O 2 dissociation curve, and thus preventing the internal tissues from receiving an adequate amount of O 2 ( 149 ). Genetic manipulation has also been utilized to generate hypoxic-like conditions or hypoxia-dependent responses ( Figure 1D ). For instance, overexpression of HIFs or microRNAs that stabilize HIF (e.g., miR-424) can lead to hypoxia-like responses ( 150 , 151 ). Similarly, the overexpression of hypoxia-activated microRNAs (hypoxamiRs), such as miR-210, can also lead to hypoxia-like responses in placental trophoblast cells from uncomplicated pregnancies, mimicking those observed in cells from preeclamptic pregnancies ( 152 ). Finally, recent advances in bioengineering have enabled the design of biomaterials that incorporate hypoxia-mimetic agents to simulate hypoxic conditions ( 153 ) by enhancing their release kinetics and reducing the cytotoxicity of the mimetic agents. ( Figure 1D ). In addition, hypoxia-inducing hydrogels containing, for instance, immobilized glucose oxidase, an enzyme that catalyzes the oxidation of glucose to hydrogen peroxide and D-glucono-1,5-lactone, has been utilized for sustained hypoxic stimuli, particularly in tumor cells ( 154 ). However, its cytotoxicity due to the production of hydrogen peroxide requires the addition of catalase or glutathione ( 155 ). Other hydrogels used in cancer research can deplete O 2 via a cross-linking reaction with the polymer network ( 156 ). While these biomaterials have not yet been employed in pregnancy studies, such approaches may prove useful for studying cellular processes by which hypoxia acts during pregnancy. 3. MATERNAL ADAPTATIONS TO HIGH ALTITUDE Women undergo significant physiological changes during pregnancy to accommodate the developing fetus, making it one of the greatest physiological challenges humans face. Of the total calories required to sustain pregnancy, only 10% are consumed by the fetus and placenta, whereas the remaining 90% support the woman’s own metabolic demands ( 157 ). These high levels of energy expenditure reflect the prolonged duration of human pregnancy and the relatively large weight of human newborns. For example, compared to gorillas, whose adult females weigh twice as much, human pregnancies last nearly a month longer, and human newborns are 50% heavier ( 158 ). Further, the energy required to complete pregnancy and lactation, expressed as a multiple of a woman’s basal metabolic rate, approximates the sustained energy expenditure of elite endurance runners, cyclists, or arctic trekkers over comparable time periods ( 159 ). Early observations by Spanish missionaries arriving in the Andes region in the 16 th century described reduced fertility and increased neonatal mortality among European settlers compared with Indigenous highlanders in high-altitude settlements ( 160 ). Despite the prolonged history of studies on fetal physiology at high altitude ( 161 ), early work on maternal responses was limited to one seminal report documenting pregnancy-associated hyperventilation ( 162 ). More comprehensive investigations did not appear until the 1980’s when the effects of the pregnancy-induced rise in maternal ventilation were shown to increase maternal arterial O 2 saturation (SaO 2 ) and correlate positively with infant birth weight ( 163 ). In this section, we discuss the effects of high altitude and chronic hypoxia on maternal physiological processes to sustain successful pregnancy outcomes. 3.1. RESPIRATORY FUNCTION 3.1.1 RESPIRATORY FUNCTION DURING PREGNANCY AT LOW ALTITUDE Several biochemical, mechanical, and physiological adaptations during human pregnancy modify respiratory function ( 164 – 166 ). The increase in circulating progesterone and estrogen levels during pregnancy results in the relaxation of smooth muscle in the airways ( 167 , 168 ) and an increase in minute ventilation (V E ), due to increased tidal volume (V T ) without a change in respiratory frequency ( 169 – 171 ) ( Figure 2A and B ). The increased ventilation results from an augmented chemosensory sensitivity to lower O 2 tensions as well as elevated carbon dioxide (CO 2 ), which is due to progesterone acting peripherally to raise carotid body neural output in response to ambient O 2 and/or elevated CO 2 tensions ( 172 , 173 ). Estrogen also acts centrally to translate the rise in carotid body neural output into increased phrenic neural activity ( 174 ). Morphological factors also affect respiratory function during pregnancy. The expanding uterus results in an expanded abdominal volume and an approximately 4 cm upward shift of the diaphragm. There is also a 5–7 cm increase in the circumference of the lower chest ( 165 , 166 ), even before the uterine size increases. Elevation of the diaphragm reduces residual volume (RV), expiratory reserve volume (ERV), and functional residual capacity, but has little-to-no effect on forced vital capacity (FVC) ( 169 , 175 ) ( Figure 2A and B ). 3.1.2 RESPIRATORY FUNCTION DURING PREGNANCY AT HIGH ALTITUDE High-altitude residence profoundly impacts respiratory function during pregnancy. The total lung capacity is greater in pregnant women at high altitude than their low-altitude counterparts, mainly due to larger ERV, inspiratory capacity, and RV ( 176 ), without a change in V T , forced expiratory volume in 1 second, or FVC ( 171 , 177 – 179 ) ( Figure 2A and C ). High-altitude women also exhibit larger total lung volumes, despite being shorter in height than low-altitude women ( 176 ). This was associated with the wider ‘barrel-shaped’ chests observed in both Andean and Tibetan high-altitude populations ( 19 , 180 – 182 ). Taken together, these structural adaptations appear to enhance maternal ventilatory capacity and may provide an advantage for maintaining fetal O 2 supply in women living at high altitude. Pregnancy augments O 2 consumption and basal metabolic rate ( 179 , 183 ). The increase in V E leads to a rise in arterial pressure of O 2 (P a O 2 ), a decrease in arterial CO 2 tension ( P a C O 2 ) ( 167 ) ( Figure 2B ), and a rise in alveolar ventilation per unit of CO 2 production ( VCO 2 ), which approximates metabolic rate. P a C O 2 dependency on V E is illustrated by Equation 1 , where V D represents dead space ventilation, and K is a constant. Equation 1 P a C O 2 = K × VCO 2 ( V E − V D ) The augmented maternal V E increases the O 2 gradient between maternal blood and the placenta, which is critical for sustaining fetal growth at high altitude ( 31 ). The hypoxic ventilatory response (HVR), or the curve describing the relationship between increasing ventilation in response to a lowering of alveolar PO 2 (P A O 2 ) or P a O 2 , is twofold higher than the non-pregnant state ( 171 ), with this rise amplified in pregnant residents of high versus low altitude ( 178 ) ( Figure 2C ). This augmented HVR accounts for the 25% rise in resting ventilation in high-altitude pregnancies ( 176 , 178 , 179 ). Furthermore, the increase in ventilation at high altitude (16.7 L/min, compared to 13.3 L/min at sea level) ( 177 ) leads to augmented P a O 2 and S a O 2 ( 177 , 178 ). The reduction in P a CO 2 is maximal early in pregnancy and remains stable throughout the remainder of pregnancy ( Figure 3A ), at both low and high altitude. However, lower absolute P a CO 2 values are present at high altitude due to starting from lower values in the nonpregnant state. P a CO 2 is lower in non-pregnant and pregnant women at moderate (Denver, CO, 1,609 m) or high altitudes (Leadville, CO, 3,100 m; Cerro de Pasco, Peru, 4,300 m) ( 171 , 177 , 179 ) ( Figure 3A and 2B ). An early longitudinal study in a small group of pregnant and non-pregnant women residing in Tuctu, Peru (4,420 m) measured alveolar PCO 2 (P A CO 2 ) and P A O 2 , showing that the mean P A CO 2 decreases from 27.94 mmHg in the non-pregnant group to 22.93 mmHg in the pregnant group, whereas pregnancy increases P A O 2 from 50.71 to 59.01 mmHg ( 162 ). By amplifying the HVR, pregnancy shifts maternal blood gases toward higher P A O 2 and lower P A CO 2 , thereby helping preserve O 2 delivery to the fetus. When pregnant sheep are exposed to chronic hypoxia (10–13% inspired O 2 ) in environmental chambers in late gestation for up to a month, the fall in maternal P a O 2 in the pregnant ewe by ~50% of baseline is accompanied by a significant fall in the maternal P a CO 2 (from 38.2 ± 1.5 to 34.6 ± 1.5 mmHg) due to maternal hyperventilation ( 91 , 94 , 184 , 185 ). Similar changes in P a O 2 and P a CO 2 were observed in sheep transported from sea level to high altitudes (3,050 m; P a O 2 : 100 vs. 49.6 mmHg and P a CO 2 : 31.4 vs. 30.6 mmHg) ( 186 ). These experimental findings in ovine models support the concept that maternal hyperventilation is conserved across species and represents a key mechanism for protecting fetal oxygenation at high altitude. As P a CO 2 is inversely related to pH ( 187 ), it will be expected that the decrease in arterial P a CO 2 will increase pH during pregnancy and further augment it at high altitude. Consequently, arterialized blood pH in pregnant women is higher at high altitudes than at low altitudes ( 177 , 179 ). One study reported that at high altitude, pregnant women exhibit more arterial alkalosis (pH = 7.44–7.46) than non-pregnant women (7.42) ( 188 ), whereas another study reported that blood pH was similar between pregnant and non-pregnant women ( 177 ). At low altitude, pregnancy only slightly increases the pH from non-pregnancy (7.45 ± 0.02 vs. 7.43 ± 0.02, respectively). In pregnant sheep studies, besides an initial short-term respiratory adjustment, arterial pH did not change when pregnant ewes were chronically exposed to hypoxia for 11 days ( 92 ), 21 days ( 94 ), or 1 month ( 184 , 185 ), indicating species-specific mechanisms of acid/base homeostasis. More studies correlating blood pH and clinical outcomes in high-altitude settings are required to further detail the interactive effect of changes in blood pH, respiratory adjustments and pregnancy. Despite the lower P a O 2 , arterial O 2 content (C a O 2 ) is better maintained in high- vs low-altitude pregnancy due to higher hemoglobin levels resulting from highlanders’ well-known increased red blood cell production. However, despite their higher hemoglobin levels, C a O 2 remains marginally lower during pregnancy in newcomer or multigenerational Andean or Tibetan high-altitude-adapted individuals compared with their lower-altitude counterparts ( 31 , 179 , 189 ). The molecule 2,3-diphosphoglycerate (2,3-DPG), found in red blood cells, is also influenced by high altitude. 2,3-DPG increases in high altitude shift the O 2 dissociation curve to the right, thereby decreasing hemoglobin’s O 2 affinity and facilitating enhanced O 2 delivery to tissues ( 190 , 191 ). Similarly, 2,3-DPG increases during pregnancy and decreases after postpartum ( 192 , 193 ), further suggesting a mechanism to ensure adequate O 2 release during pregnancy. However, no studies to date have addressed the effect of both high altitude and pregnancy on the increase of 2,3-DPG. Overall, the respiratory responses to pregnancy at high altitude encompass physical and biochemical processes that help maintain blood gas and base homeostasis under chronic hypoxia, thereby supporting O 2 availability to the developing fetus. Notably, these respiratory adjustments occur in pregnant women living at high altitude, but their magnitude and effectiveness vary by ancestry and duration of high-altitude residence. For example, in a Peruvian cohort at 4,300 m, women with at least three generations of family residence at high altitude maintained arterial oxygenation throughout pregnancy better than those with shorter family residence, despite similar increases in V E ( 176 , 177 ). More broadly, Andean and Tibetan high-altitude natives have larger lung volumes and characteristic chest morphology and exhibit genetic and developmental adaptations of the O 2 -transport system that are associated with improved fetal growth compared with women of lowland ancestry at the same altitude ( 17 , 40 , 58 ). In contrast, low-altitude visitors and recent migrants primarily rely on general acclimatization responses (increased ventilation, hemoglobin, and cardiac output) to maintain maternal and fetal oxygenation. 3.2. CARDIOVASCULAR FUNCTION 3.2.1. CARDIOVASCULAR CHANGES DURING PREGNANCY AT LOW ALTITUDE Extensive cardiovascular changes are required to meet the maternal and fetal metabolic demands during pregnancy. The maternal systemic vasculature undergoes a marked reduction in resistance, resulting in a 30% fall in blood pressure, a parallel rise in plasma volume, and a 30–60% increase in cardiac output ( Figure 4 ). These changes begin early in pregnancy, with both the fall in peripheral vascular resistance and rise in plasma volume being evident by week 6–7 ( 194 , 195 ), by which point 75–80% of the overall fall in blood pressure is present ( 196 ), reflecting rapid hemodynamic responses. The pregnancy-related increase in maternal cardiac output results from rises in both heart rate and stroke volume, with the former playing a major role early in pregnancy by week 6 and the latter becoming more prominent at later stages ( 197 – 199 ). The mechanisms underlying these early systemic vascular changes remain incompletely understood. Previously, fetoplacental hormones were considered the main drivers, but this seems unlikely given that robust changes occur before the fetoplacental circulation is established ( 195 ). In addition, no correlation has been found between early hemodynamic changes and maternal 17-beta estradiol or 17-hydroxyprogesterone levels ( 198 ). Ovarian and renal hormones are likely involved in the first-trimester decline in central blood pressure, which, interestingly, is more pronounced than that occurring in peripheral blood pressure and is sensed by the kidney, ovaries, and uterus ( 199 , 200 ). By week 6, plasma renin activity and serum aldosterone are already elevated, consistent with early activation of the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system and increased renal blood flow ( 195 , 201 ). Ovarian hormones activate endothelial nitric oxide (NO) synthase (eNOS), cyclooxygenase, and/or K + channels, and thereby decrease myogenic tone, increase vasodilator response to local or circulating factors, leading to reductions in systemic vascular resistance (SVR) ( 70 , 202 – 204 ). Specifically, relaxin, produced by the ovarian corpus luteum, can activate eNOS, as well as prostanoids, vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), and K + channels, all of which have potent vasodilator and angiogenic actions ( 205 , 206 ). These reductions in vascular resistance, prompted by changes in renal and ovarian hormones, are key contributors to the rise in blood volume beginning in the first trimester. The blood volume increase is due chiefly to increased plasma levels, and while red blood cell production increases as well, it does so to a lesser degree than the increase in plasma volume for reasons that remain incompletely understood ( 195 ). Cardiac output increases due to reduced systemic vascular resistance, blood volume expansion and a later rise in metabolic rate ( 207 , 208 ). While an increase in cardiac output is universal among mammals, the temporal pattern in humans is distinctive. Unlike other mammals in which the rise is continuous ( 209 – 213 ), in women, cardiac output rises steadily in the first and second trimesters, peaks at weeks 20–24, and then generally plateaus before declining slightly toward term ( 197 , 199 ) ( Figure 4 ). This distinctive human pattern can be attributed to the uniquely habitual human upright bipedal posture and resultant positioning of the uterus on top of the vena cava ( 197 ). The third-trimester expansion of the uterus therefore compresses the vena cava, which, in turn, leads to lower left ventricular (LV) filling and stroke volume when standing, sitting, or supine. In particular, supine posture lowers maternal internal iliac artery blood flow by 24%, umbilical vein flow by 11%, and O 2 delivery to the fetus by 5% when compared to values obtained in the left lateral position ( 198 , 199 , 214 ). Fewer studies have investigated the cardiovascular responses to hypoxia in healthy low-altitude pregnancy. One study reported that the increase in maternal heart rate in response to acute hypoxia is markedly blunted in the pregnant compared to the non-pregnant ewe, without changes in resting heart rate ( 215 ). Although the mechanism accounting for depressed tachycardic response to systemic hypoxia remains unknown, elevated levels of the primary metabolite of progesterone, 3α-hydroxy-dihydroprogesterone (3α-OH DHP), may alter gamma-aminobutyric (GABA) and/or opioid inhibition of cardiovascular centers in the brain. Administration of i.v. 3α-OH DHP to virgin female rats attenuated sympatho-excitatory responses to a fall in arterial blood pressure, similar to the response seen during pregnancy ( 216 , 217 ), by potentiating GABAergic inhibition of sympathetic outflow in the rostral ventrolateral medulla ( 216 ). In addition, the density of GABA-releasing synaptic boutons on oxytocin neurones and the opening time of GABA A receptors were increased by administration of 3α-OH DHP to virgin female rats ( 218 ). The human uterine vasculature ( Figure 5 ) undergoes dramatic structural and functional remodeling during early pregnancy to accommodate the exponential increases in uteroplacental blood flow required for fetal growth and placental function. As early as week 6 of pregnancy, the diameter of the main uterine artery has doubled ( 219 ), and similar increases are observed in the arcuate arteries during the first trimester ( 220 ). In guinea pig and rat models, these changes are attributed to outward hyperplastic and hypertrophic growth and remodeling of all layers of the uterine and radial artery vascular wall ( 221 , 222 ). The increase in uterine artery growth, measured by labeling DNA synthesis, was highest in the adventitia of the uterine and radial arteries at mid-gestation, while it gradually increased in the media and intima layers of the radial artery ( 222 ). Serum estradiol levels show a positive correlation with the increase in DNA synthesis in the uterine artery adventitia and all layers of the uterine radial artery ( 222 ), indicating that early pregnancy growth continues the menstrual cycle, during which estrogen, along with progesterone, VEGF, and uterine natural killer cells, promote angiogenesis, vascular elongation, and endothelial proliferation ( 220 ). In addition to estrogen, relaxin is likely involved, which peaks during the first trimester ( 223 ). Its receptor, RXFP1, is expressed in uterine artery smooth muscle and endothelial cells, and exogenous relaxin infusion increases uterine artery blood flow in animal models ( 224 ). Importantly, these changes in vascular growth are confined to the uterine vasculature; in guinea pigs, no parallel increases in DNA synthesis were observed in non-uterine arteries such as the mesenteric artery or thoracic aorta ( 225 ). Early enlargement of the upstream uterine and arcuate arteries occurs independently of both the initial rise in uterine artery blood flow and trophoblast invasion. Trophoblast cells, which are integral to the process of placentation, likely do not contribute, since they do not migrate past the spiral arterioles or distal myometrial arteries. Nor is the upstream vascular enlargement due to a first-trimester rise in uterine blood flow, given that endovascular trophoblasts plug the ends of the spiral arterioles until around 13 weeks when placentation is complete ( 226 ). After the trophoblast plugs are discharged, uterine blood flow rises and placental PO 2 increases approximately 2.5-fold ( 227 , 228 ). Thus, expansion of the uterine and arcuate artery diameter precedes and enables the second trimester and the continued rise in uterine artery blood flow. The enlargement of the uterine artery vascular diameter is the predominant determinant until about week 16, after which increasing blood flow velocity is chiefly responsible ( 229 – 231 ). At term, uterine artery blood flow can increase as much as 52-fold above non-pregnant values (depending on the non-pregnant values used) ( 51 , 229 – 232 ) ( Figure 6 ), whereas uterine artery blood flow relative to fetal weight declines from week 20 onwards ( 231 ). Since cardiac output plateaus, maintaining blood flow to the placenta and fetus requires uterine vascular resistance to be lower than in the other maternal vascular beds. At least three mechanisms are involved in lowering uterine relative to non-uterine vascular resistance. The first is the selective growth of the main uterine and arcuate arteries as detailed above. The second factor involves trophoblast-mediated transformation of spiral arteries into large-bore, low-resistance vessels ( 233 ). The third mechanism involves functional adaptation of myometrial arteries, which enlarge during pregnancy but remain smaller than the upstream and downstream arteries, thereby becoming the primary site of uterine vascular resistance ( 234 , 235 ). Since enlargement of the myometrial arteries is largely complete by week 16 ( 230 ), the vasodilator responses of the myometrial arteries, as well as the upstream uterine and arcuate arteries, have become a central topic for understanding uteroplacental ischemia-associated pregnancy complications. Multiple overlapping factors contribute to enhancing uterine vasodilator responses ( 236 ). Experimental animal data from pseudopregnant models support a role for early increases in estradiol and relaxin in promoting NO production and thereby augmenting vasodilator sensitivity ( 237 ). From the second trimester onward, placental estrogen acts via its estrogen receptors (ER) alpha and beta to stimulate endothelial- dependent and independent uterine artery relaxation ( 238 ). Myogenic tone, which is the pressure-dependent constriction or dilation response, is reduced in the main uterine artery ( 203 , 239 – 241 ), but increased in the downstream radial artery (rats) or myometrial vessels (human) ( 242 , 243 ). As shown by in vitro studies, pregnancy enhances the vasodilator responses in the main uterine artery as well as downstream arcuate, radial, or myometrial ( Figure 5 ) isolated vessel segments. These vasoactive responses are enhanced during pregnancy by increased endothelial production of NO, prostaglandins, hydrogen sulfide (H 2 S), and VEGF, among other factors ( 244 – 247 ). The key role of the NO and K + channel pathways in mediating the increased vasodilator responses, promoting the pregnancy-dependent rise in maternal uterine blood flow, is further supported by in vivo studies in animal models ( 248 , 249 ). Simultaneously, uterine vessels become increasingly refractory to vasoconstrictors, including angiotensin II, endothelin, norepinephrine, serotonin and neuropeptide Y ( 202 , 250 – 255 ). 3.2.2 CARDIOVASCULAR CHANGES DURING PREGNANCY AT HIGH ALTITUDE Pregnancy at high altitude presents a unique physiological challenge, requiring additional maternal cardiovascular adaptations to sustain adequate O 2 and nutrient delivery to the fetus under chronic hypoxic conditions. At high altitude, several blood gases and acid-base status changes induce strong cardiovascular responses during pregnancy. For instance, pregnant women at 4,300 m showed a marked decrease in P a O 2 , P a CO 2 , bicarbonate, base excess, and S a O 2 relative to sea-level women ( 177 ). Pregnant ewes at 3,600 m showed similar responses, where a significant P a O 2 decrease (by half) is associated with alkalosis, hypocapnia, and increased hemoglobin content ( 256 ). Systemic circulatory adaptation in high-altitude pregnancy contrasts with low-altitude pregnancy in several respects. In human pregnancies, cardiac output is lower during the second trimester in high- compared to low-altitude women ( Figure 4A and B ) due to reductions in both stroke volume and heart rate ( 257 – 259 ). Lower stroke volume was linked to diminished LV filling ( 257 , 258 ), itself a consequence of lower blood and plasma volume before and during pregnancy at high altitude ( 258 ). In altitude-adapted Peruvian highland residents (Cerro de Pasco, 4,370 m), reductions in SVR and blood pressure during the second trimester closely parallel those at sea level (Lima, 154 m) ( 257 ) ( Figure 4 ). In contrast, healthy highlanders in Bolivia (La Paz-El Alto, 3,600–4,100 m) ( 259 ) showed 30% higher SVR between 10–16 weeks of pregnancy than values reported in healthy lowland-resident women studied in the first trimester ( 208 , 260 ). However, in newcomer highland residents, the characteristic fall in blood pressure is blunted compared with low-altitude counterparts ( 35 , 49 ) ( Figure 4D ). Consistent with these findings, pregnant sheep exposed to chronic hypoxia in late gestation, either as a result of high altitude ( 261 ) or in isobaric hypoxic chambers ( 90 , 262 ), do not show the characteristic fall in arterial blood pressure observed across gestation in ewes undergoing normoxic pregnancy. Guinea pig models likewise reveal that chronic high-altitude (3,900 m) exposure limits the normal pregnancy-associated reduction in resting maternal SVR and enhances vascular contractility, as evidenced by heightened responses to vasoconstrictors such as angiotensin II and norepinephrine in isolated aortic rings ( 263 ). Chronic high-altitude hypoxia profoundly affects uterine vascular structure and function. In guinea pigs, chronic hypoxia reduces vascular growth throughout gestation in all layers of the uterine and mesometrial artery wall, as shown by DNA synthesis labeling studies ( 225 ). Comparable reductions in uterine artery smooth muscle cell proliferative and growth responses have been observed in vitro using cells cultured from chronically hypoxic versus normoxic animals ( 264 ). Chronic hypoxia also affects uterine vascular tone and biomechanical properties such as distensibility and circumferential wall stress-strain relationships. Pressurized uterine arteries isolated from chronically hypoxic pregnant guinea pigs ( 265 ) or sheep ( 266 ) display increased myogenic tone, attributed in part to inhibition of extracellular signal-regulated kinases 1 and 2 activity, augmentation of the protein kinase C (PKC) pathway, and upregulation of large-conductance Ca 2+ -activated K + (BK Ca ) channel activity ( 267 ). In guinea pigs, chronic hypoxia also raised uterine artery distensibility fourfold, decreased stiffness, and lowered elastin by 50%, suggesting that these structural changes contribute to enlargement of the uterine artery during pregnancy. In this same model, however, hypoxia shifted the stress-strain relationship such that four times as much stress was present at a given level of strain ( 265 ). Functionally, studies in both human altitude newcomers and experimental animal models demonstrate that chronic exposure to high altitude blunts the normal pregnancy rise in uterine artery blood flow, due primarily to an approximately 25% lesser increase in uterine artery diameter than observed during pregnancy under normoxic conditions, which, in turn, decreases uterine artery blood flow ( Figure 6B ). In pregnant guinea pigs, chronic hypoxia increased the uterine artery resistance and pulsatility indexes compared to normoxic controls ( 67 ). Similarly, women living at high altitudes have consistently shown reduced uterine artery diameter, lower volumetric blood flow, and greater impedance than their low-altitude counterparts ( 31 , 51 , 53 , 268 , 269 ). Consistent with these findings, pregnant sheep exposed to chronic hypoxia in late gestation in isobaric chambers do not show the characteristic fall in uterine vascular resistance or the increase in uterine blood flow measured across gestation in ewes undergoing normoxic pregnancy ( 90 , 262 ). The differential uterine vascular response to pregnancy in chronically hypoxic ewes is also accompanied by an increase in placental molecular markers of oxidative stress and activation of the unfolding protein response, as seen in placenta from preeclamptic women. Therefore, chronic hypoxia in ovine pregnancy or high altitude human pregnancy recapitulate physiological and molecular markers of preeclampsia, which may contribute to the higher rates of FGR ( 90 , 262 ). Interestingly, the prevalence of preeclampsia is significantly greater in high- vs low-altitude pregnancy, when the same criteria are used for diagnosis (i.e., blood pressures in excess of 140/90 mmHg, with proteinuria and other indices of end-organ dysfunction), and it is associated with the same adverse outcomes as seen at low altitudes ( 49 , 50 , 270 , 271 ). Vasoregulatory adaptations are also altered by chronic hypoxia. While uterine artery vasoconstrictor responses to agents like potassium chloride, phenylephrine, or the thromboxane mimetic U46619 are unaffected, pregnancy-induced stimulation of NO production is significantly reduced in the uterine vasculature of high- versus low-altitude guinea pigs, rats, and humans ( 55 , 66 , 272 ). The uterine artery vasodilator response to flow has also been shown to be markedly reduced in chronically hypoxic guinea pigs, with high flow rates even inducing vasoconstriction ( 273 ). The effects of uterine artery vasoreactivity and fetal growth in pregnant rats exposed to chronic hypoxia depend on the timing of the onset of hypoxic exposure and the magnitude of the challenge. While late-onset gestational hypoxia from days 15–20 at 11% maternal fraction of inspired O 2 (F i O 2 ) leads to impaired uterine artery dilator responses to cholinergic agonists and FGR ( 66 ), early-onset gestational hypoxia from days 6–20 at 13% maternal F i O 2 maintains fetal growth, induces enhanced responses to both constrictor and dilator agonists in the uterine vasculature ( 274 ), and increases placental volume and the fetal capillary surface area ( 275 ). Combined, these findings suggest that early-onset gestational hypoxia in rats triggers compensatory responses in the placenta and uteroplacental vascular bed to ameliorate the effects of chronic hypoxia, thereby protecting against impairment in uterine artery blood flow and FGR. K + channels play a crucial role in regulating vascular function by both promoting vasodilation and reducing myogenic tone, as shown by extensive studies using both human and experimental animal tissues ( 276 , 277 ). In particular, pregnancy increases the activity of the BK Ca channel, a major regulator of vascular function, in uterine arteries (both the main and resistance-size vessels), promoting vasodilation and reducing myogenic tone ( 203 , 278 ). High altitude attenuates the pregnancy-dependent reduction in myogenic tone by inhibiting BK Ca channels in uterine resistance vessels ( 267 ). Several mechanisms are likely involved in the chronic hypoxia-dependent reduction of BK Ca channel activity in the maternal uterine vasculature. Gestational chronic hypoxia upregulates PKC, which, in turn, reduces BK Ca channel activity in ovine pregnant uterine arteries ( 266 , 279 ). In contrast, early-onset hypoxic pregnancy in rats enhanced uterine artery dilator reactivity to nitroprusside and acetylcholine near term. Mechanistic vascular studies revealed that the enhanced endothelium-dependent reactivity to acetylcholine is mediated, in part, by activation of BK Ca channels ( 274 ). Exposure to hypoxia during pregnancy can also increase uterine artery reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation, which, when derived from the mitochondria, contributes to the enhanced uterine vasoconstrictor phenotype, as this effect can be blocked with the mitochondria-targeted antioxidant mitoquinone mesylate (MitoQ) ( 274 ). Gestational hypoxia-induced ROS also inhibits the function of steroid hormones and leads to the downregulation of the BK Ca channel β1 subunit, an important positive regulator of BK Ca channel activity ( 280 , 281 ). Pregnancy increases the expression of the BK Ca β1 subunit in the ovine uterine artery via epigenetic demethylation of its promoter ( 282 ). Conversely, chronic hypoxia enhances DNA methyltransferase activity, leading to increased methylation of the β1 subunit promoter and downregulation of its expression; this results in reduced BK Ca channel ionic currents and increased uterine artery myogenic tone ( 283 ). Moreover, the pregnancy-dependent rise in estrogen augments BK Ca channel activity in the uterine artery, whereas ERα expression is downregulated under conditions of chronic hypoxia ( 284 , 285 ), leading to reduced BK Ca channel activity and exacerbating myogenic tone ( 286 ). The hypoxia-induced reduction in ERα expression is mediated by methylation of DNA at binding sites for specific protein 1 and upstream stimulatory factor ( 284 ). Another mechanism by which chronic hypoxia has been reported to reduce BK Ca activity in the uterine circulation is through the expression of so-called “hypoxamiRs”, that is, miRNAs regulated by hypoxia, such as miR-210. miR-210 is a conserved small non-coding RNA that mediates several cellular processes (e.g., cell cycle) and is induced under hypoxic conditions, upregulated by hypoxia-inducible factor (HIF)-1α ( 287 – 289 ). In the uterine arteries of pregnant ewes exposed to chronic hypoxia, miR-210 reduces the expression of the enzyme ten-eleven translocation methylcytosine dioxygenase 1, which is critical for regulating DNA demethylation, leading to hypermethylation of the BK Ca β1 promoter and reduced BK Ca channel activity ( 261 ). Studies of myometrial arteries from healthy, pregnant women delivering appropriate-for-gestational-age (AGA) newborns at high altitude demonstrate reduced BK Ca channel activity, and this reduction is accompanied by a compensatory increase in the activity of another crucial vascular K + channel, the ATP-sensitive K + (K ATP ) channel ( 54 ). Another mechanism by which uterine vasculature may be affected by chronic hypoxia derives from evidence obtained from multigenerational high-altitude populations, who are genetically protected from high-altitude-associated reductions in uterine artery blood flow and fetal growth ( Figure 7 ). Andean residents in Bolivia (3,600 m) have a greater rise in uterine artery blood flow during pregnancy than women of European ancestry (regardless of their individual length of high-altitude residence), principally due to larger uterine artery diameter, as there were no ethnic group differences in blood flow velocity at high altitude nor any other uterine artery parameters ( 31 ). Similarly, altitude-adapted Tibetans showed higher uterine artery blood flow velocity and greater redistribution of pelvic flow toward the uterine artery during the third trimester than Han (Chinese) during pregnancy at 3,600 m ( 189 ). Andean women at high altitude had a greater pregnancy-related increase in uterine artery blood flow than their low-altitude counterparts ( 31 ), whereas Colorado high-altitude newcomers had lower levels of uterine artery blood flow from week 20 onward than seen at low altitude ( 51 ), supporting the modulating effect of ancestry on maternal physiological responses to pregnancy. Specifically, in high-altitude Andean populations, an association has been described between genetic variants predicted to activate the AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) pathway and increased uterine artery blood flow in pregnancy ( 58 ). In Bolivia, highland-resident (La Paz, 3,600 m) Andean or European women homozygous for the putatively adaptive PRKAA1 variant rs1345778 (T), which encodes the α1 catalytic subunit of AMPK, delivered infants of greater birthweight and had larger uterine artery diameter at 36 weeks of pregnancy ( 58 ). Although rs1345778 is located outside the protein-coding region, it maps to a distal enhancer-like element that can modulate transcription factor binding affinity and chromatin accessibility, thereby affecting gene expression and disease risk. In this context, the finding that the transcriptional profiles of the AMPK-mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) pathway in maternal peripheral blood mononuclear cells varied significantly by rs1345778 genotype in pregnant Bolivian highlanders is particularly notable ( 58 ). While the rs1345778 variant is enriched in Andeans (allele frequency, 0.83), it is also evident at moderate frequency (0.67) in the ancestrally-diverse, predominantly lowland populations such as those represented by the Trans-Omics for Precision Medicine cohort, which is comprised of European (52%), African (31%), Latino (10%) and East Asian (7%) ( 290 ); this suggests that its protective role is likely not confined to Andeans ( 291 ). Recent work also identified a PRKAA1 SNP (rs10035235, C&gt;T) bearing signatures of positive selection in Andeans as being associated with variation in ventilatory and sleep-related phenotypes in men and non-pregnant women at high altitudes ( 292 ). Furthermore, another recent study identified associations between maternal genotypes related to O 2 delivery and placental metabolic phenotypes in an Andean population at high altitude (3,600–4,100 m), with differences in frequency of preeclampsia ( 60 ). AMPK is a cellular energy sensor with established vasodilator effects in the coronary and renal circulations ( 293 , 294 ). More recently, AMPK activation has also been shown to regulate uterine vascular responses to pregnancy at high altitudes, both in experimental animal and human studies. In chronically hypoxic mice, AMPK activation in isolated uterine artery segments by the pharmacological AMPK activator A766962 or in vivo using 5-Aminoimidazole-4-carboxamide ribonucleotide (AICAR) consistently vasodilated uterine vessels, helped maintain normal uterine artery blood flow, and halved the hypoxia-induced reduction in birth weight ( 65 , 295 ). Similar to mouse uterine arteries, activation of AMPK induced greater endothelium-dependent vasodilation in myometrial arteries collected from pregnant women residing at high altitudes (above 2,500 m) in Colorado than in low-altitude women, but had no effect in vessels from FGR pregnancies, regardless of altitude ( 56 ). The vasodilator actions of AMPK were partially dependent on NO, but not on prostaglandins, and involved endothelium-independent pathways as well ( 56 , 65 ). Human myometrial arteries from high-altitude women showed impaired cholinergic, but not bradykinergic, NO-mediated vasodilation compared to those from women residing at a lower altitude (1,609 m) ( 55 ). Moreover, recent human studies in newcomer Colorado high-altitude residents demonstrated a positive correlation between uterine artery diameter and PRKAA1 gene expression at week 20, whereas no such relationship was observed at low altitudes ( 269 ). Collectively, these findings suggest a compensatory role for AMPK activation in the maternal uteroplacental circulation at week 20, helping to offset the suppressive effects of hypoxia-related reductions in uterine artery diameter during pregnancy. Another pathway of interest for the regulation of uterine artery blood flow during hypoxic pregnancy is peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma (PPARγ). This hypoxia-sensitive, ligand-activated transcription factor regulates metabolic homeostasis, inflammation, and vascular function ( 296 – 298 ). PPARγ also has vasoprotective properties by improving endothelium-dependent vasorelaxation ( 299 ), suppressing the synthesis of the potent vasoconstrictor endothelin-1 ( 300 ), and increasing NO production ( 301 , 302 ). In Bolivian cohorts, differences in the transcription of numerous genes of relevance for pregnancy were seen in maternal peripheral blood mononuclear cells from non-pregnant (postpartum) and pregnant (weeks 20 and 36) high-altitude women (La Paz, 3,600 m) versus sea level (Santa Cruz, 300 m). Specifically, the patterns suggested negative regulation of PPARγ at high altitude during pregnancy but not postpartum ( 303 ). Experimental studies in mice showed that dietary supplementation with pioglitazone, a pharmacologic PPARγ agonist, partially prevented hypoxia-induced FGR ( 69 ). Subsequent in vitro studies showed that troglitazone, a high-affinity PPARγ agonist, induced vasorelaxation in uterine arteries from dams exposed to late-gestation normoxia or hypoxia, with hypoxic animals showing increased sensitivity ( 73 ). These observations suggest that PPARγ may play a larger vasodilator role in the uterine vasculature from pregnancies exposed to chronic hypoxia, thereby preventing the effects of gestational hypoxia. 4. PLACENTAL ADAPTATIONS TO HIGH ALTITUDE The placenta is a transient, multifunctional organ that physically connects the developing fetus to the mother and orchestrates nutrient and gas exchange, hormone production, and immunological adaptation essential for successful pregnancy outcomes. While local tissue hypoxia is a necessary physiological condition during early gestation, particularly during implantation and placental development, chronic exposure to atmospheric low O 2 levels throughout pregnancy presents significant challenges that may compromise normal fetal and maternal physiology. Consequently, the placenta serves as an important mediator of reproductive success under hypoxic conditions, coordinating adaptive responses to aid in fetal development under conditions of reduced O 2 availability. Studies of healthy Andean women have confirmed chronic hypoxic exposure, as measurements of O 2 tension in maternal arteries demonstrate a PO 2 reduction of ~40–45 mmHg compared to sea-level pregnancies (~98–100 mmHg) ( 177 , 304 ). In addition, the decrease in O 2 levels within placental tissue is less pronounced, approximately 12 mmHg (estimated from the PO 2 gradient between maternal arteries and umbilical vein) ( 235 ), which supports the presence of placental adaptive mechanisms that preserve adequate O 2 flow to the fetus. This section reviews the current state of knowledge regarding placental adaptations to high-altitude environments, with a focus on the effects of chronic hypoxia on placental development, structure, and metabolism. Elucidating these mechanisms clarifies the principal strategies the placenta employs to help fetal growth under conditions of reduced O 2 availability. Evidence is reviewed from studies of human populations residing at high elevations, experimental animal models of gestational hypoxia, and in vitro investigations using primary trophoblasts and cell lines exposed to varying O 2 concentrations. Collectively, these models reveal convergent strategies that optimize O 2 transfer and metabolic efficiency during development in hypoxic environments. 4.1. PLACENTAL DEVELOPMENT Placenta formation and the development of the maternal-fetal interface require the coordinated growth of new blood vessels to facilitate blood flow and the exchange of O 2 and nutrients between the mother and the fetus. Below, and in Figure 8 , we summarize the main physiological, cellular, and molecular processes for placental development that are affected by chronic hypoxia. Under healthy, physiological (non-pathological) conditions (17.9 mmHg O 2 /2.5% O 2 , measured in the placental bed) ( 305 ), the extravillous cytotrophoblasts (EVTs) promote implantation by invading the maternal uterine decidua and stimulating the remodeling of spiral arteries ( 306 ). During this process, EVTs modify their adhesion profiles and regulate changes in the extracellular matrix by inducing the expression of several matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), including MMP-2 and MMP-9 ( 307 , 308 ). Furthermore, invasive EVTs also undergo integrin switching ( 309 ), characterized by downregulation of the laminin α6β4 receptor ( 310 ) and upregulation of fibronectin α5β1 ( 311 ) and laminin/collagen α1β1 receptors ( 312 ). These alterations facilitate EVT interaction with the maternal decidual extracellular matrix and enable invasion of the spiral arteries, leading to their remodeling. In preeclamptic pregnancies, this invasive phenotype is diminished, as no changes are observed in the α6β4 and weekly expression of α1β1 receptors ( 313 ). Moreover, EVTs exposed to in vitro hypoxic stimulation (0.1% O 2 for 1 h) have decreased MMP-2 activity compared with 5% O 2 controls ( 314 ). MMP-2 is strongly localized within the placental bed ( 315 ), further contributing to decreased invasion as seen in pathological conditions. Trophoblast signaling is essential for remodeling uterine end arterioles, thereby inducing a high-conductance phenotype and reducing vascular resistance. This process facilitates efficient maternal blood flow to the placenta, ensuring adequate fetal blood supply. Epithelial cadherin is expressed in the trophoblast during early pregnancy within the fusion-competent cytotrophoblast (CTB), and its downregulation is required for trophoblast invasion ( 316 , 317 ). In contrast, vascular endothelial cadherin (VE-cadherin) expression increases as the trophoblast interacts with the endothelium of the decidual spiral arteries ( 318 ), ultimately facilitating the acquisition of a vascular phenotype that enables the replacement of spiral artery endothelial cells and subsequent vascular remodeling ( 319 ). VE-cadherin expression is reduced in pregnancies that are severely complicated by preeclampsia ( 320 ). However, it is not yet established whether this reduction is a cause or a result of the placental dysfunction characteristic of this condition. Residence at high altitudes during pregnancy alters trophoblast proliferation. Placental samples from high-altitude pregnancies demonstrate increased CTB proliferation and undifferentiation ( 321 ), an effect similar to that observed in placentas of preeclamptic patients and placental explants cultured under low-O 2 conditions ( 322 , 323 ). Furthermore, integrin α6, a marker of an immature villous phenotype ( 324 ), is elevated in high-altitude placentas ( 321 ). These findings indicate a reduction in the invasive phenotype of the trophoblast in pregnancies complicated by preeclampsia or high-altitude exposure, which would be expected to reduce spiral artery remodeling, as demonstrated in vitro ( 325 ). Animal models provide mechanistic insights into the effect of hypoxia on placental adaptation. In rats, trophoblast cells from the placental junctional zone (JZ) invade deeply into the uterine spiral arteries ( 326 ). Hypobaric hypoxic exposure (420 mmHg, 11% O 2 equivalent) during mid-gestation in rats increases endovascular trophoblast invasion ( 327 ). Although hypoxia and activation HIF pathways are necessary for trophoblast plasticity and placental adaptation ( 328 ), chronic maternal hypoxia does not improve placentation or vascular remodeling. In fact, late exposure to hypoxia impedes trophoblastic invasion ( 327 ), and chronic gestational hypoxia (starting early in gestation) impairs both trophoblast invasion and uteroplacental vascular remodeling in rats ( 329 ). Exposure of guinea pigs to hypoxia during mid-gestation increased trophoblast proliferation and reduced spiral artery remodeling by these cells, thereby modeling key features of abnormal placental development linked to pregnancy complications such as FGR and preeclampsia ( 68 ). Before the first trimester, O 2 levels in the placenta are approximately 2.5 times lower than those in the decidua, to prevent the harmful effects resulting from an excess of O 2 free radicals on this tissue ( 227 ). As pregnancy advances and maternal circulation is established in the intervillous space, placental O 2 levels increase ( 330 ). Low O 2 levels are also important for trophoblast differentiation, helping maintain an immature state and thereby avoiding premature trophoblast invasion. In human villous explants, hypoxia increases HIF-1α stabilization in the early stages of trophoblast differentiation, coinciding with elevated levels of transforming growth factor beta-3 (TGF-β3) ( 331 ), an essential inhibitor of EVT differentiation and invasion ( 332 ). As O 2 rises across gestational stages, HIF-1α and TGF-β3 expression decreases, further releasing this restraint ( 331 ). Consistent with this, low O 2 exposure promotes self-renewal of human trophoblast cells while inhibiting their differentiation ( 333 ), emphasizing the regulatory role of O 2 in early development. Further research on primary cells and BeWo cell lines has shown that as syncytialization progresses, HIF-1α levels decline and HIF-2α levels rise ( 334 ). This increase in HIF-2α is linked to reduced placental growth factor (PIGF) secretion, which is typically abundant in trophoblasts and plays an essential role in the placental angiogenic profile ( 335 ). Knocking out HIF-1α and HIF-2α reduces trophoblast invasion by 17% compared to wild-type mice ( 336 ), further supporting the essential role of HIFs in regulating early stages of invasion and syncytialization. These findings demonstrate that pathological or chronic hypoxia associated with high-altitude pregnancies, which can lead to conditions such as preeclampsia, disrupts physiological processes in the trophoblast as integrin switching, cadherin expression, and MMP activity. Such disruptions impair normal trophoblast invasion, decrease vascular remodeling, and consequently compromise placental function. Evidence from animal models further suggests that transient hypoxia may promote adaptive trophoblast invasion, while sustained low O 2 levels hinder placental development. Overall, these results underscore the critical balance between physiological and pathological hypoxic signaling during placental development. 4.2. PLACENTAL STRUCTURE The first studies on placental morphological adaptations to high altitude were conducted in Peru ( 337 ). These indicated that high-altitude pregnancies were associated with a 4% increase in placental weight and an 18% decrease in birth weight compared with pregnancies at lower altitudes. Consistently, the birth weight:placental weight ratio, an indicator of placental efficiency ( 338 ), was 25% lower at high altitudes than at low altitudes ( 337 ). Subsequent studies in native high-altitude residents confirmed these patterns ( 339 ). However, other studies performed in European or Mestiza (newcomer), and Aymara or Quechua Amerindian (altitude-adapted) women living in La Paz (3,600 m) or Santa Cruz (400 m), Bolivia, revealed no interaction between altitude or ethnicity in placental weight, although a smaller birth weight:placental weight ratio was observed in high-altitude populations, and the birth weight was lower in European populations living in high elevation compared to Andean residents ( 340 ). Among individuals of European descent residing at high altitude in Leadville, Colorado (3,100 m), placental weight did not differ significantly from that at lower altitude in Denver, Colorado (1,600 m), but the birth weight:placental weight ratio was reduced in the high-altitude group ( 341 ). In contrast, research conducted in moderate altitudes (2,200–2,800 m) found no significant differences in placental efficiency compared to low-altitude (700 m) pregnancies, despite lower fetal and placental weights at the higher elevations ( 46 ). In summary, the bulk of the evidence suggests a decrease in the birth weight:placental weight ratio at altitudes greater than 3,000 m, and overall, there appears to be little consistent effect of high-altitude or prolonged residence ancestry on placental weight. Placental shape, however, appears to be affected by high-altitude residence. Early studies in high- and low-altitude residents of Peru demonstrated altitudinal differences such that approximately 26% of high-altitude placentas were irregular in shape, whereas the majority (92%) of low-altitude placentas were round or oval ( 337 , 342 ). Irregular morphology is somewhat (but not significantly) associated with higher umbilical cord hemoglobin levels ( 342 ) and has been proposed as a compensatory adaptive mechanism to enhance fetal O 2 delivery ( 337 ). Further research is needed to establish the relationships of placental shape and fetal hemoglobin levels with fetal O 2 delivery. Structural differences have been observed between high-altitude and low-altitude human placentas. Specifically, the villous membrane, consisting of an outer trophoblast layer and an inner layer dominated by fetal capillaries ( Figure 9 ), has thinner villous membranes in pregnancies in high-altitude-dwelling native Amerindians and non-native Bolivians primarily due to reduced trophoblast thickness ( 343 ). Additionally, the luminal diameter of fetal blood vessels was reduced at high altitude ( 344 ), such that the fetal blood vessels were positioned closer to the trophoblast zone and were peripherally aligned, thereby contributing to an irregular placental morphology. Another study, conducted in Central Asia at 2,220–2,800 m, reported increased capillary volume without significant changes in the intervillous space ( 46 ). Thus overall, the observed increases in capillary vascular area and villous membrane thinning enhances O 2 diffusion capacity ( 45 , 46 ) and suggests that high-altitude residence invokes dynamic changes in placental morphology and structure likely to help maintain and narrow the maternal-to-fetal O 2 gradient. The net effect of the structural changes seen at high altitudes is to increase villous volume by approximately 25%, with a similar reduction in total star volume (a measure of volume in irregular shapes) ( 345 ). Stereological estimations of the star volumes suggest that the number of villous units is similar compared to lowland-resident placentas. Alternatively, the intervillous space was reduced by about 40% relative to low-altitude placentas, accompanied by a fourfold increase in the intervillous space star volume and a decrease in intervillous space pores ( 345 ). Additional studies corroborate these results, reporting decreased villous domain star volume in highland-resident placentas ( 44 ). There is also hypercapillarization in altitude-adapted placentas ( 44 , 45 ), which likely results from hypoxia-induced production and secretion of angiogenic factors, as demonstrated in vitro. Specifically, hypoxic conditions in placental cells stimulate VEGF secretion ( 346 ), which may promote the expansion of the vascular network and contribute to altered branching patterns observed in high-altitude placentas ( 44 ). Consequently, the vascular network occupies a larger volume, increasing the surface area for O 2 exchange to compensate for reduced O 2 diffusion ( 347 ). Altogether, this data indicates that this angiogenic adaptation helps to counteract the effects of high-altitude hypoxia. Human high-altitude placentas also show increased vascularization and capillarization, which are key adaptations to high altitude ( 348 , 349 ). A reduction in placental HIF-1/DNA binding is a possible mechanism of successful adaptation ( 349 ). Conversely, HIF-1α upregulates placental soluble fms-like tyrosine kinase-1 (sFlt-1) expression, particularly in vascular and perivascular tissues of high-altitude placentas, potentially contributing to an antiangiogenic effect ( 350 ). Taken together, these studies suggest that the decreased HIF-1/DNA binding may promote a pro-vascularization state, even if sFlt-1 levels remain elevated due to HIF-1α-mediated upregulation. Another study found that increased sFlt-1 levels are primarily localized to the maternal compartment rather than the umbilical cord, which may facilitate placental vascularization and protect both the mother and the fetus from an angiogenic imbalance ( 351 ). Conversely, high-altitude placentas showed a marked increase in HIF-1α expression (and stabilization) consistent with elevated levels of downstream targets of this factor ( 352 ) including VEGF ( 321 ). Moreover, the E3 ubiquitin ligase component von Hippel-Lindau protein, a tumor suppressor that targets HIF-1α for proteasomal degradation ( 353 ), was also increased and the factor inhibiting HIF had higher mRNA but lower protein levels, suggesting that HIF signaling is more active at high altitude ( 352 ). Additional evidence indicates that circulating pro-angiogenic factors do not differ between healthy low-altitude and high-altitude pregnancies ( 354 , 355 ). These conflicting findings suggest that multiple placental mechanisms contribute to high-altitude adaptation, that these mechanisms are not solely dependent on HIF-1 and circulating pro-angiogenic factors, and that further mechanistic studies are needed to clarify the interactions between hypoxia-responsive transcription factors and their physiological roles in the placenta during high-altitude pregnancy. Besides HIF-1α, HIF-2α has also been proposed to have a role in the placental function in response to hypoxia or in high-altitude residents. For instance, human primary CTB, BeWo, and JEG-3 choriocarcinoma cell cultures exposed to hypoxia showed increased secretion of sFlt-1 that was reduced by silencing HIF-2α ( 356 ). In addition, placentas from mice exposed to hypoxia during late pregnancy (6% O 2 for 6h) showed a marked accumulation of HIF-1α and HIF-2α proteins and elevated levels of HIF targets (VEGF and erythropoietin [EPO]) compared to normoxic animals ( 357 ). Another study demonstrated elevated HIF-2α expression in placentas from complicated pregnancies, indicating a potential role in placental pathophysiology associated with FGR and preeclampsia under chronic placental hypoxia ( 358 ). A longitudinal analysis of healthy human placental samples revealed that both HIF-1α and HIF-2α protein levels are higher in early pregnancy than later stages ( 359 ), consistent with the increasing oxygenation of the placenta as pregnancy progresses. Moreover, exposure to hypoxia increased the expression of HIF-1α and HIF-2α proteins in villous explants obtained from these placentas but did not change their mRNA levels ( 359 ), suggesting that hypoxia-dependent regulation of HIF is at a protein level. Furthermore, double knockout of HIF-1α and HIF-2α results in defective placental vascularization in mice ( 336 ). In placentas from Tibetans, EPAS1 , the gene encoding HIF-2α, showed a stronger positive correlation with its partner genes and a distinct co-expression pattern with its regulators and coactivators of downstream pathways compared with non-Tibetan high- and low-altitude residents ( 9 ). Furthermore, genetic variants in EPAS1 have been consistently associated with a reduced erythropoietic response and lower hemoglobin concentrations in both Andean and Tibetan highlanders, a phenotype thought to protect against maladaptation to chronic hypoxia ( 360 , 361 ). Supporting this, Tibetan carrying an adaptive EPAS1 haplotype exhibit reduced placental HIF‑2α expression, which likely reflects an attenuated hypoxic response and reduced placental stress ( 361 ). In Andean populations, a coding variant in EPAS1 (rs570553380) is similarly associated with lower hematocrit levels. Because excessive maternal hemoglobin concentrations can impair uteroplacental perfusion and fetal development, relatively lower hemoglobin levels are proposed to confer a reproductive advantage at high altitude ( 362 ). Conversely, variants in EGLN1 , which encodes prolyl hydroxylase domain protein 2 (PHD2), have been linked to enhanced oxygen utilization and more efficient energy metabolism under hypoxic conditions ( 363 ). Together, these findings highlight the complementary roles of EPAS1 - and EGLN1 -mediated pathways in high-altitude adaptation. At the cellular level, high altitude increases the number of undifferentiated CTB cells without altering total placental surface area ( 364 ). An elevated proportion of these cells is considered an indicator of intrauterine ischemia and hypoxia in various pregnancy complications ( 365 , 366 ). Low O 2 levels disrupt CTB cell differentiation and fusion ( 367 ), thereby compromising trophoblast syncytialization and leading to reduced villous area at high altitude ( 343 , 345 ). Impaired syncytialization produces a thinner syncytiotrophoblast (STB) layer, leading to an imbalance that favors fibrinolytic over anticoagulatory activity in the intervillous space ( 368 ). Increased coagulant activity may be driven by heme oxygenase (HO)-1 deficiency ( 369 ), as short-term in vitro hypoxic exposure significantly reduces HO-1 expression in human STB ( 370 ). The regulation of HO-1 and HO-2 remains complex, and further research is required to clarify the relationship between pro-fibrinolytic activity and circulating HO-1 levels in high-altitude pregnancies. Although the hemotrichorial structure of mouse and rat placentas differs from the human hemomonochorial one, rodents are often used as models for studying the effects of hypoxic exposure on placental structure at different times and stages of pregnancy. Such studies generally show that late-gestation hypoxia reduces fetal weight by 20–25% provokes structural changes as well as placental vascular adaptations ( 371 , 372 ). Mid-term (days 11–16) hypoxia has also been shown to increase in the area of the placental labyrinth zone (LZ), which is responsible for maternal-fetal nutrient and gas exchange ( 373 ), using a mouse model of moderate hypoxia (13–14% O 2 inhalation) ( 64 ). The same study also found decreased LZ volume after acute exposure to more severe (10–10.5% O 2 ) late-gestation (days 14–19) hypoxia ( 64 ), whereas slightly less severe hypoxia (11–12% maternal F i O 2 ) in late gestation (days 15–21) did not affect LZ volume in rats ( 374 ). On the other hand, maternal exposure to severe hypoxia during mid-late pregnancy (28–30 days to term) in guinea pigs ( 68 ) or late pregnancy (days 14–19) in mice ( 64 ) expands the placental JZ, which constitutes the primary endocrine compartment of the rodent placenta and secretes hormones, growth factors, and cytokines that regulate maternal physiology and promote fetal development ( 375 ). Furthermore, several studies have demonstrated a consistent increase in the JZ:LZ ratio (reduced capacity for maternal-fetal exchange) with exposure to different levels of hypoxia at different stages of pregnancy, as reviewed elsewhere ( 376 ). However, those differences were not observed in studies using 13–14% hypoxia (days 6–20 in rats or 11–16 and 14–19 in mice) ( 64 , 275 , 377 ). In addition, early-onset gestational hypoxia (13–14% maternal F i O 2 , days 6–20) increased placental volume and the fetal capillary surface area, compensatory responses to cushion the suppressive effect of hypoxia on fetal growth ( 275 ). These data further support that observed changes in placental structure are dependent not only on the magnitude of hypoxia but also on the timing of the onset of hypoxia. The deer mouse ( Peromyscus maniculatus ), a rodent that lives at a wide range of altitudes throughout North America, has been studied as a model of the evolutionary adaptations to high altitude ( 378 ). Recent studies suggest that the maintenance of fetal growth at high altitudes in highland-adapted deer mice results from a coordinated set of genomic and structural placental adaptations, particularly a moderate expansion of the LZ ( 110 ). Specifically, exposure to moderate hypoxia in lowland animals increased placental size and expanded the maternal blood spaces compared to lowland normoxic animals, suggesting adaptive responses ( 109 ). In highland animals, such chronic hypoxia-dependent increases were even more pronounced and accompanied by an expanded surface area for gas exchange and other adaptive changes in the fetal vasculature, effects similar to those seen in adapted indigenous human high-altitude populations ( 109 ). These findings highlight the combined influence of O 2 availability and ancestry on placental adaptations. In large animal models such as sheep, exposure to high altitude during pregnancy also induces structural changes in the placenta. The total cotyledonary surface area and total vascular area of high-altitude ewes are increased, driven mainly by VEGF and, in turn, enlarging the surface area for the transfer of O 2 and nutrients between mother and fetus ( 379 ). Furthermore, chronic hypoxia alters placentome morphology by increasing the proportion of convex (C/D-type) placentomes relative to concave (A/B-type) ones ( 380 ). This has been suggested to be a marker of the functional optimization of the maternal-fetal exchange, although the number of placentomes was lower in sheep at high altitude ( 380 , 381 ). The evidence presented shows that the placenta can respond to O 2 deprivation, ultimately protecting fetal growth. Mainly, structural changes seek to optimize blood flow between the mother and fetus by increasing the angiogenic response while simultaneously improving the spatial distribution of the placenta and increasing the contact surface in the intervillous spaces, thereby improving gaseous exchange. Animal models demonstrate that hypoxia induces zone-specific adaptations that depend on the severity and timing of exposure and confirm that placental remodeling and vascular expansion help protect against FGR. Together, these findings highlight the placenta's phenotypic plasticity and structural adaptation to varying O 2 levels, providing important insights into human pregnancy adaptations under hypoxic conditions. 4.3. PLACENTAL METABOLIC ADAPTATION 4.3.1. MITOCHONDRIAL RESPIRATION AND OXYGEN CONSUMPTION The placenta’s rapid growth and multiple functions, such as gas and nutrient exchange and the production of several hormones, make it a highly metabolically active organ ( 382 ) and therefore critically dependent on mitochondrial activity. At term, placentas contain two mitochondrial subpopulations with distinct morphologies, distributed across various cell types, each fulfilling specialized roles. In the STB, mitochondria are smaller, more fragmented, and exhibit lower oxidative phosphorylation capacity, whereas the CTB displays higher oxidative capacity ( 383 , 384 ). In vitro experiments using cultured STB and CTB, as well as living explants, have demonstrated that the CTB possesses higher mitochondrial content and respiratory rates than the STB ( 385 ). Evidence from mouse models indicates that LZ and JZ mitochondria modulate their respiration rates, substrate utilization, biogenesis, and density in distinct ways, supporting efficient nutrient transfer and hormone production during fetal growth ( 386 ). Mouse placentas also exhibit a steady increase in mitochondrial number per cell during pregnancy, although this is accompanied by a mild decline in per-mitochondrion respiratory activity near term ( 387 ). This temporal increase in placental mitochondrial content is similar to that observed in humans ( 388 ). An important placental adaptation for optimizing fetal O 2 delivery under hypoxic conditions is a reduction of placental O 2 consumption. In high-altitude gestation, the placenta may undergo metabolic reprogramming, resulting in a hypometabolic phenotype that optimizes intracellular O 2 utilization and increases O 2 bioavailability ( 389 ). Studies using a sheep model of late-gestation maternal hypoxemia demonstrated reduced placental succinate dehydrogenase (complex II) and increased expression of the hypoxia-sensitive cytochrome c oxidase subunit 4I2, indicating remodeling of the mitochondrial electron transport chain to maintain ATP production efficiency ( 390 ). A recent study performed in sheep exposed to chronic hypoxia during the last third of pregnancy also showed increased levels of complexes II and IV compared to normoxic placentas ( 391 ). Pregnant mice exposed to 13% O 2 showed decreased mitochondrial respiration in LZ trophoblasts, without affecting fetal growth ( 386 ). This suggests that other compensatory mechanisms may be present to optimize fetal growth. Recent work shows that lower mitochondrial respiration rates were positively associated with umbilical venous PO 2 in healthy, highland Andean pregnancies with AGA birth weight infants, whereas this association was absent in pregnancies complicated by preeclampsia ( 60 ). In summary, these placental complex-specific adjustments appear to facilitate ATP generation while minimizing O 2 consumption, thus sparing O 2 transfer to the fetus. HIF-1 regulates mitochondrial respiration by inducing pyruvate dehydrogenase kinase, which inhibits pyruvate dehydrogenase and consequently decreases flux into the tricarboxylic acid cycle and oxidative phosphorylation ( 392 ). This metabolic shift compels cells to depend more on HIF-induced glycolysis for ATP production, thereby reducing O 2 consumption ( 393 , 394 ). Furthermore, HIF signaling can promote a subunit switch in cytochrome c oxidase, enhancing electron transfer efficiency under low-O 2 conditions and minimizing electron leakage, thereby limiting ROS production ( 395 ). Placental samples from women living at high altitudes exhibit a metabolic shift toward enhanced glycolysis, marked by reduced O 2 consumption and suppressed mitochondrial respiration, reflecting diminished oxidative phosphorylation capacity and lower protein levels of electron transport chain complexes I and IV and ATP synthase under hypoxic conditions ( 396 ). Similar results were observed in JEG-3 trophoblast-like cells and placental fibroblasts exposed to hypoxia showing suppressed respiration linked to complexes I and IV. These changes in mitochondrial function appear to be regulated at the post-transcriptional level, since the gene expression of most mitochondrial complexes remained unchanged ( 397 ). The microRNA miR-210 may regulate the placental response to hypoxia. miR-210 is upregulated in high-altitude human placentas, targeting proteins and assembly factors, such as the iron-sulfur cluster assembly enzyme 1/2, which are necessary for the function of many mitochondrial complexes and electron transport chain subunits ( 397 ). Increased miR-210 expression in JEG-3 cells and fibroblasts leads to downregulation of these factors, resulting in decreased levels of complex I and IV and, consequently, reduced electron transport efficiency ( 398 , 399 ). miR-210 overexpression disrupts normal trophoblast invasion, whereas miR-210 inhibition promotes it ( 400 ). miR-210 has also been proposed as a clinical biomarker of preeclampsia, as its levels are higher in preeclamptic maternal serum ( 400 ) and in placentas from severe, but not mild, preeclamptic women ( 401 ). Notably, experimental manipulation of miR-210 in mice has demonstrated that the ablation of miR-210 in the fetus and placenta did not reduce fetal growth under physiological conditions, but it conferred protection from hypoxia-dependent reduction in fetal growth ( 402 ). Overall, these findings highlight the role of miR-210 as an O 2 -sparing regulator and emerge as a key target in the regulation of metabolism and placental function in response to hypoxia. 4.3.2. LIPID METABOLISM Pregnancy at high altitude also alters placental lipid metabolism, reflecting adaptive responses to chronic hypoxia. Metabolomic analyses of placentas from high-altitude environments (3,100 m) have demonstrated altered lipid composition compared to those from sea level ( 403 ). Notably, the ratio of polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFA) to monounsaturated fatty acids increases ( 403 ). Because PUFAs are preferred as a peroxidation substrate, this could lead to an increase in lipid peroxidation, which may represent a potential adaptive mechanism ( 404 ). However, in the high-altitude placenta, lipid peroxidation (also a marker of oxidative stress) is decreased when compared to low-altitude placenta, further suggesting that decreased placental antioxidant activity is not associated with increased oxidative stress ( 405 ). Decreased lipid peroxidation in high-altitude placentas may represent a protective mechanism avoiding oxidative stress and lipotoxicity, as a consequence of the fine regulation of PUFA trafficking ( 406 ). However, decreased placental antioxidant capacity linked with increased biological oxidation has been shown in preeclamptic pregnancies ( 407 ), which in turn is associated with the progression of the disease. At the cellular level, hypoxic trophoblasts exhibit metabolic shifts that favor lipid storage, leading to lipid droplet accumulation due to decreased fatty acid oxidation ( 408 ). This is linked to increased glycolysis and reduced mitochondrial beta-oxidation ( 409 , 410 ). This strategy may help to prevent placental lipotoxicity resulting from impaired fatty acid oxidation, as observed in Sherpa populations ( 411 ). Consistent with this, high-altitude populations have suppression of fatty acid oxidation pathways, likely to optimize O 2 utilization for ATP production ( 412 ). In Andean highlanders, the CPT2 gene, which encodes carnitine palmitoyl-transferase 2, a critical protein for mitochondrial fatty acid import ( 413 ), has been identified as a potential factor in protecting mitochondrial respiration processes in normotensive pregnancies compared to preeclamptic pregnancies at high altitude ( 60 ). High-altitude preeclampsia also increases the accumulation of fatty acid derivatives (i.e., medium and long-chain acylcarnitines) in the placenta, further suggesting an impaired fatty acid oxidation ( 414 ). Additional mechanistic studies are required to elucidate the specific adaptive strategies employed by the placenta at high altitude. 4.3.3. GLUCOSE METABOLISM As oxidative phosphorylation is decreased, the high-altitude placenta relies more on glycolysis as an energy source. One consequence of this hypoxic metabolic shift is increased glucose utilization by the placenta and concomitant reduction in glucose transfer to the fetus ( 389 ). A study conducted in Bolivia reported a marked decrease in glucose delivery in high-altitude (3,600 m) vs. low-altitude (400 m) pregnancies, resulting from lower arterial and umbilical vein glucose concentrations ( 415 ). Interestingly, another study comparing placentas from high (2,849 ± 230 m) and moderate (&lt;1,700 m) altitudes in Colorado showed no significant differences in the abundance of the glucose transporters GLUT1 in STB microvillous membrane (facing the maternal blood) nor GLUT1 and GLUT4 in the STB basal membrane (facing the fetal capillaries) ( 131 ), suggesting that glucose transporters do not mediate decreased glucose delivery. Fetal glucose use is regulated in relation to the placenta’s own glucose requirements, with placental glucose consumption playing a central role in controlling how much glucose is transferred from mother to fetus ( 416 ). In mice exposed to hypoxic conditions (15% O 2 ) during the whole pregnancy, greater glucose use by maternal tissues may reduce the amount of glucose available to the fetus ( 417 ). Accordingly, human fetuses at high altitude showed reduced glucose consumption associated with reduced fetal glucose delivery and increased fetal lactate levels ( 415 ). Therefore, this evidence supports the rationale that the placenta oxidizes more glucose at high altitude, resulting in less glucose available to support fetal growth. Of interest, lower circulating glucose levels are present even in non-pregnant women at high altitude, suggesting that the effects of chronic hypoxia on glucose metabolism during pregnancy are a continuation of those already present before conception ( 418 ). 4.3.4. AMINO ACID METABOLISM Amino acids are vital for fetal growth, serving as the primary substrates for protein synthesis and critical biosynthetic pathways. The human placenta actively transports amino acids against concentration gradients from mother to fetus, using a set of transporter systems on the STB membranes ( 419 ). These include system A transporters, sodium-coupled neutral amino acid transporters (SNAT) 1, 2 and 4, encoded by SLC38A1 , SLC38A2 , and SLC38A4 , respectively ( 420 – 422 ), which primarily uptake small neutral amino acids (e.g., alanine, glycine) from maternal blood ( 423 – 425 ), and system L transporters, large neutral amino acid transporter (LAT) 1 and 2, encoded by SLC7A5 and SLC7A8 , respectively, which facilitate exchange of large neutral amino acids (e.g., leucine, phenylalanine) into the fetal circulation ( 426 ). The placenta also synthesizes a variety of proteins (structural proteins, enzymes, transporters, and peptide hormones) and can interconvert amino acids ( 427 ). Protein synthesis is one of the most energy-demanding processes in the placenta and is estimated to account for roughly 30% of placental O 2 consumption at sea level ( 382 ). Nutrient transport mechanisms are also modulated by hypoxia, which could be impairing transporter function. In vitro studies in cultured term human trophoblasts exposed to severe hypoxia (~3% O 2 , 24 h) show significantly reduced amino acid transport through system A, which is accompanied by a decrease in the expression of SNAT1/SNAT2 transporters ( 428 ). Similarly, placentas from mice exposed to hypoxia (10% O 2 ) during pregnancy (days 14–19) have decreased amino acid transport system A activity ( 64 ). Notably, the effect in mice is sex-dependent, as it was observed only in the placentas of female fetuses ( 63 ). Furthermore, a transcriptomic analysis of placentas from mice exposed to hypoxia during late gestation showed that genes encoding placental nutrient transport pathways were among the most downregulated ( 429 ). These changes were associated with placental insufficiency (PI) outcomes, including FGR. However, in healthy pregnant women residing at high altitude, there was no change in nutrient transport capacity of the microvillous membrane, despite reduced fetal weight ( 131 ). Moreover, these high-altitude placentas showed increased EPO receptor expression ( 131 ), a marker of hypoxic response ( 430 ), confirming that the placenta can detect low O 2 levels. This suggests that the reduction in fetal growth was not due to an intrinsic failure of placental nutrient transport. 4.3.5. SIGNALING PATHWAYS mTOR is a protein that controls cell growth in response to changes in nutrient levels and growth factor signals. mTOR is activated by essential amino acids and its inhibition induces the downregulation of systems A and L of amino acid transport in the trophoblast ( 431 , 432 ). Evidence from animal models indicates that hypoxia affects the mTOR pathway in the placenta. Specifically, pregnant rats exposed to 9% O 2 for 4 days showed decreased mTOR activation in the placenta, as measured by the fraction of phosphorylated mTOR, associated with decreased placental and fetal weight ( 433 ). Furthermore, placental mTOR signaling is lower in FGR pregnancies and correlates with decreased gene expression of lipid transport and metabolism genes ( 434 ). mTOR signaling is negatively regulated by the activation of the AMPK enzyme complex ( 435 ), which consists of alpha catalytic subunits and non-catalytic beta and gamma subunits, and becomes active under cellular stress conditions such as low energy availability or hypoxia ( 436 , 437 ). AMPK is activated by kinases, including liver kinase B1 and calmodulin-dependent kinase kinase β ( 438 ), and subsequently stimulates cellular energy restoration pathways, such as PPARγ coactivator 1-alpha ( 439 ) and acetyl-CoA carboxylase ( 440 ). Furthermore, AMPK activation inhibits the mTOR-associated regulatory protein Raptor and, through phosphorylation, activates tuberin, which subsequently inhibits mTOR ( 441 ). The role of AMPK activators as a potential intervention for high-altitude pregnancies is discussed in section 9.3.4 . Studies on human placentas from high-altitude AGA pregnancies suggested that mTOR inhibition, accompanied by increased levels of phosphorylated eukaryotic translation initiation factor 4E binding protein 1 (4E-BP1) ( 442 ), a known regulator of protein synthesis that is typically repressed by mTOR ( 443 ), represents a mechanism by which the placenta maintains protein production close to normal or physiological levels. This adaptation might help to preserve fetal growth in high-altitude pregnancies, resulting in newborns with AGA weight. Insulin-like growth factors (IGFs) primarily signal through the IGF1 receptor, activating the mitogen-activated protein kinase, MAPK, and phosphatidylinositol-3 kinase/protein kinase B (PI3K/Akt) pathways that regulate trophoblast proliferation, survival, migration, and invasion ( 444 ). These pathways also modulate the expression and activity of placental nutrient transporters ( 445 , 446 ). Consequently, adaptations within the IGF system are necessary to sustain fetal growth under hypoxic conditions. In high-altitude populations in Peru (4,300 m), maternal serum levels of IGF-binding protein 1 (IGFBP-1), which binds to IGF1/2 and reduces their bioavailability ( 447 ), are elevated compared to sea-level pregnancies, and this elevation persists into late pregnancy ( 448 ). Increased IGFBP-1 concentrations can disrupt IGF signaling and impair fetal growth, as elevated umbilical cord IGFBP-1 concentrations are associated with infants classified as small for gestational age ( 449 , 450 ). In mouse studies, deletion of Igf2 (the gene encoding IGF2) results in pronounced placental growth restriction and impaired nutrient transport, and partial Igf2 deficiency also reduces placental size and function, ultimately resulting in smaller fetuses ( 451 , 452 ). Interestingly, a decrease in IGF2 transcript levels was observed in the placenta of (altitude-adapted) Tibetan highlanders compared to non-adapted or sea-level populations ( 9 ). These findings suggest an increased placental glucose consumption during fetal development, as evidenced by higher birth weight in the Tibetan population compared with other populations residing at the same altitude ( 41 , 453 ). A rigorous experimental approach to elucidate the role of IGF2 in adapted populations may also help address existing discrepancies by providing more detailed mechanistic insight. This could involve directly estimating placental glucose consumption using Fick’s principle, through measurements of maternal (arterial and uterine vein) and fetal (umbilical artery and vein) glucose concentrations, together with assessments of uterine and umbilical blood flow. These physiological parameters could then be examined in relation to molecular changes, such as alterations in placental glucose transporter expression and circulating levels of IGFBP1 and IGF, to support a more integrated interpretation. Although current evidence indicates that adaptations of the IGF system are an essential mechanism supporting human fetal growth at high altitude, the specific regulatory pathways and their interactions with other metabolic networks remain unknown. Potential therapeutic interventions targeting IGF are discussed in section 9.3.3 . Combined, the literature suggests that the placenta adjusts its metabolism to support fetal development under conditions of limited O 2 and nutrient availability ( Figure 10 ). It alters mitochondrial function by shifting from oxidative phosphorylation to glycolysis and engages regulatory systems such as HIF signaling and post-transcriptional control. Lipid and glucose processing adapt to improve energy and O 2 efficiency, while nutrient-sensing pathways like mTOR and IGF signaling sustain amino acid transport and protein synthesis. These strategies maintain placental function and fetal survival under chronic hypoxic conditions, although they may restrict growth. Understanding these adaptations enhances our knowledge of placental function and may inform improved treatment strategies for pregnancies affected by hypoxia. 5. FETAL ADAPTATIONS TO HIGH ALTITUDE Fetal hypoxia, a leading cause of perinatal morbidity and mortality, arises from impaired O 2 delivery to the fetus ( 454 ). There are several causes of fetal hypoxia, involving maternal, placental or fetal origins. One cause is the lower PO 2 at high altitude ( 455 ), where pregnancy complications are a public health problem. Although high altitude is conventionally defined as elevations above 2,500 m ( 30 , 456 – 459 ), physiological responses may occur at elevations beginning at 1,500 m ( 7 , 460 – 462 ). Chronic hypobaric hypoxia is a profound physiological challenge to the developing fetus ( 458 , 463 , 464 ); therefore, a complex physiological and cellular response over the course of gestation must occur to ensure fetal survival. These compensatory responses include a decrease in fetal growth and altered fetal development, impacting the structure and function of several organs and systems ( Figure 11 ). 5.1. FETAL HYPOXIA The fetal defense response to hypoxia, decreasing fetal P a O 2 from normal basal levels of ~25 mmHg to values of 12–15 mmHg, ranges from immediate chemoreflex adaptations aimed at preserving O 2 supply to vital organs to slower, developmental adjustments that can permanently alter the trajectory of organ growth and function ( 454 , 465 ). The most well-documented consequence of hypoxic pregnancy is FGR, but the impact extends far beyond lowering birth weight, as chronic fetal hypoxia can affect the fetal cardiovascular, cerebrovascular, and endocrine systems and persist throughout the lifespan by increasing susceptibility to adult cardiovascular and other diseases ( 458 , 466 , 467 ). Several studies have measured maternal arterialized PO 2 at high altitude ( 162 , 177 , 304 , 468 ). Due to obvious ethical and methodological considerations, few studies have assessed human fetal PO 2 and therefore most of the fetal responses to high-altitude hypobaric hypoxia have been documented in animal models. 5.2. FETAL GROWTH Epidemiological studies worldwide have firmly established that birth weight decreases with increasing altitude ( 469 ). This has been documented in the Andean Altiplano in South America ( 32 , 37 , 470 – 473 ), the Himalayas in Asia ( 258 , 474 , 475 ), and the Rocky Mountains in North America ( 36 , 455 , 476 ). It has been estimated that for every 1,000 m of elevation gain, fetal weight decreases approximately 100 g. For instance, in Peru, above 2,000 m, birth weight decreases an average of 65 g for every additional 500 m of altitude ( 470 ). Other studies have estimated that birth weight decreases by a clinically meaningful amount, ranging from 54.7 g to approximately 96.9 g for every 1,000 m increase in elevation above sea level ( 469 , 473 ). This decrease in birth weight appears to be independent of socioeconomic status ( 37 , 477 ). Furthermore, after controlling for potential confounders, such as number of previous pregnancies, maternal age at delivery, pre-pregnancy body mass index, etc. there is an even greater decrease in birth weight with altitude ( 8 , 476 ). The high-altitude-dependent reduction in birth weight is primarily attributed to FGR, as average gestational age at delivery is typically not significantly different between high-altitude and sea-level cohorts, suggesting that the primary insult is chronic intrauterine constraint rather than premature delivery ( 472 , 473 , 478 ). A distinguishing feature of high-altitude-related FGR is asymmetric growth, characteristic of chronic intrauterine compromise or chronic hypoxia. Antenatal ultrasound studies conducted across diverse high-altitude populations, such as those in the Andean highlands (4,300 m), provide critical biometric insight into the nature of this asymmetric growth restriction. For instance, biparietal diameter, head circumference (HC), abdominal circumference (AC), and femur length (FL) exhibit reduced growth trajectories compared to sea-level counterparts, comparisons which are statistically significant from approximately 25 to 29 weeks of gestation onward ( 479 ). The restriction is substantially greater in the AC than in the HC or FL ( 479 ). This disparity, where the AC/HC ratio is reduced, reflects a fetal adaptive strategy involving the preferential redistribution of cardiac output (brain sparing), whereby blood flow is shunted to essential organs, specifically the brain, heart, and adrenal glands, preserving their growth at the expense of somatic and liver growth in the face of sustained O 2 and nutrient deprivation - the so called fetal brain sparing effect ( 454 ). Maternal and paternal ancestry influence the magnitude of altitudinal FGR ( 30 , 32 , 33 , 37 , 41 , 463 , 478 , 480 ). Multi-generational populations residing at high altitude, such as Andeans and Tibetans, exhibit evolutionary adaptations that confer substantial protection against FGR ( Figure 7B ) and other adverse outcomes. Overall, highland ancestry is estimated to decrease the altitude-associated reduction in birth weight by approximately half ( 478 , 481 – 484 ). Because uteroplacental blood flow is a primary determinant of fetoplacental O 2 supply, lower uterine artery blood flow observed at high altitude likely accounts for some of the altitude-associated reductions in birth weight. In support of this hypothesis, the significant altitude-associated reduction in fetal growth in Europeans compared to Andeans at high altitude occurs alongside reduced uterine artery blood flow and O 2 delivery by 20 weeks, well before the onset of altitude-associated reductions in fetal growth ( 485 ). Similarly, non-altitude-adapted Han Chinese women demonstrate lower uterine artery blood flow velocity and comparatively less redistribution of pelvic flow to favor the uterine artery during the third trimester than altitude-adapted Tibetans at 3,600 m ( 189 ). However, slowed fetal growth at high altitude cannot simply be due to reduced uterine artery blood flow since even the lower blood flows observed are sufficient to maintain uteroplacental O 2 delivery at near sea-level values ( 179 , 486 , 487 ). Furthermore, near-term high-altitude fetuses extract sufficient O 2 to support O 2 consumption rates equivalent to those observed at low altitudes ( 304 ). Thus, while maternal arterial oxygenation and the pregnancy-associated rise in uterine artery blood flow are important determinants of birth weight at high altitudes, neither uteroplacental blood flow nor O 2 delivery can be solely responsible for altitude-associated reductions in fetal growth. It has therefore been proposed that hypoxia is likely a trigger, rather than a direct cause of the growth reduction ( 291 ). The Andean advantage is likely attributed to genetically mediated differences in not only vascular function but also placental efficiency in both O 2 and nutrient transport, alongside greater fetal efficiency in substrate utilization ( 483 ). Genetic research supports this, identifying variation in genes regulating hypoxic response and metabolic homeostasis (e.g., EPAS1 and PRKAA1 ), which may modulate vascular and placental function to preserve growth ( 58 , 360 , 482 , 488 ). These adaptive strategies, often manifested as increased uteroplacental blood flow and enhanced maternal antioxidant capacity, protect placental bioenergetic function and nutrient transport capacity ( 458 , 478 , 489 ). In Leh, India (elevation 3,524 m), women who self-identified as Tibetan delivered infants with higher birth weights than women of Ladahki ancestry living at the same altitude, the latter group residing at high altitude for five or more generations ( 52 ), suggesting that longer generational exposures to high altitude provide better protection from reduced birth weight. In the same study, Tibetan, Ladahki, and Nepali highlanders living in Leh collectively exhibited higher birth weights and larger uterine artery diameters between 18 and 24 weeks of gestation compared to predominantly Indo-Aryan Indian women residing at low altitude (New Dehli, 216 m) ( 52 ). Although genome-wide association studies in Ladakhis did not identify associations between SNP and birth weight that remained significant after correction for multiple testing, seven variants in five genes ( CDKAL1 , HMGA2 , PLCG1 , ZBTB38 , and ZFP36L2 ) showed nominal associations with birth weight in this population ( 59 ). Studies exposing the chicken embryo model to incubation at high altitude have revealed that when fertilized eggs laid by sea-level hens were incubated at high altitude, this led to growth restriction ( 490 ), thereby resembling the adverse effect of high altitude on birth weight in human babies from European mothers undergoing pregnancy in La Paz, Bolivia ( 32 , 491 ). When eggs laid by highland hens were incubated at high altitude, they also showed significant growth restriction. However, these embryos were significantly heavier than embryos from eggs laid by sea-level hens incubated in La Paz ( 490 ). This resembles the protective effect on birth weight in human babies from mothers of Andean ancestry undergoing pregnancy at high altitude ( 32 , 491 ). Fertilized eggs laid by sea-level hens incubated at high altitude with O 2 supplementation to equate sea level PO 2 , prevented the restrictive effect of highland incubation on embryo weight ( 490 ). This group highlights that the detrimental effect of high altitude slowing fetal growth is due to the hypoxia, rather than the hypobaria, of life at high altitude. Combined, these studies in the chicken embryo unveil a direct role for O 2 in the regulation of fetal growth ( 490 ). The studies emphasize that altitude and highland ancestry must affect fetal growth and development by direct mechanisms on the fetus, in addition to maternal or placental effects or reductions in uterine artery size and blood flow ( 491 ). The nature of these direct effects of high-altitude hypoxia on the fetus is likely epigenetic, although they remain understudied. Other factors present at high altitude, such as colder temperatures, have received less attention. Exposure to cold temperatures during pregnancy increases the risk of preterm birth and reduces birth weight, as shown by a meta-analysis ( 492 ). Interestingly, other studies analyzed by the same meta-analysis show no effect of cold exposure on small-for-gestational-age rates, whereas high altitude does ( 8 , 493 ). Thus, whether there is a combined effect of cold temperatures and high altitude, or of chronic hypoxia, remains unclear. 5.3. FETAL DEVELOPMENT 5.3.1. CARDIAC PROGRAMMING The heart is the first organ to develop and function, as fetal development requires a constant supply of nutrients and O 2 . Prolonged O 2 deprivation after the first trimester of gestation triggers widespread modifications in cardiac structure and function, with extensive consequences for cardiovascular health throughout life ( 494 ). During fetal development, these adaptations primarily serve to safeguard the O 2 and nutrient supply to critical organs, such as the brain and heart. Although these compensatory responses optimize survival in utero , such fetal adaptations lead to lasting morphological and functional changes to the heart and central vasculature that extend well beyond the perinatal period ( 495 , 496 ). Hypoxia during fetal development profoundly disrupts the normal transition of cardiac cell maturation, forcing a premature shift from proliferative, mononucleated cardiomyocytes to mature, non-proliferative binucleated cardiomyocytes, a change that permanently alters cardiac reserve and regenerative capacity throughout life ( 497 ). Experimental evidence demonstrates this programming across multiple species. Maternal hypoxia increased both the proportion and size of binucleated myocytes in the fetal rat heart ( 498 ), while chronic hypoxia enhances binucleation in isolated fetal cardiomyocytes ( 499 ) and suppresses cardiomyocyte proliferation ex vivo ( 500 ). In addition, ovine studies using FGR models show that hypoxia results in fewer, albeit larger, cardiomyocytes ( 501 ). This cellular reprogramming carries profound implications because the window for cardiac cell proliferation is exceptionally narrow, occurring primarily during fetal development, with the vast majority of lifelong cardiomyocyte endowment established before and shortly after birth ( 502 ). Human studies corroborate these experimental findings, showing that intrauterine hypoxia results in a distinct reduction in cardiomyocyte volume ( 503 ) and persistent abnormalities in both cardiac contractility and relaxation ( 467 , 504 ). Heart failure affects approximately 64 million people worldwide annually and remains a leading cause of mortality ( 505 ). A central feature of heart failure is cardiac fibrosis, induced by excessive collagen deposition in the myocardial extracellular matrix ( 506 , 507 ). Excess collagen accumulation increases myocardial stiffness, diminishes elasticity, and leads to impaired diastolic function ( 508 , 509 ). Hence, excess fetal cardiac collagen accumulation may influence the risk of heart failure in later life via extracellular matrix remodeling. Gestational hypoxia fundamentally alters the structural scaffolding of the developing heart through enhanced collagen deposition. In fetal sheep hearts, gestational hypoxia increased collagen content ( 510 ). In contrast, maternal hypoxia in rats preferentially augmented collagen in neonatal (rather than fetal) hearts ( 511 ), with comparatively greater collagen type I expression and accumulation evident in adult offspring exposed to prenatal hypoxia ( 511 ). This predominance of rigid collagen type I ( 512 ), which is highly correlated with ventricular stiffness ( 513 ) and ventricular dysfunction ( 514 ), may represent maladaptive remodeling that compromises cardiac function beyond the fetal period. Ovine chronic hypoxia models or placental growth restriction models that closely parallel human FGR demonstrate similar patterns, with FGR inducing significant increases in vascular wall collagen content ( 515 ) and fetal cardiovascular dysfunction ( 516 ), thereby establishing a foundation for lifelong cardiovascular dysfunction. High-altitude development is a recognized factor associated with an increased prevalence of congenital heart disease (CHD) in populations living at high altitudes ( 491 , 517 ). A study performed in 84,000 children in the Nagqu area, Tibet (4,500 m), showed a marked increase in CHD, with patent ductus arteriosus (PDA) (66%), atrial septal defect (20%), and ventricular septal defect (9%) as the most common problems ( 518 ). The increased CHD suggests that chronic hypoxia during the highly vulnerable phase of cardiac organogenesis (gestational weeks 3–8) ( 519 ) directly interferes with the complex signaling and cellular migration necessary for proper cardiac septation. Myocardial formation begins with the fusion of cardiac progenitor cells from the primary and secondary heart fields, creating the primitive heart tube, which then undergoes looping, trabeculation, and the establishment of coronary vasculature ( 520 ). Hypoxia serves as a key signal mediating angioblast invasion and coronary vessel formation, ( 521 ) and functions as a fundamental regulator of cardiac development ( 491 , 522 ), apoptosis ( 523 , 524 ), and cardiomyocyte maturation, driving the transition from a proliferative, mononucleated fetal state to a terminally differentiated, often binucleated adult state with limited regenerative capacity ( 497 , 525 ). Chronic fetal hypoxia has also been shown to cause abnormal heart development, including myocardial thinning, ventricular septal defects, cardiomyocyte hypertrophy, and hypoplasia ( 491 , 498 , 526 ). These structural changes are accompanied by cardiac functional deficits, such as depressed contractility and altered heart rate, which can persist into adulthood ( 95 , 491 , 516 , 527 – 530 ). In addition, chronic hypoxia decreases cardiac mitochondrial DNA content and reduces the expression of the mitochondrial protein complexes, particularly in male fetuses ( 531 ). Another study functionally corroborates these observations by associating chronic hypoxia during pregnancy with a reduced fetal cardiac mitochondrial respiratory capacity and an increase in ROS production ( 532 ). The molecular mechanisms underlying hypoxia-driven cardiac development center primarily on HIF signaling ( 497 , 533 ). In mice, global knockout of Hif1a is embryonically lethal, causing severe cardiovascular defects, emphasizing its essential role ( 534 ). HIF-1α induces the transcription of pro-survival genes, including VEGF, EPO, and glycolytic genes, during mild hypoxia, while upregulating pro-apoptotic genes such as BNIP3 (encoding B-cell lymphoma 2 [BCL2] interacting protein 3) and BAX (encoding BCL2-associated X protein) under conditions of severe hypoxia or elevated ROS ( 497 ). However, the effects of chronic hypoxia on cardiac apoptotic cell levels are controversial. While fetal sheep under hypoxic conditions showed no changes in myocardial apoptosis ( 535 ), fetal rats exposed to hypoxia presented augmented apoptosis, mediated by an increase in apoptosis antigen 1 (a.k.a., Fas cell surface death receptor) and a decrease in BCL2 proteins ( 498 ). This difference may be attributed to a species-specific response, time of onset during gestation and/or to the severity of hypoxia (sheep at 3,800 m, approximately 13% O 2 ; rats at 10.5% O 2 ). Therefore, the cardiac functional deficits mentioned above may not only be due to apoptosis, but also to chronic load-induced hypertrophy, fibrosis, or unresolved mitochondrial or metabolic dysfunction within the stressed myocardium. Beyond structural congenital defects, chronic hypoxia induces physiological remodeling within the fetal heart itself. Chicken embryo studies support the hypothesis that high altitude directly affects prenatal growth and the developmental origins of heart disease via mechanisms that may be acting purely on the fetus, independent of the maternal or placental circulatory adaptations ( 458 , 491 , 536 ). For instance, development of chicken embryos in isobaric hypoxic incubators ( 530 , 537 , 538 ) or at high altitude promotes significant cardiac stress and aortic wall remodeling ( 114 , 539 ). Evidence of structural adaptation is demonstrated by the persistence of elevated right ventricular weight in human neonates born at high altitude ( 536 , 540 ). The weight of the right ventricle is proportional to the pulmonary artery pressure; thus, the maintained right ventricular weight observed postnatally suggests that the fetal right ventricle developed substantial structural hypertrophy in utero . This hypertrophy is a direct response to the ventricular functional demands with a chronically increased pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR) that characterizes the hypoxic environment ( 541 ), suggesting that the chronic pressure overload during fetal life causes permanent, prenatal structural remodeling of the heart. Using the ovine model of hypoxic pregnancy in isobaric chambers, a recent report showed that progressive fetal hypoxia leads to profound changes in fetal cardiac structure and function, resulting from a switch to left ventricular (LV) dominance triggered by fetal brain sparing ( 516 ). These findings also indicate that fetal cardiac compensatory reserves become exhausted with progressive hypoxia. In addition, chronic hypoxia during pregnancy in humans, sheep, and rodents is associated with a globular shape of the heart, cardiac hypertrophy and cardiomegaly by the end of gestation affecting cardiac performance ( 497 , 516 , 542 ). Lowland chicken embryos incubated at high altitudes (3,600 m) develop significant cardiac abnormalities, including greater cardiac mass and biventricular hypertrophy, with increased LV thickness ( 539 ). These structural changes are inversely correlated with both embryonic weight and PO 2 of the chorioallantoic artery, which is the avian equivalent of the umbilical artery in mammals ( 491 ). In contrast, embryos from highland-adapted hens exhibit resistance to hypoxia-associated cardiac remodeling when exposed to the same hypoxic conditions ( 539 ), suggesting evolutionary adaptation against altitude-associated cardiac remodeling ( 491 ). Cardiovascular disruptions initiated during embryonic development persist throughout the lifespan. Lowland chicken embryos incubated at high altitudes exhibit altered cardiac baroreflex sensitivity, systemic hypertension, reduced beta-adrenergic sensitivity, myocardial fibrosis, and cardiac hypertrophy as adults in some ( 543 – 545 ), but not all reports ( 546 , 547 ). Studies also demonstrate that adult birds exposed to controlled isobaric hypoxia during embryogenesis develop the same triad of hypertension, cardiovascular dysfunction, and pathological cardiac remodeling ( 467 , 545 , 548 ), establishing O 2 limitation as the primary mechanism underlying these developmental cardiovascular abnormalities. 5.3.2. FETAL HEMODYNAMIC RESPONSE The brain is one of the most O 2 -consuming fetal organs, after the placenta ( 549 – 551 ). As a result, fetal responses to chronic hypoxia involve prioritizing O 2 delivery to the brain through redistribution of the fetal cardiac output, a circulatory response known as fetal brain sparing ( 454 ). A recent study found low cerebroplacental ratio (CPR), measured via Doppler blood flow velocimetry, in human high-altitude pregnancy ( 552 ). Since a low CPR ratio has been observed even in high-altitude fetuses classified as AGA, such redistribution appears present even before FGR has developed. This demonstrates that the fetal brain sparing mechanism is highly sensitive to subtle changes in O 2 availability or metabolic state induced by chronic high-altitude exposure. The physiology underlying the fetal brain-sparing response is complex, integrating neural, endocrine, and local mechanisms ( 454 ). The immediate response is initiated by a carotid chemoreflex ( 465 ), inducing a peripheral vasoconstriction and bradycardia. Selective denervation of the carotid sinus nerves abolishes the bradycardia and delays the peripheral vasoconstriction, highlighting that these responses are triggered by carotid rather than aortic chemoreceptors ( 553 , 554 ). O 2 sensing in the fetal carotid body is critically linked to adenosine A 2A receptor activation ( 555 ). Treatment of fetal sheep with a selective adenosine A 2A receptor antagonist also abolishes the fetal bradycardia and peripheral vasoconstriction in response to acute hypoxia ( 556 , 557 ). Activation of the carotid chemoreflex increases vagal outflow to the heart, causing bradycardia ( 558 ), and sympathetic outflow to peripheral vascular beds, causing vasoconstriction ( 465 , 559 ). This neuronally-triggered vasoconstriction is maintained by the subsequent release of endocrine constrictors, including catecholamines, vasopressin, and neuropeptide Y ( 560 , 561 ). At a local level, peripheral vasoconstriction is modulated by a vascular oxidant tone ( 454 ), which is determined by the interaction between the vasodilator NO and ROS ( 562 ). This interaction decreases NO bioavailability and enhances local vasoconstriction. Interestingly, the brain-sparing response matures with advancing gestation, switching from an immature (tachycardia, weak vasoconstriction) to a mature phenotype (pronounced, sustained bradycardia, and intense femoral vasoconstriction) ( 454 , 563 ). Studies in sheep have shown that during high-altitude pregnancy, fetal sheep exhibit increased cardiovascular responses to chronic hypoxia, such as augmented blood flow in carotid and femoral arteries, but blunted responses to acute and intense hypoxic episodes ( 256 ). The hemodynamic response is characterized by lower mean systemic arterial pressure, higher heart rate, and altered regional blood flow, suggesting a shift toward O 2 -conserving circulatory strategies ( 256 , 564 ). Other studies in sheep have described an increased mean systemic arterial pressure and redistribution of fetal cardiac output favoring brain and heart circulations at high altitude (3,820 m) ( 565 , 566 ). These adaptations are supported by vascular bed-specific changes, such as decreased contractility in cerebral arteries but increased constrictor reactivity in femoral arteries isolated from fetal sheep ( 256 ). Further studies in ovine pregnancy exposed to periods of chronic hypoxia in isobaric chambers also provide evidence that fetal basal cardiovascular function and fetal cardiovascular defense responses to acute challenges are significantly altered. Another study reported that the hemodynamic response also redistributes substrate delivery away from the peripheral and towards the essential circulations, such as the brain, heart, and adrenal glands in the chronically hypoxic fetus ( 92 ). This resembles the fetal basal cardiovascular adaptive response in fetal sheep during high altitude pregnancy ( 256 , 564 ) and the human fetal response to highland pregnancy ( 552 ). Chronically hypoxic fetal sheep in isobaric chambers also showed markedly impaired vasomotor baroreflex function, with absent cardiac sympathetic responses and limited vasopressor responses to acute hypotension ( 567 ), and markedly altered autonomic control of heart variability ( 95 ). Long-term high-altitude hypoxemia (3,820 m) reduced both endothelial-dependent relaxations and smooth muscle contractile capacity in sheep fetal cerebral arteries, including reduced contractile responses to K + and decreased NO-dependent relaxation ( 568 ). Similarly, acute reductions in PO 2 (0% O 2 in the tissue bath for &gt;10 min) inhibited endothelium-dependent relaxation in guinea pig fetal carotid arteries, and relatively short periods of chronic hypoxia (12% O 2 , 4 days) reduced maximal acetylcholine-induced relaxation ( 569 ). Other studies in an FGR model in the guinea pig have reported preserved vascular reactivity and structure in the cerebral vasculature ( 570 – 572 ). One study indicates compensatory upregulation of vasodilator mechanisms, including increased contribution of NO and vasodilator prostaglandins, which in turn, serves to attenuate constrictor responses in chronically hypoxic fetuses ( 573 ). Similarly, microstructural changes, along with decreased wall thickness and increased luminal area, have been described in fetal carotid arteries of FGR sheep ( 574 ). Together, past studies support a model in which cerebrovascular function in the chronically hypoxic fetus is remodeled to maintain brain perfusion. Nonetheless, growing evidence supports the idea that this remodeled brain sparing in the chronically hypoxic fetus is not completely protective, with medium to long-term adverse sequalae ( 575 ). Coronary arteries also exhibit vascular adaptations in the chronically hypoxic fetus, with evidence of altered contractile responses and Ca 2+ handling. For instance, fetal coronary arteries from long-term hypoxic sheep had reduced L-type Ca 2+ channel density and diminished depolarization-induced contractions ( 576 ). In addition, fetal coronary branches from FGR sheep were more responsive to the vasoconstrictor angiotensin II and the thromboxane mimetic U46619, also displaying altered passive force-tension wall properties and compliance ( 501 ). These coronary changes are particularly significant because they may affect myocardial perfusion in periods of limited cardiac reserve in the chronically hypoxic fetus. Conversely, large elastic arteries, such as the thoracic aorta, show prominent structural remodeling in response to gestational hypoxia and FGR. Multiple experimental models document aortic wall thickening, altered extracellular matrix composition, and increased stiffness after chronic hypoxia or PI. For instance, increased wall thickness and wall-to-lumen ratio has been observed in the fetal aorta of chronically hypoxic rats, accompanied by impairment of constrictor and relaxant responsiveness ( 577 ). Similarly, biomechanical and active responses suggest an increased stiffness and contractile capacity ( 570 , 571 ), as well as impaired endothelial dependent relaxation ( 578 ) in FGR guinea pigs. Another study extended these observations in a sheep PI-FGR model, finding that segments of the thoracic and abdominal aorta stiffen through heterogeneous extracellular matrix reorganization, characterized by increased collagen, altered elastin, and reduced glycosaminoglycan content ( 579 ). Chicken embryos developed from eggs laid by sea-level hens incubated at high altitude also show pronounced aortic wall thickening ( 539 ), emphasizing a direct effect of chronic hypoxia on the fetal aortic vasculature. At the molecular level, oxidative stress markers and changes in endothelial NO signaling have been observed in aortic tissue from hypoxic fetuses, supporting ROS-mediated endothelial dysfunction as a central mechanism ( 580 , 581 ). Collectively, these findings support that chronic fetal hypoxia promotes aortic structural remodeling and functional impairment ( 582 ). Similarly, fetal peripheral muscular arteries and resistance vessels frequently show significant structural remodeling and a constrictor functional phenotype following chronic hypoxia. Several lines of evidence report increased contractile responsiveness, reduced NO-dependent relaxation, and accelerated vascular aging in peripheral vascular beds in the FGR guinea pig pregnancy ( 570 , 572 ). These alterations in peripheral vascular beds again align with preferential redistribution of blood flow towards the upper body to maintain fetal brain sparing in the chronically hypoxic fetus ( 571 ). Mechanistically, reduced NO bioavailability ( 581 ), coupled with altered eNOS expression/activation and changes in smooth muscle responses, underpins peripheral endothelial dysfunction and enhanced constrictor tone in the peripheral vasculature of the chronically hypoxic fetus ( 572 , 578 , 583 ). The effect of chronic hypoxia in the umbilical vascular bed is not well understood, with reports of varied responses in different animal models and in humans. Data from FGR sheep show microstructural changes indicating increased stiffness, but no macroscopic changes or remodeling of the umbilical arterial circulation ( 579 ). In contrast, studies in FGR guinea pigs suggest a decrease in the stiffness, reduced contractile responses ( 570 , 571 ) and decreased endothelial-dependent relaxation ( 578 ) in the umbilical arterial circulation, the latter effect also reported in umbilical arteries isolated from human infants from FGR pregnancy ( 584 , 585 ). Further studies are required to define how these changes in the umbilical circulation in the chronically hypoxic fetus affect fetal growth. 5.3.3. PULMONARY DEVELOPMENT AND MALADAPTATION Chronic high-altitude hypoxia also affects the development of the pulmonary vasculature, promoting thickening of the pulmonary artery media wall ( 541 , 586 , 587 ). This structural change, which occurs during fetal development, significantly increases the baseline PVR ( 588 ). The excessive prenatal thickening of the pulmonary media is correlated with failure of the circulatory fetal-to-neonatal transition at birth ( 12 , 586 , 589 ). While the fetus requires high PVR to shunt blood away from the unventilated lungs, the exaggerated thickening caused by chronic hypoxia prevents the rapid and sufficient relaxation of the pulmonary vasculature upon exposure to atmospheric O 2 after birth. The hypoxic pulmonary hypertension (PH) has been associated with activation of pulmonary artery Rho/Rho-kinase ( 590 ). In addition, remodeling of the pulmonary circulation is associated with increased expression and signaling of the epidermal growth factor receptor ( 591 ) and VEGF and its receptors ( 592 ). However, excessive activation switches into a structural constraint contributing to the development of persistent PH of the newborn (PPHN) ( 586 , 593 – 596 ) and increased susceptibility to PH and right heart failure later in life ( 597 – 599 ). This has been shown in several animal models gestated at high altitude ( 588 , 600 – 606 ) or in isobaric hypoxic chambers ( 72 , 114 , 539 , 607 – 610 ), and triggers echocardiographic indices of PH in adulthood. This implies a direct effect of chronic hypoxia on the fetal pulmonary circulation with persisting adverse effects into adulthood ( 599 , 611 – 613 ). Chronic hypoxia also impacts airway and parenchyma development and function ( 613 – 616 ). The HIF pathway is a key regulator in lung development, acting on genes containing hypoxia response elements ( 617 , 618 ). Hypoxic conditions result in the enlargement of distal airspaces, particularly evident in the saccular stage ( 619 ). Concurrently, a significant decrease in septal wall volume is observed in the canalicular stage ( 619 ). These structural alterations – enlarged airspaces and reduced septal tissue – are characteristic features of arrested alveolar development, resembling chronic lung disease phenotypes such as bronchopulmonary dysplasia ( 620 ). Additionally, chronic hypoxia can disrupt at least two vital processes in lung development. First, the reabsorption of fetal lung liquid, driven by the upregulation of epithelial sodium channels, particularly increasing expression of genes regulating sodium ( SCNN1B , ATP1A1 , ATP1B1 ) and water ( AQP4 ) movement ( 614 , 621 , 622 ). Second, the maturation of pulmonary surfactant, a lipoprotein complex that reduces surface tension in the alveoli and is crucial in preventing respiratory distress ( 622 ). Its production is regulated by hormones and key proteins such as surfactant protein (SP)-A, SP-B, and SP-C, which have been shown to increase under chronic hypoxia ( 622 ). These may be adaptive responses in preparation for the successful fetal to neonatal transition at birth. 5.3.4. NEURODEVELOPMENTAL ADAPTATION Structural consequences associated with chronic hypoxia in the fetal central nervous system include reduced total brain volume ( 623 ) and impaired hippocampal axonal maturation ( 624 ). At the microscopic and cellular levels, impaired fetal development is linked to altered cortical volume and structure, neuronal loss, and significant myelination deficits ( 623 – 625 ). In addition, hypoxic fetuses develop changes in the white matter vasculature, characterized by an increased capillary density ( 625 ). These findings indicate a failure of the normal neuro-organogenesis and cellular maturation, resulting in structural deficits that are observed despite the compensatory hypervascularization and the redistribution of blood flow provided by the fetal brain-sparing response. Furthermore, gestational hypoxia increases the blood-brain barrier permeability, thereby augmenting oxidative stress and inflammatory mediators ( 626 , 627 ). Notably, maternal antioxidant treatment protected adult offspring against memory loss and hippocampal atrophy in a rodent model of hypoxic pregnancy ( 628 ). Combined, clinical and preclinical studies demonstrate that FGR and chronic fetal hypoxia are associated with significant neuropathology, including reduced brain volume, cortical grey matter deficits, and white matter injury involving neuroinflammation and hypomyelination ( 629 – 631 ). Moreover, the structural and functional alterations in the brainstem may underlie the cardiovascular ( 95 , 632 ) and respiratory ( 633 , 634 ) morbidities observed in FGR and chronically hypoxic fetuses. 5.3.5. PRETERM BIRTH AND STILLBIRTH Evidence from populations in the Himalayas and the Andes shows that high altitude is a significant risk factor for adverse pregnancy outcomes, particularly stillbirth and preterm birth ( 635 ). High-altitude residency elevates the odds ratio (OR) of stillbirth by nearly fourfold (3.9) and increases the risk of preterm birth by 70% (OR of 1.7) compared to sea-level populations ( 636 ). Multiple studies report a significantly higher incidence of stillbirth at high altitudes than low altitudes. The primary driver is believed to be chronic fetal hypoxia, which can lead to FGR, inadequate placental function, and ultimately, fetal demise ( 472 ). This association is dose-dependent, with risk escalating as altitude increases ( 474 , 637 , 638 ). In addition, stillbirth is exponentially magnified when chronic hypoxia is compounded by hypertensive complications of pregnancy (i.e., preeclampsia or gestational hypertension), elevating the risk of stillbirth to OR 6.0 ( 472 ). The increases in maternal hemoglobin levels (polycythemia) and blood viscosity have also been correlated to an increased risk of stillbirth ( 637 ). In several studies, the average gestational age and the rate of preterm birth at high altitude are unaffected ( 472 , 478 , 493 ), with one study showing an increased incidence of preterm birth at elevations &gt;2,000 m ( 48 ). However, the high prevalence of pregnancy complications like preeclampsia and FGR at high altitude often necessitates obstetric intervention that leads to preterm delivery ( 472 , 639 ). Still, studies in the Rocky Mountains showed no increase in the rate of preterm birth ( 8 , 640 ), or in infant mortality resulting from preterm birth, at least in part due to improved medical care ( 641 ). Under conditions such as those experienced by the overwhelming majority of contemporary highland populations, where advanced medical care is not available, the increased frequency of FGR and hypertensive disorders of pregnancy would be expected to act synergistically to raise neonatal and infant mortality ( 50 ). However, the risk is not uniform across all populations, indicating that long-term ancestral adaptation provides substantial protection ( 482 , 642 ). 6. FETAL TO NEONATAL TRANSITION AT HIGH ALTITUDE At birth, the cardiopulmonary system rapidly transitions from placental to pulmonary gas exchange, a process driven by O 2 exposure and the increased production of endothelial NO and prostacyclin ( 586 ). This shift depends on the coordinated maturation of the pulmonary vasculature and airways during fetal life, ensuring successful adaptation to extrauterine respiration. In a healthy fetus, oxygenated blood bypasses the lungs through vascular shunts, which preserves high PVR until birth. As gestation advances, PVR gradually falls as the pulmonary vasculature and airways mature ( 643 ). Limited angiogenesis during the canalicular stage (16–25 weeks) gives way to extensive capillary expansion and air-blood interface formation from the saccular stage (26–38 weeks) through adolescence, with shared signaling pathways ( 586 ) governing both airway and vascular development. At low altitude, the cardiopulmonary transition is complete within hours, characterized by the rapid closure of fetal shunts and a decrease in systolic pulmonary artery pressure (sPAP) from approximately 60 mmHg immediately after birth to near adult levels (10–15 mmHg) within days ( 644 – 646 ). In contrast, highland neonates frequently experience a delayed cardiopulmonary transition, with persistently elevated sPAP lasting weeks to months ( 456 ). For example, newborns in Morococha, Peru (4,540 m), maintained sPAP values near systemic levels (&gt;60 mmHg) for 72 hours postnatally ( 647 ), while those in Mexico City (2,240 m) ( 648 ) and Leadville, Colorado (3,100 m) ( 456 ) exhibited elevated sPAP levels lasting days to weeks after birth. The prolonged cardiopulmonary transition and sustained elevation of sPAP places additional stress on the developing cardiopulmonary system and precipitates downstream abnormalities in the lung and its pulmonary circulation. 6.1. ALVEOLAR SIMPLIFICATION AND IMPAIRED LUNG DEVELOPMENT Oxygen tension is crucial for the formation of the prenatal and postnatal lung saccules and alveoli, which determine the surface area available for pulmonary gas exchange ( 649 – 651 ). Hypoxia disrupts the septation of peripheral air spaces, a process required for alveolar formation that continues into postnatal life ( 652 ), thereby limiting proper lung development and functional capacity. In mice, hypobaric hypoxia (505 mmHg, ~13.5% O 2 ) during late gestation (days 16.5–19.5) results in a reduced radial alveolar count (a measure of alveolar number) and increased mean linear intercept (an index of alveolar size); these structural deficits are apparent at two weeks of age and persist into adulthood ( 612 ). Similar findings are evident in rats, where chronic exposure to 13% O 2 from gestation through postnatal day 14 irreversibly attenuates alveolar septation, resulting in increased alveolar size and a reduced number of alveoli ( 649 , 653 ). Due to the extreme developmental sensitivity of the lung, even brief hypoxic insults have substantial impacts; in rats, nine hours of maternal hypoxia on the last day of pregnancy, combined with a 1–2 hour exposure after birth, was shown to delay septation, slow the postnatal increase in lung volume, and accelerate alveolar wall thinning ( 650 ). Combined prenatal and postnatal hypoxia (the “double hit” model) amplifies these effects, leading to severe alveolar simplification, decreased airspace volume, and enlarged parenchymal airspaces ( 654 ). Traditional ovine models of PI demonstrate that fetal hypoxia leads to reduced pulmonary alveolarization at 115 days of gestation ( 655 ), with significantly lower lung diffusion capacity and compliance at 8 weeks ( 656 ), and fewer but larger alveoli at 2 years of age ( 657 ). More recently, the EXTrauterine Environment for Neonatal Development (EXTEND) artificial womb system has advanced this field by enabling precise manipulation of fetal oxygenation, independent of maternal influences, in preterm lambs ( 619 , 658 , 659 ). Using the EXTEND system, fetal sheep exposed to a gradual reduction in O 2 delivery (~14 ml/kg/min) for 14–21 days during the saccular stage of lung development developed enlarged airspaces and reduced septal wall volume, alongside impaired fetal growth ( 619 ). In addition to alveolar formation, adequate transition to air breathing requires appropriate production of pulmonary surfactant, which reduces surface tension at the air-liquid interface within the alveoli and prevents vascular collapse. Surfactant maturation occurs predominantly during the saccular stage, corresponding to 25 weeks of gestation in humans or 120 days of gestation for the lamb fetus ( 660 ). The effects of gestational hypoxia on surfactant production are temporally dependent ( 615 , 661 ): late-gestation hypoxia increases surfactant production ( 622 , 662 ), whereas early hypoxia suppresses SP regulation ( 615 , 663 , 664 ). Hypoxic exposure during the critical saccular stage not only impairs alveolar structure but also reduces SP-B and the number of surfactant-producing alveolar type 2 cells, thereby exacerbating the disruption of lung development ( 619 , 658 , 659 ), In humans, impaired fetal oxygenation also has a profound and durable impact on lung architecture and respiratory health. Bronchopulmonary dysplasia, a chronic lung disease characterized by alveolar simplification and abnormal pulmonary vasculature, stands as the most definitive clinical model for investigating how hypoxia influences alveolarization, given that disrupted oxygenation is a fundamental factor in its underlying pathology. Even without mechanical ventilation or extreme prematurity, FGR fetuses subjected to chronic hypoxia in utero exhibit a similar BPD-lung phenotype: reduced alveolar number and enlarged airspaces. This suggests that hypoxia is the fundamental common denominator in both BPD and FGR-related respiratory dysfunction ( 665 – 667 ). Respiratory risks related to hypoxia extend beyond prematurity alone, as preterm FGR cases exhibit marked alveolar simplification with enlarged airspaces and reduced alveolarization ( 668 ), and are more vulnerable to subsequent respiratory disease in early childhood than prematurely born infants without FGR ( 633 , 666 , 667 , 669 ). In addition, term FGR infants show worse respiratory outcomes than their AGA peers ( 670 ). 6.2. NEONATAL PULMONARY HYPERTENSION Neonatal PH manifests as either PPHN or PH secondary to severe pulmonary vascular disease associated with bronchopulmonary dysplasia ( 671 ). Both forms are relevant at high altitudes and raise neonatal and infant mortality rates ( 672 ). Studies from El Alto, Bolivia (4,100 m) report a PPHN prevalence 3.7 times greater ( 12 ) than global estimates of 1.8 cases per 1,000 live births ( 673 ), with affected neonates exhibiting severe hypoxemia, high PVR, and right-to-left extrapulmonary shunting of deoxygenated blood in the absence of CHD ( 674 ). High-altitude ancestry confers protection against the development and persistence of PH in newborns. Newborns of Tibetan origin maintain mean S a O 2 levels above 85%, while those of Han origin show lower S a O 2 (~70%), which continues to decline over time, being lower during sleep and accompanied by signs of hypoxemia ( 18 ). Overall, the Tibetan population shows higher neonatal oxygenation than Andean and European newborns. However, only Han newborns show a progressive decline in S a O 2 ( 675 ). Therefore, reduced oxygenation in Han newborns may be a primary factor contributing to the higher incidence of PPHN, whereas the altitude adaptation observed in Tibetan populations, characterized by high and sustained S a O 2 , likely provides better neonatal oxygenation trajectories from an early stage in life. Comparative studies provide compelling evidence for the developmental programming of pulmonary vascular function in humans at high altitude, where chronic hypobaric hypoxia acts as a foundational stressor ( 472 , 594 , 596 , 676 ). In addition, when preeclampsia is superimposed upon this hypoxic environment, particularly when complicated by FGR, it synergistically exacerbates neonatal pulmonary complications. Recent work comparing pulmonary vascular function between infants born to women with preeclampsia versus those with normotensive pregnancies in La Paz and El Alto, Bolivia, also provides compelling evidence for the developmental programming of pulmonary vascular function in humans at high altitude. Specifically, preeclampsia complicated by FGR significantly increased the risk of neonatal PH as indicated by echocardiographic evidence of greater right ventricular systolic pressure (RVSP) and lower pulmonary artery acceleration time (PAAT), an index of vascular compliance, compared to controls ( 596 ). Importantly, these effects were independent of gestational age at birth, indicating that differences likely reflected disease-specific effects rather than factors related to prematurity. In addition, these findings underscore the importance of comprehensive assessments that incorporate both pressure-dependent and pressure-independent measures. While reduced PAAT and early changes in pulmonary artery compliance may coincide with mild elevations in PVR, these alterations are often not accompanied by a detectable rise in pulmonary artery pressure (PAP). Consequently, focusing exclusively on quantitative estimates of RVSP or qualitative pressure-dependent measures, such as septal wall flattening and right ventricle morphology, may have limited sensitivity in detecting more subtle pulmonary vascular abnormalities in infants ( 677 ). For this reason, future research seeking to identify the impact of early life exposures such as hypoxia on the pulmonary vasculature in neonatal or pediatric populations should include PAAT, which more broadly captures maturational alterations in compliance and reflects increased vascular resistance and pressure in the absence of PH per se ( 678 , 679 ). Fetoplacental hypoxia is an essential contributor to the association between vascular disorders of pregnancy and abnormal pulmonary vascular function during neonatal life at high altitudes. Preeclampsia exacerbates fetal hypoxia at high altitudes, as demonstrated by elevated cord blood EPO and hemoglobin levels and reduced umbilical venous PO 2 at delivery in highland Andean cohorts ( 594 , 596 ). The ramifications of this hypoxic exposure are clear and are underscored by the finding that neonates with elevated PAP or reduced vascular compliance show greater fetal hypoxemia than those with normal pulmonary vascular function ( 596 ). This aligns with foundational studies linking ambient hypoxia, periodic breathing patterns, and infantile PH in high-altitude neonates ( 456 ). 6.2.1. NITRIC OXIDE PATHWAY DYSREGULATION Disruption of the NO pathway is fundamental to the development of hypoxia-driven pulmonary vascular changes. Newborn lambs raised at high altitude mount a compensatory, though insufficient, increase in total lung NO synthase and eNOS expression; however, this is accompanied by reduced levels of soluble guanylate cyclase, increased phosphodiesterase-5 (PDE5), and diminished HO expression and carbon monoxide production ( 680 , 681 ). This imbalance reflects a core disturbance in NO-mediated pulmonary vascular homeostasis that contributes to enhanced pulmonary vasoconstrictor reactivity. The importance of eNOS in lung development is further highlighted by studies showing that eNOS-deficient mice subjected to mild postnatal hypoxia fail to recover normal alveolar structure, with persistent alveolar simplification that persists despite prolonged recovery under normoxic conditions ( 682 ). Chronic hypoxia limits the typical rise in O 2 tension at birth, thereby suppressing the stimulation of eNOS-dependent NO production and the relaxation of fetal pulmonary vascular tone ( 586 , 605 , 683 ). Endothelial dysfunction manifests as enhanced vasoconstrictor production ( 588 , 684 – 686 ), impaired NO bioavailability due to enhanced reactive O 2 scavenging ( 687 – 691 ), diminished eNOS expression ( 692 , 693 ) or NO synthesis ( 680 ), and altered enzyme phosphorylation dynamics impacting activity ( 694 ). Additionally, enhanced Ca 2+ signaling, evidenced by the observation that blocking store-operated Ca 2+ channels attenuates hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction more effectively in animals gestated at high altitude, may contribute to increased vasoconstrictor responsiveness ( 695 , 696 ). 6.2.2. ANGIOGENIC IMBALANCE AND THE VASCULAR ENDOTHELIAL GROWTH FACTOR (VEGF)/SOLUBLE FMS-LIKE TYROSINE KINASE-1 (sFlt-1) AXIS Angiogenic imbalance represents a central mechanism underlying pulmonary vascular dysfunction following perinatal hypoxic exposure. Persistent pulmonary vascular abnormalities may also result from impaired angiogenesis due to reduced bioavailability of VEGF, a potent proangiogenic protein secreted by airway epithelial cells that initiate pulmonary angiogenesis during lung development ( 697 ). Reduced VEGF expression has been observed in infants with severe bronchopulmonary dysplasia, a condition often occurring alongside PPHN ( 698 ). Experimental animal models further demonstrate that maternal hypoxia reduces VEGF protein expression in the fetal lung ( 699 ), and show that inhibition of VEGF in the fetal lung or amniotic fluid with sFlt-1 or other VEGF-specific antagonists leads to pulmonary vasculature pruning and increased arterial wall thickness ( 700 , 701 ). Therapeutic approaches targeting VEGF and sFlt-1 are discussed in sections 9.2.1 and 9.2.2 , respectively. Preeclampsia-associated sFlt-1 overproduction by the placenta provides a mechanistic link between maternal complications and offspring pulmonary vascular dysfunction. Circulating sFlt-1 exerts antiangiogenic effects by sequestering VEGF and PIGF, thereby preventing activation of cognate receptors and disrupting vascular homeostasis ( 702 ). Insufficient placental oxygenation drives excessive sFlt-1 production in preeclampsia, as hypoxia upregulates sFlt-1 expression in placental villous explants and primary CTB cultures ( 703 ). This mechanism is expected to impede fetal lung angiogenesis by reducing VEGF bioactivity. Clinical evidence of sFlt-1-mediated effects is seen in elevated maternal and umbilical cord plasma sFlt-1 levels among highland Andean preeclampsia cases versus controls, which positively correlated with neonatal PVR ( 596 ). Although fetal pulmonary vessels are in direct contact with fetal (cord) plasma, amniotic fluid, an ultrafiltrate of maternal plasma, fills the fetal airways, and therefore, elevated amniotic sFlt-1 levels observed in preeclampsia ( 704 ) may also contribute to pulmonary abnormalities ( 701 ). 6.2.3. MECHANISTIC TARGET OF RAPAMYCIN (mTOR) PATHWAY One mechanism potentially influencing the association between perinatal hypoxia and PH is the mTOR pathway. The mTOR pathway serves as a key regulatory mechanism, linking airway and vascular development during lung morphogenesis. In the developing lung, the mTOR complex 1 (mTORC1) influences elongation and branching of the conducting airways while temporally and spatially coordinating pulmonary vascular development through its interaction with the HIF-1α signaling pathway ( 699 ). Angiogenesis is initiated through the activation of an mTORC1-HIF-1α complex, which increases vascular endothelial growth factor A (VEGFA) transcription and secretion from advancing airway epithelial cells. The functional interaction of the mTORC1 component raptor and HIF-1α enhances HIF-1α binding to transcriptional factors (e.g., cAMP response element binding protein [CREB]-binding protein [CBP]/histone acetyltransferase p300), subsequently amplifying HIF-1α activity ( 705 ). Raptor recruits mTOR to HIF-1α through the presence of a putative mTOR signaling motif located within the HIF-1α N-terminus ( 706 ). This coordinated pathway temporally and spatially unifies airway branching morphogenesis with pulmonary vascular development, and its disruption may contribute to the developmental programming of PH ( 707 ). 6.3. CONGENITAL HEART DISEASE (CHD) CHD is markedly more frequent in neonates born at very high altitudes (&gt; 3,500 m), largely because chronic hypoxemia maintains ductal patency ( 708 ) by preserving fetal vasodilator signaling, impairing O 2 -sensitive ion channel function, and preventing the constriction-induced ischemic remodeling required for permanent ductal closure. Low O 2 tension shifts ductal tone toward vasodilation by increasing NO, prostaglandin E2, and carbon monoxide production ( 586 ). Differences in O 2 -dependent ion channel signaling also critically influence ductal tone. In the term ductus arteriosus, rising PO 2 at birth activates the mitochondrial-K + O 2 sensor (NDUFS2/Complex I) in ductal smooth muscle cells, increasing mitochondrial ROS production that inhibits smooth-muscle K + channels and opens L-type Ca 2+ channels to cause strong constriction of the ductus arteriosus ( 709 ). In contrast, prematurity-related PDA mainly results from developmental immaturity of these O 2 -sensing mechanisms rather than environmental O 2 deprivation ( 710 ). Developmental changes in the ductus arteriosus during gestation normally reduce prostaglandin sensitivity and enhance O 2 -responsive ion channel function, but in preterm infants, the ductus retains elevated levels of/heightened sensitivity to vasodilators such as NO and prostaglandin E2 ( 711 , 712 ) and shows reduced expression and O 2 -sensitivity of voltage-gated K + channels, all of which promote sustained patency ( 713 ). Consequently, the distribution of CHD at high altitudes differs substantially from that of lowland populations, with PDA representing the predominant condition, rather than the more typical ventricular septal defects observed at lower elevations ( 517 ). In El Alto, Bolivia (4,100 m), the prevalence of hemodynamically relevant CHD in neonates was five times higher than global estimates ( 12 ), with PDA comprising most cases. Similarly, a cross-sectional study of 84,302 school-aged children in Nagqu, Tibet (4,500 m), reported a 5% CHD prevalence, with PDA accounting for 66% of cases ( 518 ). Even higher prevalence rates emerge at extreme altitudes: in China’s Qinghai Province of China, 13% of children aged 3–19 years living above 4,200 m were diagnosed with CHD ( 714 ), though the relative distribution of lesions varied between these China-based studies, with one reporting PDA prominence ( 517 ), while another, more recent study found atrial septal defects to be most common ( 714 ). Notably, altitude-related CHD patterns emerge even at moderate elevations, suggesting a continuum of altitude-related effects rather than a threshold phenomenon. For instance, data from Colombia showed that children living at altitudes of 1,285–3,000 m had an increased incidence of PDA and LV outflow obstruction compared to children living at sea level ( 715 ). The clinical implications of such effects extend beyond the immediate neonatal period. In neonates, PDA is quite common and often resolves spontaneously; however, the delayed or inhibited normal ductal closure observed at moderate or high altitudes necessitates monitoring and potential intervention. Persistent, untreated PDA can progress to serious consequences, including heart failure, endocarditis, or pulmonary edema, which may be exacerbated by concurrent PH that often develops with chronic altitude exposure. 7. LONG-TERM CONSEQUENCES OF PREGNANCY AT HIGH ALTITUDE The developmental origins of adult health and disease framework, introduced by David Barker more than three decades ago ( 716 ), posits that biological or environmental exposures during preconceptual, prenatal, and early postnatal life influence physiological function and disease susceptibility throughout the lifespan. Insufficient oxygenation, whether due to high-altitude pregnancy or sea-level pregnancy complicated by increased uterine vascular resistance, such as preeclampsia, is one of the most pervasive and disruptive environmental stressors during embryonic and fetal development that can predispose offspring to complications at birth and increase lifelong susceptibility to disease ( 467 , 580 , 717 , 718 ). Understanding the long-term impacts of gestational hypoxia at altitude is particularly important due to its utility to serve as a natural model for studying prenatal environmental stressors and its substantial public health relevance for highland communities. 7.1. CARDIOVASCULAR SYSTEM The developing cardiovascular system is highly sensitive to alterations in O 2 supply and FGR provides a clinically well-characterized model of chronic fetal hypoxia. Advanced echocardiographic techniques, including tissue Doppler imaging and speckle-tracking echocardiography, enable detailed evaluation of myocardial contractility, diastolic function, and neonatal hemodynamics, revealing abnormalities not detectable with conventional imaging ( 719 ). In FGR, tissue Doppler imaging reveals subtle myocardial dysfunction in the neonatal period, including altered tissue annular velocities ( 720 ). Notably, despite apparent catch-up growth yielding relatively normal-sized cardiac dimensions, FGR cases exhibit persistent myocardial dysfunction as early as 2 years of age ( 721 , 722 ). Small-for-gestational-age infants also demonstrate a 10% reduction in longitudinal strain during the first week of life compared to controls ( 723 ), and FGR neonates show marked decreases in LV ejection fraction and global longitudinal strain, reflecting impaired adaptation to the increased workload that follows birth ( 724 ). This subclinical dysfunction persists into preadolescence, as evidenced by reduced myocardial longitudinal strain ( 725 ). Moreover, fetuses below the 10 th weight percentile, irrespective of umbilical artery pulsatility index and CPR, display more globular cardiac dimensions that correlate with increased perinatal morbidity ( 726 ). Although these studies were conducted at low altitude, they provide a mechanistic framework for understanding how FGR – more common in high-altitude pregnancies – could adversely affect cardiac structure and function in such settings, a hypothesis that remains to be directly tested in high-altitude populations. While human pregnancy complicated by FGR has been linked to changes in fetal cardiac structure and function, it is still not fully understood whether such alterations stem directly from ongoing fetal hypoxia. A recent study ( 516 ) applied innovative in vivo ultrasound imaging techniques in pregnant sheep to assess how the fetal heart responds to chronic isobaric fetal hypoxia in late gestation. In this model, progressive fetal hypoxia led to profound changes in fetal cardiac structure and function, associated with a switch to LV dominance triggered by fetal brain sparing. The findings also indicate that fetal cardiac compensatory reserves become exhausted with progressive hypoxia ( 516 ). The cardiac responses of fetal sheep in hypoxic pregnancy are similar to those observed in human FGR pregnancy, supporting chronic fetal hypoxia as a key contributor to fetal cardiac remodeling and dysfunction in human FGR pregnancy ( 516 , 727 ). Combined, these human FGR and experimental hypoxia data indicate that chronic fetal hypoxia can drive adverse cardiac remodeling. However, direct evidence that comparable cardiovascular changes occur in pregnancies at high altitude is currently limited, so extrapolations to high-altitude populations should be regarded as hypothesis-generating rather than definitive. Sensitization of the sympathoadrenal medullary system is a common feature of developmental hypoxia responses across vertebrate species. Basal levels of plasma norepinephrine and/or epinephrine are increased in hypoxic fetal sheep ( 563 , 728 – 730 ), fetal llamas ( 105 ), and embryonic chickens ( 547 , 731 ). The mammalian fetal peripheral vasoconstrictor response to hypoxia, part of the fetal brain-sparing response and triggered by sympathetic activation, becomes markedly sensitized by chronic hypoxia ( 728 ). While beneficial in utero , sustained sensitization can become detrimental, contributing to cardiac and vascular remodeling and dysfunction that may increase cardiovascular risk throughout life. Alterations in cardiac structure and function can translate into measurable clinical consequences that persist postnatally. Hypoxic pregnancy in rats has been shown to program endothelial dysfunction, hypertension, cardiac wall remodeling, and cardiac sympathetic dominance in adult offspring ( 580 , 717 , 732 , 733 ). Programmed cardiac sympathetic dominance may represent an attempt to maintain cardiac output in the face of increased afterload ( 580 , 732 ). Chronic hypoxia in rat pregnancy (10% O 2 from day 15–21 of gestation) also increases the sensitivity of the adult 6-month-old rat heart to ischemia/reperfusion injury, as indicated by increased myocardial infarct size and decreased postischemic recovery of LV function ( 734 – 738 ). Together, these findings suggest that early adaptations establish a foundation for progressive cardiovascular injury that becomes increasingly apparent with age. A recent study showed that gestational hypobaric hypoxia can exert long-lasting effects on the cardiovascular health of one-year old guinea pig offspring, contributing to cardiac remodeling, vascular dysfunction, oxidative stress, and inflammatory changes ( 739 ). In addition, studies using sheep reveal that hypoxic pregnancy programs sympathetic dominance, endothelial dysfunction, and systemic hypertension in the adult offspring ( 184 , 185 ). Several of the observed cardiovascular effects at adulthood due to pregnancy at high altitude are preserved even after returning to normoxic conditions after birth ( 564 ), underscoring the potential for profound O 2 deprivation during critical developmental periods to produce durable cardiovascular alterations. In humans, early childhood manifestations of FGR pregnancy can include premature development of atherogenic changes within the abdominal aorta ( 740 ) and disproportionate vessel wall thickening that persists independent of body size ( 741 ). Multiple studies have documented increased blood pressure in neonates ( 742 , 743 ), infants ( 744 ), children ( 745 , 746 ), and adults ( 747 ) with a history of FGR. The development of endothelial dysfunction and hypertension in childhood or young adulthood ( 748 – 750 ) among those born with reduced birth weight further underscores how fetal hypoxia creates a permanent shift in cardiovascular physiology, establishing disease trajectories that unfold across decades. Epidemiological data indicate an association between low birth weight, which frequently occurs at high altitude, and an increased risk of hypertension and cardiometabolic disease in adulthood ( 751 , 752 ). Pregnant women exposed to high altitude during pregnancy are more susceptible to developing preeclampsia ( 35 , 472 ), and hypertensive disorders of pregnancy are associated with increased cardiovascular risk in affected offspring, including early onset of cardiac hypertrophy, aortic remodeling, endothelial dysfunction, hypertension, and ischemia-reperfusion injury in adulthood ( 495 , 503 , 504 , 580 , 740 ). In a Danish population-based study including more than 2.4 million individuals born from 1977 to 2018, individuals born following pregnancies complicated by hypertensive disorders face substantially higher risks of early-onset cardiovascular disease (CVD), including more than double the risk of hypertensive disease and a 49% increased risk of myocardial infarction before age 40 ( 753 ). These findings, together with the increased prevalence of preeclampsia at high altitude, suggest that hypertensive disorders of pregnancy may represent an important pathway by which high-altitude exposure influences long-term cardiovascular risk, although this has not yet been systematically studied in high-altitude cohorts. In rats, even brief prenatal hypoxia has been shown to elevate systolic blood pressure in adulthood, accompanied by increased sympathetic activity and impaired baroreflex sensitivity ( 754 ). Furthermore, vascular endothelial function is also affected by prenatal hypoxia. Several studies demonstrate endothelial dysfunction in adult offspring, driven by blunted NO-mediated vasodilation, enhanced vasoconstriction, and increased arterial stiffness ( 580 , 581 , 739 , 755 – 758 ). The latter is particularly significant because increased large-artery stiffness is a well-established risk factor for hypertension and atherosclerosis in later life ( 759 , 760 ). These experimental findings provide mechanistic support for how hypoxic pregnancy, including that occurring at high altitude, might contribute to later cardiovascular disease. Similarly, epigenetic modifications have been shown to influence the programming of vascular dysfunction. Hypoxic gestation decreases NO synthesis in the arteries of adult rat male offspring via the miR-155–5p/nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate oxidase/ROS axis ( 581 ). Furthermore, exposure to antenatal hypoxia is associated with a hypercontractile state of the arteries in adult male offspring, mediated by hypomethylation of the angiotensin type 1 receptor (AT1R) promoter, which increases AT1R expression ( 761 ). Notably, increased AT1R expression, which mediates most of the effects of angiotensin II ( 762 ), is associated with severe cardiovascular defects, including increased vascular stiffness ( 763 ) and hypertension ( 764 ). Collectively, past studies in humans and in mammalian and preclinical animal models support the concept that chronic fetal hypoxia, as observed in FGR and modeled experimentally, can have immediate and long-term consequences for cardiovascular structure and function. In the context of high-altitude pregnancy, these findings suggest plausible mechanisms by which chronic hypoxia might program cardiovascular disease across the life course, but confirmatory data from high-altitude populations are still largely lacking and represent an important gap for future research. While low birth weight and FGR are more common at high altitude, systematic longitudinal studies that directly link in utero high-altitude exposure to detailed cardiovascular phenotyping in offspring are scarce. Prospective cohort studies in highland populations are needed to test whether the programming patterns described above are recapitulated in this setting. 7.2. PULMONARY SYSTEM Gestational hypoxia and related maternal vascular disorders increase offspring risk for CVD and pulmonary dysfunction. Evidence from experimental animal models and human studies suggests that O 2 deprivation during critical perinatal developmental periods results in lasting structural and functional changes in the pulmonary vasculature and peripheral airways, establishing a developmental basis for later-life pulmonary vascular and respiratory dysfunction ( 564 , 595 , 596 , 765 ). 7.2.1. EVIDENCE FROM ANIMAL MODELS Rodent models consistently demonstrate that early-life O 2 deficits induce persistent changes to the pulmonary vasculature. In mice, pups exposed to hypobaric hypoxia (380 mmHg) from postnatal day 2 to 9 developed right ventricular hypertrophy (RVH) during the neonatal period ( 766 ). In mice, perinatal exposure to hypobaric hypoxia from embryonic day 15 through postnatal day 4 resulted in elevated RVSP, reduced PAAT, and RVH at 8 weeks of age under normobaric normoxia conditions ( 699 ). Likewise, neonatal rodents exposed to normobaric hypoxia (10% O 2 , from gestational day 10.5–18.5 or during whole gestation) exhibited reduced pulmonary artery compliance, exaggerated hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstrictor response, and increased medial thickness of the small pulmonary arteries ( 611 , 765 ), with severe RVH, elevated PVR, and remodeling evident after hypoxic exposure (13% O 2 ) from birth through two weeks of life ( 688 , 767 ). Likewise, rat pups exposed to hypoxia showed increased medial thickness of the small pulmonary arteries, RVH, reduced PAAT, and an exaggerated hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstrictor response during adulthood ( 611 , 765 , 768 ). Sheep models of chronic gestational hypoxia and PI also show significant and lasting changes to the pulmonary blood vessels. Newborn lambs exposed to chronic gestational hypoxia (3,600–3,800 m) exhibited higher baseline PVR, increased sPAP, intensified pulmonary vascular contractile responses to hypoxia and vasoconstrictive agents, and thicker, more muscular small pulmonary arteries compared to lowland controls ( 588 , 686 , 769 ). Interestingly, these effects were maintained for at least 2 weeks, even when returned to sea-level immediately after birth ( 686 ). Furthermore, the observed effects are strongly related to biomechanical alterations of several vascular beds, towards cardiovascular stiffening ( 770 – 774 ). Chronic PI models of FGR in sheep similarly exhibit reduced fetal pulmonary vessel density and impaired vascular growth at 115 days of gestation, or approximately 78% of term ( 655 ). Graded oxygenation protocols further reveal that highland newborn lambs have higher sPAP and PVR at any arterial PO 2 compared to their lowland counterparts ( 588 ), indicating differential sensitivity to hypoxic stress. Recent advances with the EXTEND artificial womb system have reinforced these findings. Specifically, fetal sheep exposed to precisely controlled hypoxia (reducing O 2 delivery by ~14 ml/kg/min) for 14–21 days in the saccular stage display a pronounced reduction in macroscopic vessel number, loss of endothelium within the microvasculature, decreased microvessel density, and increased vessel muscularization. Prospective sheep studies show that highland gestation yields lasting cardiovascular and pulmonary adaptations that persist into adulthood. Unlike rodents, male sheep gestated and born at high altitude had lower mean pulmonary artery pressure (mPAP) and PVR in adulthood compared to those gestated and born at low altitude ( 564 ). Furthermore, when exposed to acute hypoxia, high-altitude-born adult sheep also display blunted systemic and pulmonary pressure responses ( 564 ), suggesting that developmental hypoxia programs both basal and reactive cardiopulmonary function and that such effects vary between species. Avian models further support these concepts. Adult chickens from highland incubated eggs have an increased risk of PH ( 599 ) and altered cardiac baroreflex sensitivity ( 543 ). 7.2.2. EVIDENCE FROM HUMAN STUDIES Some infants exhibit early normalization of pulmonary pressures, suggesting a capacity for recovery. For example, in prospective cohort studies from La Paz, Bolivia and Leadville, Colorado, pulmonary vascular abnormalities observed in some neonates born to preeclampsia cases normalized by 2–12 months of age ( 596 , 775 ). Despite this apparent early stabilization, infants experiencing early pulmonary vascular insults remain at elevated risk of progressive pulmonary vascular abnormalities later in childhood and adulthood. Prematurity, low birth weight, and PH each independently increase the risk of later pulmonary vascular disease. For instance, prematurity increases the risk of PH by 8.5 times in childhood and more than threefold in adulthood ( 776 ), and is associated with higher sPAP at rest and during exercise in otherwise healthy adults ( 777 , 778 ). Similarly, lowland individuals with low birth weight (&lt; 2,500 g) have a 40% higher risk of pulmonary vascular disease after 12 years of age (779). Likewise, neonatal PH exaggerates pulmonary vasoreactivity responses at high altitude (4,559 m) in adulthood despite having normal basal pulmonary pressures at low altitude (378 m) ( 780 ). Maternal hypertensive disorders of pregnancy also increase the risk of pulmonary vascular dysfunction in affected offspring. In children and young adults (6 to 21 years of age) born to women with preeclampsia at high altitude (3,600 m; La Paz, Bolivia), basal PAP was 30% higher and signs of RVH more common compared to peers exposed only to the high-altitude environment ( 781 ). Importantly, siblings of preeclampsia-exposed offspring who experienced normal pregnancies exhibit normal vascular function, demonstrating that the vascular dysfunction seen in offspring of mothers with preeclampsia is attributable to preeclampsia itself, rather than inherited traits ( 781 ). Larger, population-based studies confirm these associations. In a cohort of highland Bolivians (3,600–4,100 m), young men (18–25 years) with excessive erythrocytosis (EE), a preclinical form of chronic mountain sickness (CMS), accompanied by pulmonary vascular dysfunction were more than five times more likely to have been born to mothers experiencing hypertensive complications of pregnancy and six times more likely to have experienced perinatal hypoxia compared to their healthy, highland counterparts ( 598 ). Finally, a 55-year follow-up study of 28 adolescents from Leadville, Colorado (3,100 m) found that those with high-altitude PH in youth were more likely to report functional limitations such as reduced exercise tolerance later in life, even though overt comorbidities, including PH, were not observed ( 782 ). 7.3. CHRONIC MOUNTAIN SICKNESS CMS, or Monge’s Disease, is a progressive disorder observed in long-term high-altitude North American and Andean, but not Tibetan, residents that is marked by pronounced polycythemia, persistent arterial hypoxia, and ventilatory impairment without underlying cardiopulmonary disease ( 783 – 785 ). The Qinghai Score defines CMS as a hemoglobin concentration ([Hb]) ≥19 g/dL in women or ≥21 g/dL in men, accompanied by symptoms such as dyspnea, sleep disturbance, cyanosis, venous dilatation, and neurological manifestations ( 783 ). CMS affects a substantial proportion of highland populations, with prevalence rates of 8–16% in men ( 786 , 787 ) and 9% in women residing at high altitude (Cerro de Pasco, Peru) ( 788 ). Within this group, 45% of affected individuals were postmenopausal women, and the potential influence of hormonal changes in older women was discussed. In addition, a previous study reported that the incidence of EE in male miners with permanent (&gt;10 years) residence at high altitude increases markedly with age, reaching 18.8% at 40–49 years, 27.4% at 50–59 years, and 33.7% at 60–69 years ( 789 ). A previous observation also suggested that CMS may be a clinical manifestation of aging ( 790 ). Taken together, these findings support a substantial age- and sex-related component and suggest a significant role of menopause in the incidence of CMS. The condition frequently leads to severe pulmonary complications, including elevated sPAP, PH, and RVH ( 791 – 795 ), often leading to disability and mortality due to right heart failure. Consequently, CMS is a significant public health concern for highland populations worldwide, particularly in the Andean, Himalayan, and North American Rocky Mountain regions ( 796 , 797 ). Emerging evidence recasts CMS as a disorder with perinatal origins rather than a purely adult-onset disease ( 598 , 798 ). In a community-based survey of 981 young highland men in La Paz-El Alto metropolitan region (3,600–4,100 m), adverse perinatal oxygenation and maternal hypertensive pregnancy complications were 6.4-fold and 5.3-fold more prevalent, respectively, in individuals with EE ([Hb] ≥18.3 g/dl) compared to healthy, altitude- and age-matched controls ( 598 ). EE represents a preclinical phase of CMS, characterized by progressive physiological deterioration, thereby providing a valuable window into the developmental origins of CMS ( 784 ). Physiological profiling revealed that reduced alveolar volumes were the primary determinant of impaired gas exchange efficiency in EE cases, as diffusion capacity normalized once corrected for alveolar volume ( 598 ). These men also had higher sPAP pressures, reduced PAAT, and increased right ventricular wall thickness compared to controls. Critically, these abnormalities were independent of hematocrit or blood viscosity, indicating that vascular programming, rather than secondary effects of EE, drives early PH. Individuals with EE or CMS also exhibit distinctive sleep-disordered breathing patterns, characterized by greater apnea-hypopnea indices and more pronounced nocturnal hypoxemia ( 799 – 801 ), suggesting fundamental alterations in respiratory control mechanisms. The modest elevations in PAP observed in young adults with EE, while not immediately life-threatening, may represent the earliest detectable manifestations of a progressive pathophysiological disease process. Understanding that these changes reflect developmental effects rather than acute altitude effects suggests that early intervention strategies could potentially prevent or modify disease progression before irreversible changes occur. This approach aligns with broader trends in personalized medicine and may ultimately prove more effective than traditional therapeutic strategies targeting established or advanced diseases. 8. MATERNAL CARDIOVASCULAR DISEASE AT HIGH ALTITUDE The relationship between pregnancy complications and maternal cardiovascular health is one of the most compelling examples of developmental programming affecting adult disease risk. At high altitude, where preeclampsia and FGR occur with increased frequency ( 8 , 30 , 32 , 35 – 37 , 49 , 51 , 268 , 270 , 469 , 470 , 802 – 804 ), pregnancy complications function as powerful predictors of lifelong maternal CVD, perhaps providing more information about maternal future CVD risk than offspring health outcomes ( 805 ). Since CVD remains the leading cause of mortality among women globally, accounting for approximately 25% of deaths ( 806 ), understanding these connections offers critical opportunities for early identification and prevention of CVD. The mechanisms underlying this maternal programming begin with the profound physiological stress that pregnancy complications place on the cardiovascular system. During preeclampsia, for example, the placenta releases excessive levels of anti-angiogenic factors, particularly sFlt-1, while reducing proangiogenic factors like PlGF ( 807 – 809 ). This imbalance creates widespread endothelial dysfunction and microangiopathy that extends far beyond the pregnancy itself. The same angiogenic factor disturbances that cause maternal symptoms during preeclampsia drive the vascular remodeling processes characteristic of coronary artery disease and heart failure ( 807 , 810 ). Women with elevated sFlt-1/PlGF or reduced PlGF during hypertensive disorders of pregnancy are at increased risk of postpartum hypertension ( 811 ), deterioration of cardiac structure, and elevated lipid levels ( 812 , 813 ). The cardiovascular remodeling initiated during complicated pregnancies persists long after delivery, creating a trajectory toward chronic disease. Most women with hypertensive disorders of pregnancy develop profound echocardiography abnormalities, including altered LV geometry, cardiac remodeling, and diastolic dysfunction ( 814 – 817 ). Rather than resolving postpartum, these structural and functional changes often worsen over time, progressing toward overt CVD ( 818 , 819 ). Two-thirds of women experiencing a hypertensive disorder of pregnancy maintain elevated blood pressure six months after delivery, with increased risk reported up to three years after delivery ( 702 , 817 , 820 ). For these women, the risk of developing chronic hypertension increases 4–10-fold within five years ( 821 , 822 ). The severity and timing of pregnancy complications determine the magnitude of future CVD risk, revealing a clear dose-response relationship. Early-onset or severe preeclampsia cases carry the highest risk ( 820 , 821 ) and tend to have more severe CVD outcomes ( 823 ). Specifically, early-onset preeclampsia (before 34 weeks of pregnancy), carries up to a 9–10-fold increased CVD-related mortality risk, while late-onset preeclampsia increases CVD-related mortality risk twofold ( 824 , 825 ). This gradient reflects the severity of the underlying pathophysiology. Recent research has identified striking parallels between preeclampsia and heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF), including shared mechanisms of cardiac hypertrophy, impaired angiogenesis, and chronic inflammation ( 826 , 827 ). These commonalities suggest that preeclampsia may initiate pathways that predispose to HFpEF development, providing mechanistic insight into elevated long-term cardiovascular mortality. Large-scale epidemiological studies corroborate links between pregnancy complications and adverse maternal cardiovascular health. A meta-analysis of over 10 million women demonstrated that preeclampsia doubles the risk of adverse cardiovascular outcomes in the decade after birth ( 823 ) and increases CVD-related death by two- to five-fold regardless of the follow-up duration ( 828 – 830 ). The cardiovascular consequences extend beyond heart disease to include cerebral microvasculature damage evident 5–10 years post-preeclampsia ( 831 , 832 ), a five-fold greater risk of end-stage renal disease ( 833 ), a 2–4 fold greater risk of chronic renal disease, and a 50% increased risk of atherosclerotic events ( 834 – 836 ). Prospective studies of highland resident women (La Paz, Bolivia) further demonstrate that preeclamptic women at altitude develop enlarged right heart chambers, increased pulmonary artery dimensions, and elevated PVR that persist six weeks after delivery ( 837 ). In addition, maternal peripheral SaO 2 , forced expiratory flow, and bronchial permeability index were blunted six weeks after delivery in women whose pregnancies were complicated by preeclampsia ( 837 ). At low altitude, preeclampsia is well established to approximately double long‑term maternal cardiovascular risk and increase CVD‑related mortality two‑ to five‑fold ( 838 , 839 ). In high‑altitude populations, pregnancies occur in a context of higher blood pressure and systemic vascular resistance and show greater frequency and/or severity of hypertensive disorders and FGR than at low altitude ( 49 , 270 , 839 ), suggesting that preeclampsia in this setting imposes at least comparable, and even greater, lifetime cardiovascular burden, although altitude‑stratified CVD outcome data remain limited. Experimental models corroborate these clinical observations, providing mechanistic insights into the programming process. The reduced uterine perfusion pressure (RUPP) rat model, which reduces uterine blood flow by 40% during late pregnancy, induces cardiac hypoxia and metabolic reprogramming in the maternal heart. This model produces lasting maternal cardiovascular remodeling, including increased LV mass and hypertension, while paradoxically preserving fetal cardiac metabolism except for reduced myocardial glucose levels ( 840 ). These findings suggest that maternal cardiovascular programming occurs as an adaptive response to maintain fetal well-being during pregnancy complications, but this adaptation comes at the cost of long-term maternal cardiovascular health. Beyond classical cardiovascular outcomes, high-altitude women also face risks of EE and CMS, yet little is known about how reproductive history shapes these syndromes. To our knowledge, no studies have directly compared EE or CMS risk between parous and nulliparous women, but postmenopausal status at altitude is associated with higher hematocrit, lower O 2 saturation, and higher CMS scores ( 788 , 841 ). These findings, together with experimental data showing that estrogen attenuates hypoxia-associated erythropoiesis ( 842 ), support a role for ovarian hormone-mediated protection and menstrual blood loss in modulating EE and CMS susceptibility. Together, this evidence establishes pregnancy complications at high altitude as critical sentinel events that identify women at high risk of future CVD. While existing research has predominantly focused on neonatal and fetal outcomes associated with high-altitude pregnancies, comparatively less attention has been directed toward the long-term maternal cardiovascular consequences, highlighting an important gap in the literature and underscoring the need for further investigation. 9. THERAPEUTIC TARGETS Maternal and fetal hypoxia are prominent factors leading to uteroplacental vascular dysfunction, which is at the core of impaired pregnancy complications like FGR and preeclampsia. Placental dysfunction resulting from chronic O 2 deprivation triggers a cascade of metabolic alterations and excessive ROS production, which in turn disrupts vascular homeostasis and impairs vasodilator signaling ( 90 , 391 ). Consequently, preclinical models and clinical trials have attempted to address O 2 signaling pathways, revealing additional mechanisms underlying vascular dysfunction induced by chronic hypoxia at different developmental stages. Preclinical evidence on the efficacy of antioxidants in preventing CVD in the offspring of hypoxic pregnancy has recently been extensively reviewed ( 843 ). In this regard, antioxidant therapies for fetal chronic hypoxia face key challenges, including the need for reliable short-term predictive indicators to facilitate the initiation of early-phase clinical trials, and safety data during pregnancy. Here, we examine how dietary interventions, angiogenic and vasoactive factors, as well as metabolic and hormonal pathways, contribute to and may be harnessed as therapies for chronic maternal and fetal hypoxia. Drawing on avian, rodent, ovine, and human studies, this section surveys state-of-the-art translational research targeting hypoxia-driven vascular dysfunction, which underlies impaired maternal systemic and uterine vascular function as well as fetal growth and the developmental programming of cardiovascular dysfunction in adult offspring. The current advancements using in vitro, preclinical models, and clinical trials for the therapies discussed in this section are summarized in Figure 12 . While many therapies are designed for FGR and preeclampsia, they have relevance for high-altitude populations where chronic environmental hypoxia is the primary driver of vascular dysfunction. Accordingly, this section integrates a discussion of hypoxic-specific interventions alongside treatments for FGR and pre-eclampsia, highlighting shared mechanistic targets and unique therapeutic requirements for high-altitude gestation. 9.1. DIETARY INTERVENTIONS Nutritional interventions, including vitamin C, allopurinol, spermidine, PUFA, and polyphenols, have shown therapeutic promise by directly or indirectly targeting the key pathways affected by chronic hypoxia. In this regard, nutritional interventions have shown pleiotropic and complementary effects on maternal, placental, and fetal cardiovascular systems, with molecular pathways and experimental outcomes varying across models. 9.1.1. Vitamin C Antenatal vitamin C, also known as ascorbic acid or ascorbate, has been extensively studied across diverse animal models to evaluate its efficacy in mitigating the effects of maternal and fetal hypoxia. Ascorbate supplementation in rats exposed to maternal hypoxia (13–15% O 2 , gestational days 6–20), reduces placental oxidative stress markers (e.g., 4-hydroxynonenal, heat-shock protein 70), along with improving fetal birth weight ( 377 , 580 , 628 ). In chronically hypoxic sheep, third-trimester maternal intravenous vitamin C (200 mg*kg −1 *d −1 ) restores fetal growth and prevents systemic hypertension during adulthood ( 184 ). Maternal ascorbate administration in sheep increases plasma ascorbate levels and facilitates placental O 2 exchange without altering uterine blood flow ( 184 , 844 ). Vitamin C may also restore reduced levels of vitamin E and glutathione ( 184 ), thereby amplifying antioxidant defenses. Consistently, high vitamin C levels in the serum of pregnant women have been associated with a reduction in both placental oxidative stress and apoptosis ( 845 ). In addition to its antioxidant effects, vitamin C stabilizes HIF-1α ( 846 ), a marker of placental dysfunction ( 847 ), which upregulates VEGF to drive angiogenesis. In vitro studies using human term trophoblasts have demonstrated reduced apoptosis after vitamin C treatment ( 848 ), whereas another study, which exposed first-trimester trophoblasts to 2% O 2 for 24 h showed increased apoptosis and reduced markers of placental oxidative stress after treatment with vitamin C for 24 h ( 849 ), suggesting that oxygenation context and pregnancy progression can alter these cellular responses. In hypoxic rat placentas, ascorbate abolished lipid peroxidation ( 377 ), while in ovine twin pregnancies, it enhanced placental efficiency by increasing the transfer of antioxidants to the fetus ( 850 ). Additionally, vitamin C activates the nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2/antioxidant response elements (Nrf2/ARE) pathway, boosting antioxidant enzymes such as HO-1, while suppressing nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB)-mediated inflammation ( 851 ). The net effect of these mechanisms seems to be consistent with improved endothelial integrity and nutrient transport across the maternal, placental, and fetal compartments. Furthermore, vitamin C protects fetal cardiovascular development by reducing oxidative injury in cardiomyocytes and endothelial cells ( 580 ). In adult offspring from gestational hypoxic sheep and rats, maternal ascorbate supplementation prevented aortic remodeling and preserved NO-dependent vasodilation ( 184 , 852 ). Furthermore, hypoxia-induced brain sparing is partially reversed, with improved fetal arterial O 2 content ( 628 ). Vitamin C modulates maternal and fetal hypoxia in a species-specific manner. Sheep (epitheliochorial placenta) and rodents (hemochorial placenta) both show robust benefits of vitamin C on fetal growth under hypoxia. However, human trials, such as the Vitamins In Preeclampsia trial, reported no growth improvement and an increased incidence of low birth weight ( 853 ), likely due to late intervention, combination therapies (e.g., with vitamin E), or variable baseline nutrient status under conditions related to chronic fetal hypoxia ( 854 – 857 ). Conversely, vitamin C levels in early pregnancy are inversely related to birth weight in humans (e.g., 14.1 ± 2.0 μg/ml – 3.2 kg vs. 7.6 ± 1.1 μg/ml – 3.4 kg), emphasizing the importance of maternal micronutrient baseline status when considering supplementation (845). Moreover, studies in trophoblast cell lines show that high levels of vitamin C and E decrease cell viability ( 858 ), and induce apoptotic genes under in vitro hypoxic conditions ( 849 ). These differences may contribute to the lack of success in translating the benefits of vitamin C observed in preclinical models into human pregnancy. 9.1.2. Allopurinol and Spermidine Allopurinol, a xanthine oxidase (XO) inhibitor that prevents the conversion of hypoxanthine to xanthine and xanthine to uric acid, has been proposed to mitigate oxidative stress by reducing uric acid and scavenging ROS ( 859 ), preserving NO bioavailability, and enhancing uterine artery vasodilation ( 860 ). In rodent models of chronic hypoxia, maternal allopurinol crosses the placenta and inhibits XO activity in placental and fetal tissues ( 861 ). This effect counters the adverse effects of vascular aging and function, as evidenced by reduced telomere attrition in offspring and rescued endothelial function in aged progeny ( 862 ). Maternal allopurinol also prevents cardiac dysfunction in adult offspring programmed by chronic hypoxia during pregnancy ( 863 ). Allopurinol’s translational value is supported by clinical trials ( 864 ) that confirmed its efficient transfer to the fetus under reduced O 2 conditions ( 865 ) and its improvement in markers of brain injury ( 866 ). Spermidine is a polyamine with antioxidant properties stemming from metabolism of L-arginine via the arginase/ornithine decarboxylase pathways. In hypoxic pregnant rats, maternal spermidine supplementation restores fetal polyamine balance, normalizing heart weight, and reducing cardiomyocyte apoptosis and fibrosis ( 867 , 868 ). Spermidine activates autophagy via the AMPK/mTOR pathway, stabilizes placental HIF-1α to drive VEGF-mediated angiogenesis, and enhances mitochondrial membrane potential in cardiomyocytes ( 869 ). The effects of spermidine under gestational hypoxia have been mainly reported in rats, however, its role in promoting placental function and fetal growth under normoxic conditions is well supported in large mammalian models ( 870 – 872 ). Spermine, which is synthesized from spermidine and often shows stronger biological effects, has been reported to alter trophoblast gene expression through acetyl-CoA biosynthesis and histone acetylation ( 873 ). Thus, further studies are warranted to assess the efficacy of spermidine for improving fetal growth in human pregnancies under conditions of chronic hypoxia. 9.2. ANGIOGENIC AND VASOACTIVE FACTORS 9.2.1. Vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) gene therapy The VEGF signaling pathway has a crucial role during early development at the maternal, placental, and fetal levels ( 874 ). Gene therapy using the VEGFA isoform, VEGF-A 165 , in high-protein overnourished pregnant sheep improves the maternal uterine vascular function by enhancing endothelial-dependent relaxation and increasing uteroplacental circulation. These effects are mediated via upregulation of placental VEGF receptors and downstream effectors, such as angiopoietin and endothelial tyrosine kinase ( 875 ). Ovine studies showed that overexpression of VEGF-A 165 in the uterine artery significantly increases uterine artery blood flow by upregulating eNOS and inducing perivascular angiogenesis, thereby promoting vasodilation ( 876 ). Further, in growth‐restricted sheep, VEGF-A 165 overexpression in the uterine artery increases fetal growth velocity and normalized fetal weight ( 875 ). Similar results were reported in a guinea pig FGR model by mid-gestation application of VEGF-A 165 to the uterine circulation ( 877 ). However, inconsistent outcomes on fetal growth were also found in the guinea pig model, with potential sex-specific effects ( 878 , 879 ). Maternal systemic delivery of another VEGF isoform, VEGF-A 121 , has shown protective effects in RUPP rats, mainly via the assessment of maternal markers of vascular dysfunction, such as restored blood pressure, reduced free plasma sFlt-1 levels, and improved renal NO signaling ( 880 ). In that report, VEGF-A 121 was delivered using a biopolymer carrier that ensured VEGF distribution in the maternal compartment ( 880 ). In this regard, ex vivo perfusion of human placentas with a vector containing another isoform, Ad-VEGF-D, showed limited transfer and no adverse effects, suggesting future safety for placenta-specific delivery ( 881 ). Nonetheless, this intervention may potentially benefit the impaired pulmonary vascular development observed in chronic hypoxic fetuses (discussed in 6.2.2 ). Despite differences in target compartment, delivery route, and therapeutic gene, reports in the literature suggest a partial protective effect of VEGF in the fetus. Overall, these studies using various strategies to increase VEGF availability and/or function across various signaling cascades (IGF/VEGF), NO/vasodilatory pathways, and inflammatory regulators (NF-κB/IKK-β) provide compelling evidence for VEGF being able to correct the molecular deficits of PI ( 875 , 882 ). Consequently, the EVERREST trial ( NCT02097667 ) and newer studies may provide conclusive evidence for VEGF-based therapies against impaired fetal growth in pregnancies affected by hypoxia. In women, the EVERREST consortium aimed to deliver VEGF-A 165 via an adenoviral vector directly into the maternal uterine arteries of women with early-onset severe FGR ( 883 ). 9.2.2. Soluble fms-like tyrosine kinase-1 (sFlt-1) Recognition of sFlt-1 as a central mechanistic mediator of preeclampsia has opened new therapeutic avenues for clinical intervention. Several evidence show that women diagnosed with preeclampsia have increased serum levels of sFlt-1 ( 809 , 884 , 885 ), whereas pilot studies using extracorporeal apheresis have lowered the elevated sFlt-1 levels, reduced proteinuria, and stabilized blood pressure in very preterm preeclamptic women ( 886 , 887 ). Moreover, preclinical studies in preeclamptic placental explants show that inhibiting ferroptosis reduced both sFlt-1 release and lipid peroxidation ( 888 ), suggesting that targeting upstream pathways controlling sFlt-1 production may offer additional therapeutic benefits. 9.2.3. Cysteine-related molecules Several studies have used the synthetic derivative of L-cysteine, N-acetylcysteine (NAC), to enhance cysteine levels and glutathione synthesis ( 889 , 890 ), thereby improving maternal and fetal vascular function under hypoxic conditions by mitigating oxidative stress and enhancing endothelial function. In hypertensive pregnancy models in rats (i.e., RUPP), oral NAC supplementation (100 mg*kg −1 twice daily) alleviates maternal hypertension and restores uterine blood flow by restoring reduced glutathione levels and inhibiting redox-sensitive inflammatory pathways (e.g., NF-κB) ( 891 ). Similarly, in hypoxic guinea pig pregnancies, NAC reduced lipid peroxidation (e.g., lowering malondialdehyde levels) and DNA damage in fetal liver, heart, and lung ( 892 , 893 ). Gestational hypoxia-induced fetal myocardial 3-nitrotyrosine and MMP-9/collagen accumulations are inhibited by NAC, curtailing inducible NO synthase-derived peroxynitrite formation ( 894 ). The protective effects of NAC have been described in diverse FGR models and exhibit organ-specific variability, with pronounced benefits observed in the brain and heart. For instance, asymmetric dimethylarginine-induced FGR in mice is reversed by NAC, which reduces placental oxidative stress and increases Akt/mTOR pathway activation ( 895 ). In spontaneous FGR pigs, maternal NAC supplementation prevents placental inflammation and oxidative stress, reducing the rate of low birth weight ( 896 ) and conferring protective postnatal outcomes ( 889 , 897 ). Vascular effects of NAC in the umbilical and fetal circulation have been described in a model of progressive uterine artery constriction in guinea pigs. In this model, impaired placental function is characterized by increased umbilical artery pulsatility and resistance indices, reduced placental efficiency, and asymmetric fetal growth ( 898 ). This phenotype is also linked to changes in the placental LZ, including increased fibrosis, reduced vascularization, and decreased trophoblast volume, surface area, and diffusing capacity, along with higher levels of apoptosis markers in the interlobium zone ( 899 ). Maternal supplementation with NAC in this model restores fetal growth and enhances placental efficiency ( 578 ). Ex vivo vascular function assays show heterogeneous contractile changes in fetal aorta, umbilical, carotid and femoral arteries ( 570 – 572 ) but a generalized endothelial dysfunction that is rescued by NAC supplementation ( 578 ). Furthermore, the protective effects of NAC in this guinea pig model are associated with biomechanical and structural outcomes that persist during the postnatal period ( 572 , 758 ). Interestingly, the improvement in endothelial function occurs along with decreased responsiveness to NO. Fetal NAC administration to hypoxic chicken embryos restores endothelium-dependent relaxation via H 2 S-mediated vasodilation, even in NO-deficient states ( 583 ). In addition to the vascular pathways involved in the effects of cysteine-related mediators under maternal and fetal hypoxia, NAC benefits on endothelial function appear to occur at the epigenetic level. Fetal hypoxia and FGR induce hypomethylation of the promoter for eNOS ( NOS3/Nos3 ) in human umbilical vein endothelial cells ( 900 ), endothelial cells from fetal aorta, femoral, and umbilical arteries, and adult offspring aorta from guinea pigs ( 578 , 758 ), and endothelial cells from chicken embryos ( 583 ). NAC reverses these epigenetic alterations, restoring promoter methylation and transcriptional regulation ( 578 , 583 , 758 ). Cysteine-related mediators exert direct effects on placental and fetal cardiovascular function as a substrate for H 2 S synthesis ( 901 – 903 ). Endogenous H 2 S production is augmented in the uterine arteries of pregnant women compared to non-pregnant women and is associated with enhanced ex vivo relaxation ( 904 ). H 2 S donors directly relax vascular smooth muscle via BK Ca channel activation and potentiate NO signaling, further improving uterine ( 244 ) and placental ( 905 ) perfusion, although impaired H 2 S synthesis in human placental tissue downregulates PIGF release and increases sFlt-1 secretion ( 906 ). Reduced expression of enzymes related to H 2 S synthesis is observed in human placentas from FGR ( 907 , 908 ) and preeclamptic ( 906 ) cases. Similarly, in vivo inhibition of H 2 S synthesis in pregnant mice induces FGR, an effect prevented by H 2 S donors ( 906 ). The protective effects of H 2 S precursors and donors have been demonstrated in several preeclampsia-related rodent models, with positive effects on maternal vascular function, placental angiogenesis, and fetal growth ( 909 – 911 ). In addition to its role in preventing maternal and placental vascular dysfunction, H 2 S has cardioprotective effects during early development. In chicken embryos, administration of the H 2 S donor sodium hydrogen sulfide (NaHS) improved tolerance to ischemia and reperfusion, via opening of myocardial K ATP channels ( 903 ). Therefore, H 2 S may prove a useful therapeutic agent to protect the human fetal heart against ischemia-reperfusion injury, as may occur in complicated labor ( 903 ). 9.2.4. Phosphodiesterase-5 (PDE5) inhibitors The NO-cyclic guanosine monophosphate (cGMP) signaling axis is fundamental to maintaining vascular tone and regulating uteroplacental perfusion, processes often dysregulated in pregnancies complicated by fetal hypoxia and FGR. Selective PDE5 inhibitors, such as sildenafil and tadalafil, attenuate the hydrolysis of cGMP to its inactive form (5’-GMP), which potentiates and prolongs NO signaling in the circulation. Clinical evidence supports sildenafil as an effective treatment for PH in adults ( 912 ) and prospective trials in neonates suggest similar benefits, with the potential to restore oxygenation and reduce mortality in both preterm and term newborns with PH ( 913 ). In an experimental animal model of PPHN, by exposing pregnant dams to late gestation hypoxia, sildenafil demonstrated additional protective effects beyond vasodilation, including the suppression of hypoxia-induced pulmonary arterial smooth muscle cell proliferation and the reduction of RVH through inhibition of Notch3 signaling ( 914 ). Clinical observations in high-altitude neonates suggest that sildenafil provides measurable benefits by reducing the duration of O 2 dependence and improving systemic arterial SaO 2 ( 594 ). Furthermore, in lambs gestated at sea level normoxia and high-altitude hypoxia, sildenafil reduced mPAP under basal conditions and prevented mPAP increases during hypoxia, with highland newborn lambs showing larger vasodilator responses to sildenafil compared to their lowland counterparts ( 681 ). The latter was associated with an increased pulmonary PDE5 expression and an enhanced ex vivo vasodilation response in high-altitude lambs. In contrast to the beneficial effects on the pulmonary vasculature of sildenafil treatment after birth, the therapeutic effects of sildenafil during pregnancy and early fetal development have yielded mixed results. In pregnant sheep with single umbilical artery ligation, leading to fetal hypoxia, maternal sildenafil administration further impaired fetal growth, an effect associated with a blunted cardiovascular response to chronic hypoxia ( 915 ). Similarly, the large-scale, multicenter STRIDER consortium trials showed no benefit of sildenafil in terms of increased fetal growth or extended gestation ( 916 ), with the Dutch arm of the trial terminated prematurely following evidence of significantly increased neonatal PH in severe FGR human pregnancies treated with sildenafil, without reduction in the risk of perinatal mortality or morbidity ( 917 ). The chicken embryo model has been used to address any potential direct therapeutic effects of sildenafil on fetal cardiovascular function, independent of potential adverse effects of the drug on the maternal and/or placental compartments ( 537 ). In this study, sildenafil treatment during hypoxic incubation restored endothelial-dependent relaxation in peripheral resistance arteries in the chicken embryo by improving antioxidant defenses, thereby protecting against oxidative stress and normalizing NO levels. Although sildenafil restored vascular function, it did not protect against hypoxia-induced growth restriction in the chicken embryo ( 537 ), consistent with a lack of protection against FGR in human and ovine studies ( 915 , 916 ). Tadalafil is a PDE5 inhibitor with higher specificity than sildenafil and longer-lasting effects in increasing NO bioavailability ( 918 – 920 ). In pregnant mice with RUPP, tadalafil treatment mitigates hypertension in dams treated with the NO synthase inhibitor N(G)-nitro-L-arginine methyl ester (L-NAME) ( 921 , 922 ). Concurrently, the treatment provides end-organ protection, significantly reducing proteinuria and improving specific histological markers of renal injury. This included correcting glomerular endotheliosis in RUPP mice ( 922 ) and ameliorating glomerular oxidative stress in L-NAME-induced models ( 921 ). Importantly, tadalafil treatment in the L-NAME-induced mouse model of adverse pregnancy improved synaptogenesis and myelination in FGR offspring ( 923 ). Tadalafil also normalizes plasma sFlt-1 levels and increases PlGF levels in both RUPP and L-NAME-treated dams ( 921 , 922 ). Improved fetal and developmental outcomes are also observed following tadalafil treatment, as it prolongs the gestational period ( 921 ) and improves fetal survival and growth in RUPP and L-NAME pregnant mice. Growth improvement is associated with the restoration of key nutrient-sensing pathways. Specifically, tadalafil treatment restored the expression of placental phosphorylated mTOR signaling components, including 4E-BP1 and S6 ribosomal protein, to levels comparable to controls ( 924 ). Maternal infusion with tadalafil in pregnant sheep modestly improved fetal oxygenation with little to no effects on placental vascular function or transference to the fetal circulation ( 925 ). In late gestation human pregnancy, FGR pregnancies treated with tadalafil showed a reduction in markers of hypoxia (HIF-2α and mTOR) compared to non-treated patients ( 926 ). These effects are related to improved markers of placental function in vivo and fetal growth ( 927 ). In this context, current clinical trials are evaluating the potential of tadalafil as a therapeutic agent in human pregnancy complicated by early-onset FGR ( 928 – 930 ). As reviewed in ( 918 ), treatment with tadalafil shows efficacy in prolonging pregnancy in women with early-onset FGR, with lower fetal, neonatal, and infant mortality in the treatment group and no severe adverse maternal events. 9.3. METABOLIC AND HORMONE PATHWAYS 9.3.1. Mitoquinone mesylate (MitoQ) Mitochondrial activity in the placenta supplies ATP for trophoblast function and regulates redox signaling under hypoxia. Hypoxia alters oxidative phosphorylation, increases ROS, and stabilizes HIF pathways, shifting metabolism and promoting mitophagy or apoptosis ( 391 ). These changes impair trophoblast invasion, endothelial NO bioavailability, and angiogenic signaling, causing vasoconstriction, endothelial dysfunction, and reduced uteroplacental perfusion ( 185 , 931 ). MitoQ is an analogue of coenzyme Q10 that acts as a mitochondria-targeted antioxidant and has been proposed to counteract the effects of maternal hypoxia ( 843 ). In rodent hypoxic pregnancy models, maternal MitoQ administration during gestation normalizes uterine artery reactivity in hypoxic dams, an effect associated with an enhanced NO-mediated stimulation of BK Ca channels ( 274 ). At the placental level, nanoparticle encapsulated MitoQ (nMitoQ) prevents hypoxia-induced oxidative stress ( 932 ) and free MitoQ increased placental volume, fetal capillary surface area, and mitochondrial stress in hypoxic pregnancy in rats ( 275 ). Notably, some sex-specific effects of nMitoQ in protecting the placenta to maternal hypoxia have been found. For instance, nMitoQ does not rescue fetal growth or oxygenation in male rats but improves placental efficiency and antioxidant defenses in both male and female offspring born to hypoxic dams, and increases placental Vegfa and Igf2 levels only in placentas from female fetuses exposed to hypoxia ( 933 ). These sex-specific effects may result from a different response to chronic intrauterine hypoxia between male and female fetuses ( 934 ). The fetal safety of free MitoQ has been tested recently, demonstrating that maternal administration of MitoQ to pregnant sheep crosses the placenta, leading to therapeutic levels in the fetal circulation ( 185 ). Importantly, this treatment regimen preserved fetal brain-sparing responses to acute hypoxia in fetal sheep and protected against FGR in hypoxic ovine pregnancy ( 185 ). Further, treatment of MitoQ restores endothelial function and protects cardiac dysfunction in hypoxic chicken embryos, highlighting direct protective effects of MitoQ on embryonic development independent of effects on the mother and/or placenta ( 185 ). Maternal treatment with MitoQ in mammalian pregnancy has also been reported to have longer-term effects in the cardiovascular system, lung, and liver of the adult offspring ( 185 , 843 , 935 – 939 ). For instance, maternal treatment with MitoQ prevented hypertension in the adult offspring programmed developmentally by hypoxic pregnancy in sheep ( 185 ). Further, maternal MitoQ in hypoxic pregnancy in rats protected adult offspring against increased α 1 -adrenergic reactivity of the cardiovascular system, enhanced reactive hyperemia in peripheral vascular beds and sympathetic dominance, hypercontractility, and diastolic dysfunction in the heart ( 939 ). Importantly, the cardiovascular dysfunction in adult rat offspring programmed by hypoxic pregnancy could be diagnosed in vivo by analysis of blood pressure and heart rate variability, suggesting that these noninvasive biomarkers could be translated to the human clinical setting. Combined, the literature supports the potential application of mitochondria-targeted antioxidants to prevent maternal, placental, fetal and adult offspring physiological dysfunction in pregnancy complicated by chronic hypoxia. 9.3.2. Melatonin Melatonin, a hormone primarily involved in regulating the circadian rhythm, demonstrates multifaceted cardiovascular benefits in models of maternal hypoxia and FGR. Compelling evidence shows that maternal melatonin administration leads to increased circulating levels of melatonin in the fetus ( 940 – 943 ). In sheep, treatment with melatonin increased uterine artery blood flow ( 944 ) and umbilical artery blood flow ( 844 ). In hypertensive pregnant mice, the vasodilator effects of melatonin are NO-dependent ( 844 ) and enhanced by regulating BK Ca channels ( 945 ). Within the placenta, melatonin mitigates oxidative damage by scavenging ROS and activating the Nrf2/ARE pathway, boosting antioxidant enzymes like superoxide dismutase and glutathione peroxidase ( 946 , 947 ). Melatonin improved placental efficiency and birth weight and increased the placental expression of antioxidant enzymes in rodent undernourished pregnancy ( 948 ). Furthermore, melatonin negatively regulates anti-angiogenic factor in the placenta ( 947 ), and its placental receptor is essential for adequate fetal growth and VEGF-dependent angiogenesis ( 949 ). Translational use of melatonin is partially supported by phase I clinical trials aimed at improving outcomes in pregnancies affected by FGR ( 950 , 951 ) and preeclampsia ( 952 ). As in preclinical models, benefits in maternal vascular function and prolonged gestation are observed ( 953 ). In high-altitude hypoxic sheep, maternal melatonin during gestation did not improve fetal growth ( 954 ) but enhanced postnatal endothelial- and NO-dependent relaxation in middle cerebral arteries ( 955 ) and boosted antioxidant/pro-oxidant ratio in pulmonary circulation ( 954 ). This was associated with an increase in the plasma antioxidant capacity and a decreased ROS generation at the main pulmonary pro-oxidant sources ( 954 ). Similarly, in hypoxic pregnant rats, melatonin did not prevent FGR nor impair placental efficiency but protected the offspring from aortic wall remodeling ( 940 ) and cardiac dysfunction ( 529 ). Impaired maternal melatonin secretion impaired the fetal heart antioxidant defenses in RUPP mice, a phenotype rescued by melatonin administered subcutaneously ( 956 ). Direct fetal effects of melatonin have been also tested in hypoxic chicken embryos, showing protective cardiac and vascular effects, along with decreased markers of oxidative stress ( 530 ). Longer-term protective effects of maternal melatonin treatment in mammalian adverse pregnancy have also been reported. For instance, in rats, maternal treatment with melatonin rescued vasoconstrictor hyper-reactivity of peripheral arteries ( 940 ), maladaptive cardiomyocyte Ca 2+ handling ( 529 ), and cardiac mitochondrial dysfunction ( 532 ) programmed developmentally by hypoxic pregnancy. 9.3.3. Insulin-like growth factor (IGF) Similar to VEGF, IGF-1 plays a key role in placental and fetal development, with compelling evidence that IGF signaling is central to many conditions that impair fetal growth. IGF-1 binds placental IGF-1 receptors, activating PI3K/Akt and mTOR pathways that stimulate cell proliferation and nutrient transporter expression ( 957 , 958 ). FGR in rat dams exposed to 13% O 2 is characterized by increased expression of IGFBP-1 and -2 in the fetal liver ( 959 ). In human liver cell cultures, hypoxia evokes phosphorylation of IGFBP-1 via inhibition of mTOR, IGFBP-1 can bind to bioavailable IGF-1 and thus decrease IGF-1 signaling ( 958 ). Similar changes in IGF signaling mediators in the fetus have been found in sheep models of impaired uterine perfusion with preserved maternal oxygenation ( 960 ). In human FGR cases, altered levels of IGF signaling mediators also involve placental-specific changes occurring at the maternal interface, characterized by increased levels in total and phosphorylated IGFBP-1 ( 961 ). Increased circulating levels of IGFBP-3 and reduced levels of IGF-1 have also been reported in maternal and cord blood of pregnancies affected by preeclampsia ( 962 ). The regulation of placental IGF signaling by hypoxia is partially independent of changes in maternal food intake and inversely related to the level and chronicity of hypoxia ( 64 , 963 ). For instance, pregnant mice exposed to 13% O 2 show reduced maternal food intake along with a subtle reduction in fetal weight, but no changes in placental nutrient transport. However, exposure to 10% O 2 further impaired fetal weight, placental nutrient transport capacity, and IGF signaling pathways ( 64 ). There appears to be some sex-specific regulation of IGF signaling in the placenta and fetuses, with basal and hypoxia-triggered differences in IGF-2 suggesting a greater resilience in females to impaired intrauterine oxygenation ( 63 , 964 ). Intra-placental delivery of an adenoviral vector encoding human IGF-1 (Ad-hIGF-1) has been tested in multiple FGR models. In a mouse FGR model (uterine artery branch ligation), injection of intra-placental Ad-hIGF-1 restores fetal birth weights of growth‐restricted pups to control levels ( 965 ). In a naturally occurring rabbit runt model (reduced blood flow to the third implant), a single intra-placental Ad-hIGF1 injection fully normalizes fetal birth weight, as well as fetal liver and muscle masses, without changing placental weight ( 966 ). The vector is largely confined to the placenta, with minimal off‐target gene transfer detected in maternal or fetal tissues. In addition to viral vectors, placenta‐targeted nanoparticle delivery of IGF-1 has shown efficacy. For example, a recent study used a polymer-based nanoparticle to deliver a human IGF-1 transgene under a trophoblast-specific promoter to the guinea pig placenta ( 967 ). Repeated ultrasound-guided injections of the IGF-1‐nanoparticle into the placenta resulted in transgene uptake, corrected fetal weight in nutrient-restricted pregnancies, increased fetal blood glucose, and reduced fetal cortisol, without adverse maternal effects ( 968 ). 9.3.4. AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) activators Another regulator of metabolism with therapeutic potential is the AMPK pathway. Studies in mouse uterine arteries ( 65 ) and human myometrial arteries ( 56 ) show that hypoxia exposure or high-altitude residence during pregnancy enhances the vasodilator responses induced by pharmacological activation of AMPK. In contrast, myometrial arteries from human FGR cases at high altitude show reduced responses to AMPK activation ( 56 ). Furthermore, in vivo subcutaneous administration of the AMPK agonist AICAR partially prevents the hypoxia-evoked FGR in mice ( 969 ). In human trials, metformin, another indirect activator of AMPK, has been tested with mixed results. Metformin is used to treat type 2 and gestational diabetes and has been shown in meta-analyses to reduce or have no effect on birth weight compared with insulin treatment ( 970 ). A double-blind, placebo-controlled randomized clinical trial showed that extended-release metformin prolongs gestation by 7.6 days in preterm preeclamptic pregnancies ( 971 ). However, other trials have shown that metformin use during pregnancy can increase the risk of obesity in children ( 972 , 973 ). While such inconsistent results inspire caution, it is worth noting that all women in these trials involving metformin had underlying risk factors for FGR. In fact, no study on the effects of metformin treatment during pregnancy has had severe preeclampsia or FGR as its primary outcome ( 974 ). 9.4. Potential exercise-based therapies In normal pregnancies, regular moderate‑intensity exercise promotes advantageous morphological changes in the placenta ( 975 , 976 ). Specifically, chronic exercise during pregnancy increases the expression of placental FATP4, a key fatty acid transporter, suggesting improved fatty acid metabolism and availability from mother to fetus ( 977 ). Moreover, mild‑ and moderate‑intensity exercise may improve angiogenic placental markers ( 978 ), with protective antioxidant effects ( 978 , 979 ). These findings indicate that the improved aerobic conditioning of physically active mothers improves placental function ( 980 ) without compromising neonatal outcomes ( 981 ). Indeed, a case study in pregnant Sherpa woman performing mild to intense exercise at high altitude suggest no negative effects on perinatal outcomes ( 982 ). Further proof of concept in rats with placental insufficiency induced by RUPP suggests that exercise during pregnancy may counteract the impaired placental perfusion resulting from hypertensive maternal states ( 983 ). Consequently, the evidence supports routine moderate exercise as standard of care for healthy pregnant women. Despite the benefits observed in normal pregnancies, the role of exercise in pregnancies complicated by FGR remains inconclusive, with evidence showing both reassuring and potentially unfavorable signals. In women with FGR fetuses, a single bout of submaximal maternal exercise led to fetal bradycardia and a 12% rise in umbilical artery pulsatility index during recovery, with smokers, sedentary patients, and male fetuses exhibiting significantly higher pulsatility index values before and after exercise, indicating increased placental vascular resistance ( 984 , 985 ). In contrast, mild intensity graded treadmill walking (limited to maternal heart rate ≤150 bpm) does not significantly alter the umbilical artery Doppler parameters in either normal or FGR fetuses ( 986 ). More comprehensive studies with Doppler studies applying submaximal exercise shows that uterine and umbilical perfusion remain unchanged in both normal and FGR pregnancies during training, but persistent negative parameters in the post exercise period in FGR ( 987 , 988 ). Similarly, the evidence regarding exercise in pregnancies complicated by preeclampsia is unfavorable, with scarce support that warrant caution. In late pregnancy, standardized exercise testing in hypertensive women revealed that placental blood flow was consistently lower in all pathological groups compared to normal controls ( 989 ). Similarly, in preeclamptic pregnancies, mild intensity exercise induces a marginal improvement in placental blood flow, along with a considerable fetal bradycardia ( 990 ). Therefore, until high‑quality prospective trials define safe intensity, timing, and duration threshold, exercise in hypoxic pregnancies should be approached with caution, remaining as an open field for research. 9.5. Considerations and translational challenges While the therapeutic targets discussed above show significant promise, the transition from preclinical models to clinical practice is often hindered by off-target effects and unforeseen fetal safety concerns. A notable example is the failure of the STRIDER trials, where sildenafil, despite showing robust benefits in animal models, was associated with increased neonatal pulmonary hypertension in humans, highlighting the translational gap between species-specific placental architectures ( 797 ). Similarly, the antioxidant paradox seen with Vitamin C supplementation suggests that high doses may inadvertently disrupt essential redox signaling required for normal development ( 733 ). Comparable undesired effects have been observed in L-arginine supplementation protocols ( 991 ). To mitigate these risks, current research is pivoting toward targeted delivery systems, such as nanoparticle encapsulation and trophoblast-specific promoters, which sequester therapeutic agents within the placental architecture to minimize maternal systemic exposure and direct fetal toxicity ( 812 , 848 ). Furthermore, to enhance the transference from bench to clinical application, increased integration of computational biology and advanced in vitro platforms is required. In silico modeling of pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics allows researchers to simulate drug distribution ( 992 , 993 ). A crucial issue, considering the additional complexity that the maternal-placental-fetal interaction imposes, in which the prediction of undesirable effects becomes more challenging ( 994 ). By combining these sophisticated tools with conserved pathways identified in high-altitude cohorts and models, researchers may refine therapeutic windows and improve the safety profile of interventions for chronic hypoxia-driven dysfunction. To date, there are no specific interventions to counteract the effects of altitude during pregnancy. Current pharmacological approaches, such as low-dose aspirin to reduce the risk of preeclampsia ( 995 ), antihypertensive therapy to control maternal blood pressure ( 996 , 997 ), and delivery when the maternal or fetal condition warrants it ( 998 ), are not altitude-specific and are based on general obstetric guidelines. More broadly, as medical guidelines indicate, descending to lower altitudes or using supplemental O 2 offers the strongest and most compelling evidence for counteracting high-altitude sickness ( 999 ); however, whether this is effective during pregnancy remains to be determined. 10. CONCLUSIONS The PO 2 in air decreases with increasing altitude, and prolonged exposure can lead to chronic hypoxia. During pregnancy, chronic hypoxia induces several physiological adaptations across the maternal, placental, and fetal compartments. These adaptations converge at a common endpoint, ensuring the delivery of appropriate O 2 and nutrients to the developing fetus. Failure of these adaptive mechanisms increases the risk of pregnancy complications and long-term health issues for both mother and offspring. For the mother , the more pronounced effects of chronic hypoxia during pregnancy are on the respiratory and cardiovascular systems. Of particular significance are the effects on uterine circulation, including reduced pregnancy-dependent uterine artery widening and remodeling, as well as impaired vasodilator reactivity, resulting in a diminished increase in uterine artery diameter and blood flow with advancing hypoxic pregnancy. Consequently, the diffusion of O 2 and nutrients from the mother to the fetus at the maternal-fetal interface is reduced. Within the placenta , most anatomical adaptations to chronic gestational hypoxia aim to increase the contact surface area between the maternal and fetal circulations, thereby optimizing O 2 diffusion and nutrient transfer to the fetus. Metabolically, the placenta decreases its O 2 consumption, shifting toward anaerobic metabolism as an additional strategy to spare adequate O 2 delivery to the fetus. For the offspring , FGR is the most common adverse consequence, but exposure to chronic hypoxia during gestation also increases the risk of CHD and susceptibility to cardiovascular, metabolic, and neurological disorders in later life. We now know that multigenerational high-altitude residence protects against the detrimental effects of chronic hypoxia on pregnancy, as humans and other mammals with a prolonged highland residence ancestry show a reduced risk of pregnancy complications at high altitude. Although high-altitude pregnancy research has been conducted for nearly 70 years, significant gaps remain in understanding how chronic gestational hypoxia impacts all three maternal, placental, and fetal compartments. For instance, although some genes of interest have been discussed in this review, the specific variants that confer protection to populations adapted to high altitude remain unidentified, and the relationship between these variants and improved fetal growth preservation has not yet been established. Populations of distinct highland origin may employ different adaptive strategies, and the convergence of these pathways also requires further investigation. While some studies in animal models of chronic hypoxic development demonstrate the influence of epigenetics, the role of these mechanisms in the maternal, placental, and fetal adaptation to hypoxia remains unclear. Future research should adopt an integrative, cross-disciplinary approach that addresses all maternal-placental-fetal components simultaneously, ideally through continuous long-term studies that track physiological changes throughout pregnancy. For instance, longitudinal studies during high-altitude pregnancy can clarify the progression of maternal cardiovascular adaptations, placental vascular remodeling, and fetal growth patterns, which cross-sectional studies may not fully capture. Additionally, combining detailed in vivo measurements of uterine artery and fetal vessel Doppler flowmetry, with ex vivo uteroplacental and fetal vascular physiology, placental morphometry, and single-cell multi-omics approaches could help define protective versus harmful pathways, thereby identifying early diagnostic biomarkers and/or targeted treatments. Mechanistic studies have identified several potential therapies that could successfully mitigate detrimental effects of chronic hypoxic pregnancy. However, due to interlinked pathways regulating complex traits, not all adverse consequences have been reversed by any one treatment. Accordingly, combined interventions may broaden the efficacy of therapeutic agents to further reduce the harmful effects of chronic hypoxia and enhance protective mechanisms for both mother and offspring. Convergent adaptive strategies in human highland populations emphasize conserved mechanisms, such as improved uteroplacental O 2 delivery, that warrant further investigation, particularly in understudied groups, including those residing in the East African Highlands. Incorporating cutting-edge technologies, such as human trophoblast organoids and organ-on-a-chip placental-vascular systems, will further help elucidate specific mechanisms affected by chronic hypoxia, including HIF pathways, angiogenic balance, and metabolic reprogramming. Finally, CRISPR-based gene editing of EPAS1 , PRKAA1 , and other genes will enhance our understanding of these adaptive processes. The study of chronic hypoxia or high-altitude exposure during pregnancy is significant for improving reproductive health among the nearly 80 million individuals residing in highland regions, many of which are remote and resource-limited. Most of the studies discussed are founded on the basis of physiological ranges from low-altitude populations or normoxic control groups, which is scientifically correct. However, developing specific population-based clinical parameters for high-altitude inhabitants is an urgent necessity, as using sea-level standards to diagnose and treat these populations may leads to misinterpretations, systematic medical errors and misallocation of resources. By establishing “altitude-normal” physiological ranges, we can better understand the physiological responses, enable more precise clinical diagnoses, and the formulation of public health policies that truly reflect the unique biological reality of millions living in hypoxic environments. The comprehensive study of the physiological pathways and potential interventions discussed in this review may also facilitate a more effective management of sea level pregnancies complicated by hypertensive disorders of pregnancy, chronic fetal hypoxia and FGR, which remain a major global public health challenge. CLINICAL HIGHLIGHTS Over 500 million people worldwide live in high-altitude regions (elevations of 1,500 m or higher), with 80 million above 2,500 m, where a more significant environmental exposure to chronic hypoxia is observed. Living at high altitude raises the risk of pregnancy complications, including a three- to four-fold increase in the incidence of preeclampsia and a two- to three-fold increase in the incidence of fetal growth restriction, compared to low-altitude residents. Adaptations to high altitude, as observed in multigenerational highland populations (e.g., Andean or Tibetan) and high-altitude-adapted animal models, offer valuable insights into molecular, cellular, and physiological processes such as uteroplacental vascular regulation, placental function and fetal adaptations that may inform the development of future therapies for pregnancy complications and their long-term consequences. High altitude provides a natural laboratory for studying the effects of chronic hypoxia on pregnancy, the development of pregnancy complications, and long-term effects on offspring. However, crucial gaps remain in our understanding of the processes triggered by high-altitude exposure. Specifically, investigating high-altitude populations and animal models exposed to chronic hypoxia during pregnancy can help identify potential treatment strategies, which are currently lacking, and these strategies can be applied to pregnancy complications at any altitude.
