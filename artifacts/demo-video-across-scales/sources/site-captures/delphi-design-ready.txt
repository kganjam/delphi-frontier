The relation in the numbers
V = √(2 m g / ρ S C_L)
This revision, ballasted: 26.1 g, S = 67.8 cm² → 11.1 m/s

Steady level lift, lift equal to weight. ρ = 1.225 kg/m³, C_L = 0.5, g = 9.81 m/s². The same balance is V = √(2 WL / ρ C_L), with wing loading WL = m g / S. Taxi, launch and ground effect sit outside it.

Where the shape comes from

This outline follows Daane et al. 2021 Data S1A: pectoral length 107.5 mm on candidate fish3d-v3c-daane-length, with area scaled by the square of that length, and the pelvic fins inserted at 0.48 standard length.

Fish 1990 measured wing loading from 11.7 to 208.9 N/m² on specimens of 0.003–0.53 kg, and relays that juveniles under 5 cm do not glide. Davenport 1992 reports swimming impaired beyond about 78–79% of standard length. A centre of gravity behind the pectoral fins is only a qualitative flag: no numeric centre of gravity was retrieved for a living flying fish. Park & Choi 2010 discuss lift between the pectoral and pelvic fins, but camber was not taken from that paper, so these meshes are flat plates.

Those ranges are Frontier envelopes C1, C3 and C6c. They are measured bounds on preserved or reported fish, not a Lean law, and they were not remeasured on this mesh.

Laws this design uses
Thompson 1917
Speed varies as the square root of linear size in swimming, flight and walking
v ∝ √L · Lean law

Leaving aside differences in mechanical construction, swimming (Froude's law), flight (minimum stable speed) and walking (the leg swinging as a pendulum) all give the same result: an animal's velocity tends to vary as the square root of its linear dimensions.

The hand trim speed is the steady-lift case of this law. At a fixed lift coefficient, speed goes with the square root of wing loading, and wing loading grows with size.

Thompson 1917
Power needed for flight grows as length^3.5 while muscle power grows as length^3
W ∝ L³·⁵ · Lean law

A bird of fixed proportions must fly at a minimum speed proportional to the square root of its linear size, so the work needed for flight varies as the 3½ power of linear size while the work its muscles can supply varies only as the cube; flight thus becomes harder as birds get larger (about 1.4 times harder per doubling of length).

That same square-root speed is why the power to stay aloft grows faster than muscle mass. This glider uses the speed relation. It does not test the statement about birds.

Thompson 1917
Structures that must resist bending are built as hollow tubes
Hollow tube · Source claim

Where stiffness against bending in all directions is required, organisms use a hollow cylindrical construction with little or no material in the core, as in the thin-walled, almost empty wing bones of long-winged birds such as the albatross, quills, and hollow stems of reeds and straw.

The body is a thin hollow shell, in the spirit of a tube that resists bending. The wall thickness is a print choice, not a measured fin ray or a bird bone.

Mass check
Fails the hand limit

The hand requirement is V_trim ≤ 8 m/s at C_L 0.5. On 52.9 cm² that is 10.5 g. On this area the same formula allows 13.6 g. Ballasted mass is 26.1 g.

r4 is a fit-and-balance prototype. Its right-hand print files are proper mirrors. It has not been printed or flown.
