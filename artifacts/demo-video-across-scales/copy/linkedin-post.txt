What would it take to turn “make a zebrafish fly” into a design we could actually test?

I spent Halloween weekend exploring that question at the ScienceClaw Hackathon at MIT Media Lab with Joel Dietz, Kavita Kulkarni, Ashley Caselli and Amit S. Together, we built Delphi Bio.

The bigger idea is a biological compiler: describe the capability you want, design across scales, and work toward candidate DNA with a plan to build and test it.

MorphoSwarm Studio explores adversarial design with a decentralized swarm of AI agents: proposing designs, choosing simulations and experiments, and challenging each other's assumptions. Its recorded studies span molecular motion, cell signaling, fin mechanics, fluid flow and collective behavior. We even 3D-printed fish and cell-assembly models for the judges. Biological validation is still ahead.

What excites me most is connecting the scales. Can energy supply keep up with flight? Could proteins make fins tougher without adding weight? How would a school coordinate takeoff—and what would change for its ecosystem?

Answering those questions means developing and running experiments across scales, especially where the scales meet.

That’s what building toward verified biology means to me: extract mathematical relationships from literature and data, check the deductions, and make assumptions visible. Compose compatible models and explore their control knobs. Then test whether biology agrees.

Delphi is also building the knowledge infrastructure for this: claims that travel with their evidence, uncertainty and disagreements. New findings should update the record and flag designs that depend on it. Every simulation and experiment can give the next project something to build on.

I see exciting connections to AI BioDesign, CellScapes and the SeaHub team at the Allen Institute. Their work is a continuing source of inspiration, alongside Michael Levin, D’Arcy Thompson and the communities making science more open and reusable.

The best part of the weekend was the team. Joel, Kavita, Ashley and Amit: thank you for the ideas, the long hours and the willingness to challenge each other’s assumptions. I learned a lot building with you.

We came away with 2nd place overall and first-place awards from Nebius and alphaXiv. Thank you to Markus J. Buehler, the LAMM team and the organizers, and to Nebius, alphaXiv, NVIDIA, Google, Formlabs and E14 Fund for supporting the weekend.

The flying fish is one design brief. The pieces of a biological compiler are coming together—for enzymes, biobots, regenerating tissues and designs we haven't imagined yet.
